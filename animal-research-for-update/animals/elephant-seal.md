# Elephant Seal (*Mirounga leonina*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Elephant Seal (southern elephant seal)
- **Scientific name:** *Mirounga leonina* (Linnaeus, 1758), family Phocidae (true seals)
- **Exact animal represented:** the southern elephant seal of the Southern Ocean and sub-Antarctic islands. The smaller northern elephant seal (*Mirounga angustirostris*) of the eastern Pacific is a separate species and is used here only where noted.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male (bull), **3,000.0 kg** and **460.0 cm total length**, representing a harem-holding bull rather than a record giant.
- **Sex choice:** sexual size dimorphism is extreme. Species accounts give adult bulls about 2,200–4,000 kg, while females typically weigh about 400–1,000 kg; an average mature bull can be more than twice a female's length and up to ten times her weight. Bulls are the fighting sex, battling for access to harems.
- **Population variation:** body size differs between breeding islands and with age; only older, larger bulls usually hold harems. Bulls above 4,000 kg and 6 m are exceptional and not canonical.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Animal Diversity Web: adult males **2,200–4,000 kg**; females about 500–1,000 kg in the same summary.
- Wikipedia species summary: adult bulls **3,000–3,700 kg**; other summaries give a mature bull average near 3,175 kg.
- Bell et al. (1997) developed photogrammetry and morphometric methods for estimating the mass of bulls that cannot be weighed directly, which is why many bull masses are estimates.
- **Canonical:** **3,000.0 kg**, the low end of the adult-bull range from Wikipedia and mid-range in ADW's figures.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Length
- ADW: adult males average about **4.2 m**, maximum about 6.2 m.
- Wikipedia: bulls **4.6–5.8 m** including the hind flippers.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 460.0 cm** total length, consistent with a 3,000 kg bull and between the two conventions.
- **Confidence:** Moderate; sources differ on whether hind flippers are included.

### Height
No standard standing height applies to a prone phocid, although bulls rear up high when fighting. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- On land elephant seals move at about **5 km/h (1.4 m/s)** by humping on their bellies (tertiary summary).
- No measured maximum swimming speed was found for southern elephant seals; a study of a female northern elephant seal measured cruising swim speed, which is not a maximum.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (unresolved). The land figure is a slow crawl, not a top speed, and is not used.

### Lifespan
- Species summaries: up to about **23 years**; an average wild lifespan of about **21 years** is quoted, with females living longer than males.
- A study of age-dependent survival found that extreme polygyny leads to different survival patterns in males and females, with males facing higher costs.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 21.0**, low confidence, because it is a species-level summary figure and bulls usually die younger.

### Bite and dentition
- Bulls fight with large canine teeth, aiming bites at a rival's neck.
- No bite-force measurement was found. **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.**

### Defensive structure
Protection comes from enormous mass and a thick blubber layer. Rival bulls aim their bites at the neck, where fights leave scars. There is no armor.

### Diving physiology
- Routine dives last about **20–30 minutes** to average depths over 400 m, with surface intervals averaging about 2 minutes; animals spend about **86–92%** of their time underwater during months-long foraging trips.
- The deepest recorded elephant seal dive is **2,388 m** (CSIRO).
- Breath-holds of up to about two hours are reported in summaries; field records of maximum dive duration from tagging studies are shorter, around 80 minutes or more.
- Elephant seals carry about twice the blood volume of a land mammal of the same size, and their blood has about 50% more haemoglobin.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The elephant seal's fighting power comes from sheer mass and its canine teeth. Rival bulls rear up on their fore-flippers to tower over each other, then lunge and slam, biting at the neck and leaning their weight against the opponent. Most contests end within a few minutes when one bull retreats. Outside the breeding beach the elephant seal is not a predator of large animals; it eats squid and fish. Its offense is therefore a crushing, biting shove from a 3-tonne body, dangerous at close range but slow and short-reaching.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
On land it moves by humping along on its belly at about walking pace. In water it is a streamlined, efficient diver rather than an agile sprinter. Maneuverability is low on land and moderate at sea.

### Endurance
This is the elephant seal's standout quality. During months-long foraging trips it dives almost continuously, spending 86–92% of its time underwater, with routine 20–30 minute dives to hundreds of metres and surface breaks of about two minutes. It has made the deepest dives recorded for any seal, down to 2,388 m. In the northern elephant seal, a separate *Mirounga* species, researchers found that seals sleep only about two hours a day at sea, drifting down in a "sleep spiral" during dives.

### Senses
Large eyes, typical of deep-diving seals, and whiskers that detect water movement, as in other true seals, support hunting at depth. Species-specific sensory measurements were not found.

### Intelligence and tactics
Bulls establish rank through fights and then maintain it largely through ritual displays: loud roars through the enlarged nose, rearing postures and chest slams on the ground. A study of the northern elephant seal found evidence that males learn to recognise rivals' calls and use them to assess opponents. Male southern elephant seals that return to the same foraging areas tend to live longer. This supports moderate tactical ability centred on ritual contests and navigation, not problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Colonial on land during breeding and moulting; solitary at sea. Harem bulls were older and larger than non-harem bulls in field studies, with access to females determined by dominance rank. Harems can reach several hundred females, though a South Shetland study found weaker polygyny than the textbook picture.

### Fighting and aggression
Bull fights are intense but usually brief, and most dominance is settled by display without full fights. Breeding bulls are dangerous to anything in their way on a crowded beach, but elephant seals do not attack other large animals at sea.

### Environmental strengths
- Deep, cold open ocean, where it can dive far beyond the reach of most opponents.
- Breeding beaches, where mass and rearing height dominate.
- Long engagements where huge energy stores and oxygen reserves outlast opponents.

### Weaknesses
- Very slow and clumsy on land.
- Limited offensive weapons beyond bite and mass.
- No armor; killer whales take weaned pups and occasionally larger seals.
- Not a fast, agile swimmer.
- Low aggression outside the breeding season.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** smaller animals on a beach that can be crushed or bitten; any opponent that must follow it into deep water.
- **Mixed:** large predators such as big sharks that attack in open water.
- **Bad:** killer whales, fast agile predators that avoid its front end, and any fight requiring pursuit.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Abyssal Breath-Hold** — routine dives last 20–30 minutes to 400–800 m, with a record of 2,388 m, backed by twice a land mammal's blood volume and extra haemoglobin. In a battle at sea it can simply go where an opponent cannot follow.
2. **Rear-and-Slam Duel** — bulls rear up on their fore-flippers, then crash down and lunge, biting at a rival's neck with large canines and bearing down with three tonnes of body.

### Unique traits
1. **Largest Carnivoran on Earth** — the southern elephant seal is the largest pinniped and the largest member of the order Carnivora, with bulls around 3,000–3,700 kg.
2. **Trunk-Nosed Bull** — adult males grow an inflatable proboscis used to produce extremely loud roars in the breeding season.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Southern elephant seals breed on sub-Antarctic islands such as South Georgia, Kerguelen and Marion, and on the Península Valdés coast of Argentina. At sea they range across the Southern Ocean to the Antarctic ice edge, foraging along boundaries between water masses.

### Diet and ecology
They eat mainly squid, including glacial squid and smooth hooked squid, and a range of fish, caught during deep dives. Killer whales prey on elephant seals, with high success against newly weaned pups and much lower success against older animals.

### Social structure
On land during breeding, dominant bulls hold harems of females, from a few dozen to several hundred at large colonies, while lower-ranking males wait at the edges. At sea elephant seals travel and feed alone.

### Reproduction and life history
Females give birth on the breeding beaches, where dominant bulls hold harems. Weaned pups later go to sea on their own, a vulnerable stage when killer whales take many of them. Harem bulls are older and larger than other males, so only a minority of males win breeding access. Females live longer than males on average.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List. The global population was estimated at about 650,000 in the mid-1990s and 664,000–740,000 in 2005, with recent summaries near 700,000, though trends differ between breeding populations. In 2023 highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 killed an estimated 17,000 southern elephant seals at Península Valdés, including at least 95–96% of pups in surveyed areas, with evidence of mammal-to-mammal spread. Population models predict a long recovery for that colony.

### Special features
- Largest body size of any seal and any carnivoran.
- Inflatable proboscis in adult males.
- Very large blood volume and high haemoglobin for oxygen storage.
- Thick blubber for insulation and energy.
- Large canines used in male contests.
- Large eyes suited to dim, deep water.

### Human interaction
Elephant seals were once hunted commercially for their blubber oil. Today they are major attractions for visitors to sub-Antarctic islands and Península Valdés, and they are widely studied with tags that record their dives and movements.

### Genuine fun facts
- The deepest dive recorded for an elephant seal is **2,388 m**.
- At sea elephant seals spend up to about **90%** of their time underwater, taking breaks of only about two minutes.
- A mature bull can weigh up to **ten times** as much as a female.
- Elephant seals have about twice the blood volume of a land mammal their size.
- Northern elephant seals, a separate species, were found to sleep only about two hours a day at sea, often while spiralling down during dives.
- The 2023 bird-flu outbreak at Península Valdés killed about **17,000** southern elephant seals.

### Concise site-ready summary
The southern elephant seal is the largest seal and the largest carnivoran on Earth, with bulls around 3 tonnes. Rival bulls rear up and slam into each other with bites to the neck, and at sea the species makes near-continuous dives to hundreds of metres, with a record of 2,388 m. It is enormous and very hard to wear down, but slow on land and armed with little beyond teeth and mass.

### Rich narrative profile
On a sub-Antarctic beach in the breeding season, a harem bull is an intimidating sight. Three tonnes of blubber and muscle rears up on its fore-flippers, the swollen trunk-like nose roaring, the chest crashing down on the sand. When displays fail, rival bulls tower over each other and lunge, biting at the neck and leaning with all their weight until one gives way. Most contests are over in minutes, and older, larger bulls usually win access to the females.

At sea the same animal becomes one of the most extreme divers on the planet. For months it barely stops diving, spending up to about 90% of its time underwater and surfacing for only a couple of minutes between dives that routinely reach hundreds of metres. It has been recorded at 2,388 m. Twice the blood volume of a land mammal, extra haemoglobin and oxygen-rich muscles make this possible. The ocean has few places an elephant seal cannot go, and few animals that can follow it there.

In ABS terms the elephant seal is a titan built for endurance rather than killing. Its Stamina and Special are near the top of the roster because of its diving physiology, and its Defense is high because of its sheer mass and blubber. Its Attack is moderate because its weapons are limited to canine bites and body slams, and its Agility is low. Killer whales can still take elephant seals, especially young ones, but a full-grown bull in deep water is one of the hardest animals in the ocean to catch or wear down.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** bull masses are often estimated rather than weighed; ranges span 2,200–4,000 kg. Canonical 3,000 kg is a representative harem-age bull.
- **Length:** sources mix totals with and without hind flippers.
- **Breath-hold:** two-hour figures come from popular summaries; tagging studies report shorter maximum dive durations.
- **Lifespan:** the 21-year average is a species summary; bulls usually die younger.
- **Northern species:** sleep and rival-learning findings come from the northern elephant seal and are used only as context.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Walrus:** no walrus research report exists yet, and the live walrus values are legacy data (for example Attack 1.7 and Agility 8.3), so they are not usable anchors. On evidence, a 1,200 kg walrus with long tusks should have a better weapon than the elephant seal, while the 3,000 kg elephant seal should be heavier, tougher and far stronger in Stamina.
- **Versus Sea Lion (300 kg; 55.0 / 49.0 / 84.0 / 84.0 / 78.0 / 72.0):** ten times the mass gives the elephant seal much higher Attack and Defense, and its diving gives higher Stamina and Special. The sea lion keeps far higher Agility and Intelligence.
- **Versus Leopard Seal (420 kg; 70.0 / 55.0 / 78.0 / 70.0 / 68.0 / 74.0):** the leopard seal keeps higher Attack as a true predator with a killing bite, while the elephant seal is far larger and tougher with much greater endurance.
- **Versus Hippopotamus (1,800 kg; Attack 90.0, Defense 84.0):** the hippo's tusks, aggression and thick hide keep it far above in Attack and a little above in Defense despite lower mass.
- **Versus Orca (5,000 kg; 94.0 / 78.0):** killer whales prey on elephant seals; the orca stays far above in Attack, Agility and Intelligence.
- **Versus Emperor Penguin and Leatherback Sea Turtle (Stamina 95.0 each):** comparable diving and travel specialists; the elephant seal's 90.0 Stamina sits just below them because its best evidence is diving rather than record long-distance travel.
- **Anti-inflation check:** size alone does not raise Attack; the elephant seal is not a large-animal predator. Diving is counted in Stamina and Special for distinct reasons: sustained output versus the ability to escape to depths opponents cannot reach.
