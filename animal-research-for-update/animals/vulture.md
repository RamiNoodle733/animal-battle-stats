# Vulture (*Gyps fulvus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Vulture (researched as the Eurasian griffon vulture, also called griffon vulture or Eurasian griffon)
- **Scientific name:** *Gyps fulvus* (Hablizl, 1783)
- **Exact animal represented:** the Eurasian griffon vulture, the large pale-buff Old World vulture of southern Europe, North Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia. The site's live photo is a griffon vulture taking off, so this report rates *G. fulvus*. The legacy catalogue entry lists *Gyps africanus* (white-backed vulture); that name does not match the photo and should be replaced by *Gyps fulvus* when this report is imported.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult of about **8.0 kg**, **105 cm** long, with a **2.6 m** wingspan.
- **Sex choice:** males and females overlap almost completely in size (males 6.2–10.5 kg, females 6.5–10.5 kg in the nominate race), and reintroduction studies found no sex difference in competitive behavior at carcasses. The specimen is therefore an adult of either sex; older adults, which are more dominant at carcasses, are the relevant fighting class.
- **Population variation:** the Indian subspecies *G. f. fulvescens* averages about 7.1 kg. Extreme adult weights of 4.5–15 kg are reported, the top figure probably from captivity. Spain holds more than 90% of the European population.

### Length and wingspan

### Speed
- Radio-tracked and observed griffons on Crete had a mean **inter-thermal gliding speed of 18.8 m/s** (about 68 km/h). Mean cross-country speed on windless days was 5.1 m/s, with a maximum of 13.3 m/s, because time spent climbing in thermals lowers the overall pace (Xirouchakis and Andreou 2009).
- A 12-species tracking comparison confirmed that heavier-wing-loaded soarers such as *Gyps* glide faster and turn on wider circles than lighter species.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 18.8 m/s**, the measured mean gliding speed. It is not a stoop or dive speed.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high (field measurement, one island population).

### Lifespan
- Maximum recorded lifespan **41.4 years** for a captive bird (Carey and Judge longevity records, cited by Wikipedia).
- No reliable average wild lifespan was located. Griffons are long-lived, slow-breeding birds laying one egg a year.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 41.4**, retained as a documented maximum (captive), in line with the condor report's treatment; it is not an average wild life.
- **Confidence:** Low as a typical value.

### Beak, feet and bite
- A strong hooked beak with cutting edges tears hide and flesh; the bare head and neck keep feathers clean inside a carcass (Life review, 2021).
- The feet are built for walking on the ground rather than seizing prey, unlike an eagle's talons.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bill-force measurement exists.

### Defensive structure
- None beyond feathers and a large body. The dense neck ruff and bare skin are for feeding hygiene and heat control, not protection. Posture changes raise bare-skin exposure from about 7% to 32%, more than doubling convective heat loss in still air.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The griffon is a scavenger, not a hunter. Its one real weapon is a heavy hooked beak made for ripping open carcasses, backed by strong neck muscles for pulling tissue. Its feet are suited to walking on the ground rather than catching prey, so it cannot grip and kill like an eagle. It "does not normally attack larger living prey", but Spanish griffons have been reported killing weak, young or sick animals when carrion was scarce. In Catalonia, 683 farmer complaints of "vulture attacks" were logged from 1996 to 2020, 80% during the birthing season and mostly involving cattle, which points to newborn or birthing livestock rather than healthy adults.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Griffons are specialist thermal soarers. High wing loading lets them glide fast between thermals but makes weak thermals hard to use and flapping expensive. Take-off needs prolonged flapping, during which heart rate triples to a mean of about 222 beats per minute (maximum 382). Once in a thermal, adults adjust fine-scale circling and climb better than juveniles under difficult wind shear. On the ground they hop and run clumsily around carcasses. Their agility is aerial and energetic, not a fast-turning pursuit style.

### Endurance
This is the species' strongest physical category. Soaring griffons spend only about 1.43 times their basal metabolic rate in flight, and heart rate falls back to near-resting values within 10 minutes of take-off. Seasonal home ranges in France averaged 473 km² in winter and 1,272 km² in spring; colony foraging ranges in Israel and France are about 10,000–12,000 km², with long-range movements beyond 300 km. Griffons regularly fast for several days between meals and cope with fasts of up to two weeks.

### Senses
Griffons locate carcasses by sight, and by watching other vultures descend. Their visual fields give wide coverage of the ground below and to the sides, a small binocular zone and large blind areas above, below and behind the head. In the head-down foraging posture they are often blind in the direction of travel, which is why they collide with wind turbines. There is no special smell sense.

### Intelligence and tactics
Social information is their main tactic: a vulture lowering its legs to land signals a carcass to others, and a group can find a carcass within 2–3 hours. Soaring skill improves with experience, since adults outperform juveniles in challenging thermal conditions. These are real cognitive strengths for foraging, but they give little tactical flexibility in a fight.

### Sociality and hunting
Colonial cliff nesters that forage and roost in groups and feed in crowds. About 30 griffons can strip a sheep carcass in half an hour. Density affects breeding success, with the best sheltered ledges and caves taken first.

### Fighting and aggression
Carcass feeding is a scrum of threat displays, wing-spreading, pecking and jostling. Older adults are more inclined to display aggression and dominance; there is no sex difference in competitive behavior. Griffons are also photographed fighting each other on the ground. The fights are about access to food and status, not killing.

### Environmental strengths
- Open mountains, gorges and steppe with strong thermals.
- Warm, sunny weather that makes long cheap soaring flights possible.
- Crowded carcass sites where size and numbers dominate smaller scavengers.

### Weaknesses
- Weak, walking feet; no killing grip.
- Expensive, slow take-off and poor performance in weak thermals, cloud or rain.
- Light, unarmored body on the full-roster scale.
- Blind spot in the direction of travel when foraging.
- Low willingness to fight healthy animals.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** tiny or weak animals, carcass disputes with smaller scavengers, contests decided by endurance or long-range searching.
- **Mixed:** similar-mass birds such as other large vultures, where size and dominance behavior decide access.
- **Bad:** eagles and other true raptors, any mammalian predator, armored or large animals, and anything that catches it on the ground before take-off.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Thermal Marathon** — soaring flight costs about 1.43 times resting metabolism and lets a griffon search foraging ranges of around 10,000 km² with almost no flapping; it wins long searching or waiting contests, not short fights.
2. **Carcass Crew Descent** — a vulture lowering its legs to land signals a find to others, and a crowd of about 30 can strip a sheep in half an hour; a group arrival quickly overwhelms smaller scavengers at food.

### Unique traits
1. **Acid Gut Shield** — a digestive tract with a pH of about 1–1.5 and a protective gut microbiota let it eat decaying carcasses that would sicken most animals.
2. **Bare-Head Thermostat** — the bare head and neck stay clean inside carcasses and act as radiators; posture changes raise bare-skin exposure from about 7% to 32%.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Resident in the Iberian Peninsula, the eastern Atlas Mountains, the eastern Alps and Balkans, Anatolia, the Caucasus, parts of Arabia and the Iranian plateau, and east to the Tian Shan and Altai Mountains. It breeds on crags and cliffs and forages over open landscapes of scrub, grassland and small woods where carcasses can be spotted. Spain held an estimated 30,946 breeding pairs in 2018; Crete has the largest island population.

### Diet and ecology
An obligate scavenger of large carcasses, historically wild ungulates and now largely livestock. Birds eat from the mouth and anus inward, and food at supplementary feeding stations is preferred when natural food is scarce or flight conditions are poor. Mean foraging radius on Crete was about 15 km, with livestock carrion up to 9 km from a colony used with little competition. Carcass removal is an important ecosystem service that reduces disease transmission.

### Social structure
Highly social. Birds nest in loose colonies, roost together and forage in mixed-age groups, following one another to food. At carcasses older adults dominate younger birds.

### Reproduction and life history
One egg per breeding attempt, laid on a cliff ledge or in a cave; sheltered sites produce more fledglings. Strong natal philopatry: many birds recruit as breeders in the colony, and sometimes on the same cliff, where they hatched. The captive longevity record is 41.4 years.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (BirdLife International 2021), with a growing Spanish population. Threats include illegal poison baits, veterinary drugs such as NSAIDs in carcasses, food shortages after EU carcass-removal rules brought in over BSE, collisions with wind turbines, and outbreaks such as the 2022 H5N1 avian influenza outbreak in Spain and France. Reintroductions and releases have restored or reinforced colonies in France, Bulgaria and Sardinia.

### Special features
- Wingspan 2.3–2.8 m and wing loading of about 9 kg/m².
- Soaring flight at about 1.43 times basal metabolic rate.
- Bare head and neck with a pale neck ruff.
- Strong hooked beak with cutting edges; ground-walking feet.
- Digestive tract pH about 1–1.5.
- Can fast for up to two weeks.

### Human interaction
Griffons clear livestock carcasses, and supplementary feeding stations are the main management tool supporting them today. In parts of Spain, farmer complaints of vulture attacks on newborn or weak livestock have grown since sanitary rules cut food supplies. Griffons are highly vulnerable to poison baits and wind turbines because they feed in groups and cannot see ahead while scanning the ground.

### Genuine fun facts
- A griffon in soaring flight uses only about 1.4 times its resting energy.
- Heart rate triples at take-off but drops back near resting levels within 10 minutes of soaring.
- About 30 griffons can finish a sheep carcass in half an hour.
- Their gut acid has a pH of about 1–1.5.
- When foraging, a griffon is often blind straight ahead, which is why it hits wind turbines.
- Spain holds more than 90% of Europe's griffon vultures.
- A captive griffon lived to 41.4 years.

### Concise site-ready summary
The griffon vulture is a big 8 kg scavenger with a 2.6 m wingspan that soars for hours at almost no energy cost while scanning thousands of square kilometres for carcasses. It has a strong tearing beak and an acid gut, but its weak feet and light, unarmored body make it a poor fighter against anything healthy.

### Rich narrative profile
The griffon vulture is built around soaring. With broad wings spanning up to 2.8 m and a body of about 8 kg, it rides thermals over mountains and plains at barely more than its resting metabolic rate, covering foraging ranges of around 10,000 km² from its cliff colonies. Take-off is the hard part: a triple-speed heartbeat and heavy flapping lift it into the first thermal, after which it can glide between updrafts at close to 19 m/s.

Its tools are those of a carcass specialist. A heavy hooked beak rips open hides, the bare head and neck stay clean inside the body cavity, and an extremely acidic gut handles decaying meat. Griffons find food by sight and by watching each other, and a feeding crowd strips a carcass in minutes, with older adults bullying their way to the best places. Occasionally, when food is short, griffons have attacked newborn or weakened livestock.

In a fight, the griffon is outclassed by almost any predator of similar size. It cannot grip or kill with its feet, it is slow to get airborne, and its body offers no armor. Its ABS profile therefore stays low in Attack and Defense while scoring high in Stamina and Special, reflecting an animal that wins by outlasting and out-searching rivals, not by overpowering them.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Species identity:** the legacy entry names *Gyps africanus*; the photo and this report are *Gyps fulvus*.
- **Mass:** 6.2–10.5 kg (field guides) vs 6–11 kg (GPS studies); 15 kg records are probably captive birds.
- **Speed:** 18.8 m/s is a mean glide speed; overall travel speed is far lower (5.1 m/s mean on windless days).
- **Lifespan:** 41.4 years is a captive maximum, not an average wild life.
- **Livestock attacks:** based on farmer complaints; only about 22% of claims were compensated, so many were not confirmed kills.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Condor (10 kg; A34 D22 Ag70 St82 I69 Sp74):** a slightly lighter soaring scavenger with similar weapons. The griffon sits just below in Attack (32.0) and Agility (66.0, higher wing loading and clumsy take-off), level in Defense, and a point higher in Stamina (83.0) on its measured soaring economy and fasting.
- **Versus Bearded Vulture (6.2 kg; A29 D20 Ag74 St80 I68 Sp76):** the griffon is heavier with a flesh-tearing beak, so Attack is higher (32.0 vs 29.0). The bearded vulture keeps higher Agility and Special for its precise bone-dropping and bone-digesting specialization.
- **Versus Golden Eagle (5 kg; A40 D21 Ag88 St79 I66 Sp70):** the eagle is lighter but a true predator with killing talons, so it stays well above the griffon in Attack and Agility; the griffon edges it in Stamina.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the legacy Raw Power 66, Weaponry 76 and Speed 96.5 are rejected. A scavenger with walking feet does not have strong weapons, and a 19 m/s glide is not a near-ceiling speed.
