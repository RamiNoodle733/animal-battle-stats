# Wild Boar (*Sus scrofa*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Wild boar (also wild pig or Eurasian wild pig)
- **Scientific name:** *Sus scrofa* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild Eurasian and North African wild boar, the wild form of the species from which domestic pigs were bred. Feral domestic pigs and hybrid "wild hogs" in the Americas and Australia are not the canonical form. The site's live photo shows a wild boar in Kraków, Poland, so the specimen follows central European boar.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult European male ("boar") of about **100 kg**, **80 cm** at the shoulder and **150 cm** head-body length, with lower tusks exposed about **10 cm**.
- **Sex choice:** males are heavier, grow ever-lengthening tusks, develop a thick shoulder shield in the rut and do the fighting over mates.
- **Population variation:** size changes enormously across the range. Mediterranean males can average only about 50 kg, most European males 75–100 kg, eastern European males 110–130 kg, and some males in the Russian Far East and Manchuria 270–350 kg. The canonical 100 kg sits at the top of the typical European range, not at Far Eastern extremes.

- Coarse, dense bristly coat and a heavy, wedge-shaped head and neck.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
A boar attacks by charging and pointing its tusks at the target. Human injuries are typically penetrating wounds to the thigh and lower limbs; in a Spanish emergency department series of 34 patients, 50% had superficial wounds, 38.2% penetrating wounds and 71.9% of wounds were on the legs. Hunting dogs take the brunt of this weapon: over 797 driven hunts in Czechia, boars inflicted 150 injuries on dogs, of which 18.8% were severe and 7.4% fatal. Old boars have been recorded goring a small tiger and a tigress to death in self-defense, and a large male tiger died of wounds from a boar it killed. A group of boar was once seen killing and eating an adult axis deer, but boar do not normally hunt large animals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A compact, short-legged body with a wedge-shaped head lets the boar charge through dense thickets. It can reach about 40 km/h, jump 140–150 cm and swim long distances; one boar swam about 11 km from France to Alderney in 2013. Its head and neck work as a plough, digging 8–10 cm into frozen ground and turning rocks of 40–50 kg.

### Endurance
GPS-collared boar in the Czech Republic and Sweden travelled about **5 km a day** normally and about **8 km** on the day of a drive hunt, fleeing an average of 1.8 km and staying away about 26 hours before returning. In the rut, males travel long distances in search of sows, eating little on the way. Endurance is good for a heavy ungulate but not exceptional.

### Senses
Smell is excellent, good enough that boar have been used for drug detection in Germany, and hearing is acute. Eyesight is weak: boar lack color vision and cannot recognize a standing human 10–15 m away. Wild boar keep higher expression of olfaction-related genes in the nasal lining than domestic pigs.

### Intelligence and tactics
Boar adjust behavior to experience: individuals that had lived through more drive hunts were more likely to leave their range when hunted. Near Prague, GPS-tagged boar kept the same movements despite huge numbers of human visitors but spent 41% more energy and slept worse, showing they tolerate people while staying alert. In a documented rescue, an adult female manipulated wooden logs holding shut a cage trap and freed two trapped young, which the authors describe as complex, precisely targeted problem solving. Domestic pigs of the same species show cognitive complexity comparable to animals usually considered intelligent.

### Sociality and hunting
Female-led sounders of sows and their young, led by an old matriarch; males leave at 8–15 months and live mostly alone, joining sounders in the rut. The boar is an omnivore that gets most of its food by digging for underground plant parts and burrowing animals, and it moves into oak and beech forests when they fruit. A 50 kg boar needs about 4,000–4,500 calories a day.

### Fighting and aggression
Rival males fight fiercely during the rut, protected by the shoulder shield they grow beforehand. Sows defend piglets and adopt the young of sows that die. Attacks on humans are rare but can be serious.

### Predator defense
The wolf is the main predator over most of the range, and boar are a positively selected wolf prey in places such as central Spain, the Romanian Carpathians and the Slovak Carpathians. Wolves mainly take piglets and subadults and rarely attack adult sows; deep snow makes boar far more vulnerable. Tigers in the Russian Far East depend heavily on boar, and leopards in Iran take boar but generally avoid adults above their preferred prey size. The giant 270 kg-plus males of the Far East are generally immune from wolf predation.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense forest, reed beds and scrub, where it charges from cover.
- Farmland and forest mosaics with abundant food.
- Rivers and lakes, which it swims readily.
- Urban edges, where it tolerates people.

### Weaknesses
- Poor eyesight and no color vision.
- Deep snow badly limits movement.
- Short-range weapon that requires a head-on charge.
- Shield is seasonal and covers only the shoulders and flanks.
- Piglets and young are easy prey.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** dogs, single wolves, medium predators, animals that must fight it in dense cover.
- **Mixed:** leopards, black bears and similar-sized animals with strong weapons.
- **Bad:** tigers, brown bears, wolf packs in deep snow, and much larger herbivores.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Tusk-Point Charge** — a charge from cover with the tusks pointed at the target; the sharp lower tusks, exposed 10–12 cm, cause penetrating wounds, mostly to the legs, and kill a share of the hunting dogs they strike.
2. **Rut Shield** — before the breeding season males grow a 2–3 cm layer of tough subcutaneous tissue from the shoulder blades to the rump that protects vital organs from a rival's tusks.

### Unique traits
1. **Detection-Dog Nose** — the boar's sense of smell is good enough that boar have been used for drug detection in Germany, and wild boar keep more olfaction gene activity than domestic pigs.
2. **Venom-Blocking Receptor** — pigs carry amino-acid changes in the snake α-neurotoxin binding site of the muscle nicotinic receptor, functionally equivalent to those of honey badgers and hedgehogs, which resist cobra-type venom.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
One of the most widely distributed land mammals: native across Europe, North Africa and much of Asia, east to the Russian Far East and the Lesser Sunda Islands, in forests, reed beds, scrub, farmland and mountains. Introduced and feral populations, often mixed with domestic pigs, occur in the Americas, including 44 US states. It lives increasingly in and around cities such as Kraków, Bordeaux, Budapest and the suburbs of Prague.

### Diet and ecology
An omnivore that roots for underground plant parts and burrowing animals, feeds on acorns and beech mast in fruiting years and raids crops. Its rooting upturns large amounts of soil. Boar are also an important prey for wolves and tigers, and invasive feral pigs raid turtle nests where they have been introduced.

### Social structure
Matriarchal sounders of related sows and young, typically about 6–20 animals. Adult males are mostly solitary outside the rut. Sows in a sounder protect all the young together, and piglets can nurse from other sows.

### Reproduction and life history
Sows usually first breed at about two years; males rarely take part in the rut before 4–5 years because older males keep them away. Piglets weigh about 0.6–1.0 kg at birth. Heavy juvenile mortality means the average boar lives only 1–2 years, and few pass 4–5 years in the wild.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. Numbers are growing in much of Europe, where driven hunts with dogs are used to manage them, bringing crop damage, urban conflict and concern over African swine fever. Outside its native range feral pigs are a damaging invasive animal.

### Special features
- Ever-growing tusks; lower tusks exposed 10–12 cm.
- Seasonal 2–3 cm shoulder-to-rump shield in rutting males.
- Excellent sense of smell, weak eyesight.
- Can jump 140–150 cm and swim more than 10 km.
- Pig muscle receptors resist snake α-neurotoxins.

### Human interaction
Boar have been hunted for thousands of years and appear in cave art and in the myths of many cultures. Today they are a major game animal, and driven hunts with dogs are common in Europe. Attacks on people are rare; in one urban case series most wounds were on the legs and only one of 34 patients had a complication.

### Genuine fun facts
- Wild boar have been used as drug-sniffing animals in Germany.
- A boar swam about 11 km from France to the island of Alderney in 2013.
- Rutting males grow a natural shield up to 3 cm thick over their shoulders.
- A mother boar once freed two trapped young by working the logs that held a trap door shut.
- A boar's snout can turn over rocks of 40–50 kg.
- Some Far Eastern boar weigh as much as a brown bear, up to about 300 kg.
- Domestic pigs were bred from wild boar.

### Concise site-ready summary
The wild boar is a 100 kg Eurasian pig with sharp, ever-growing tusks, a rut shield of tough tissue over its shoulders and an excellent nose. It charges from cover with its tusks, which can kill dogs and have even killed tigers, though wolves and tigers still prey on young and weakened boar.

### Rich narrative profile
A wild boar is a compact wedge of muscle and bristle: around 100 kg in a big European male, 80 cm at the shoulder, with a long snout, small eyes and tusks that never stop growing. The sharp lower tusks are the main weapons, and a charging boar drives them into legs and flanks. Every autumn, males grow a shield of tough tissue up to 3 cm thick over their shoulders before fighting rivals for access to sows.

The species is spectacularly versatile. Boar live from Mediterranean scrub to Siberian taiga, dig through frozen ground with their snouts, swim between islands and have moved into the suburbs of many European cities. They learn from hunting pressure, tolerate crowds of people while staying alert, and have shown striking problem solving, as when a sow freed trapped young from a cage trap. Their sense of smell is keen enough for drug detection, even if their eyesight is poor.

On the ABS scale the boar is a strong mid-weight brawler. It is lighter than the giant forest hog and a large black bear and cannot match a wolf pack or a tiger, but its tusks, toughness and temper make it one of the most dangerous animals of its size to confront in dense cover. Its ratings sit in the roster's moderate middle, with Ferocity and Senses as standout substats.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** geographic variation is extreme (50 kg Mediterranean to 350 kg Far East); 100 kg represents a large European male.
- **Lifespan:** average life of 1–2 years (ADW) reflects piglet mortality; 5 years represents an animal that reaches adulthood.
- **Speed:** 40 km/h is a literature figure without a stated method.
- **Venom resistance:** shown at the receptor level in pigs; not a demonstrated immunity to all snakebite in wild boar.
- **Photo:** the live photo may be a sow or subadult; the ratings describe an adult male.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Giant Forest Hog (210 kg; A56 D50 Ag50 St60 I48 Sp54):** as the giant forest hog report expected, the heavier forest hog stays above the boar in Size (47 vs 44) and Raw Power (52 vs 50) and slightly in Attack. The boar's rut shield gives it a one-point edge in Defense (51.0), and its speed, jumping, swimming and documented learning put it well ahead in Agility (58.0) and Intelligence (63.0).
- **Versus Gray Wolf (50 kg; A55 D41 Ag66 St89 I78 Sp64):** wolves prey heavily on boar but mostly on piglets and subadults. The wolf keeps a one-point Attack edge as a skilled predator and far higher Stamina and Intelligence; the heavier, shielded boar is much higher in Defense (51.0 vs 41.0).
- **Versus Black Bear (120 kg; A56 D49 Ag70 St66 I68 Sp61):** a slightly heavier generalist. The bear's grappling forelimbs, climbing and cognition keep it higher in Attack, Agility and Intelligence; the boar is slightly higher in Defense from its shield and toughness.
- **Versus Warthog (85 kg; A47 D44 Ag60):** the boar is heavier and more aggressive with a rut shield, so it rates higher in Attack and Defense, while the leggier warthog is a little more agile.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the legacy Attack 75.4, Defense 71.4 and Toughness 94 are rejected; a 100 kg pig that wolves and tigers regularly kill is dangerous but not in the roster's upper tier. The legacy Agility 16.6 was far too low for a 40 km/h animal that jumps 1.5 m.
