# Pallas's Cat (*Otocolobus manul*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Pallas's cat (also manul)
- **Scientific name:** *Otocolobus manul* (Pallas, 1776); sometimes placed in *Felis* as *Felis manul*, the name Animal Diversity Web still uses
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild Pallas's cat of the cold montane steppes, rocky grasslands and scree of Central Asia, from the Caucasus and Iran through Mongolia and the Tibetan Plateau. Two subspecies (*O. m. manul* and *O. m. nigripectus*) are treated together.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **4.0 kg**, near the top of the usual 2.5–4.5 kg range (Wikipedia) and inside ADW's 2.5–5.0 kg range without using the heaviest records.
- **Sex choice:** sexual dimorphism is slight. ADW records "no significant sexual dimorphism; however, males may be slightly heavier"; male and female skull ranges overlap widely (Wikipedia). A male is used because males fight other males in the breeding season (ADW notes facial scratches on males).
- **Population notes:** winter fur is greyer, denser and less marked than summer fur. Individuals have been recorded from about 570 m up to 5,073 m (Wikipedia) and up to 5,600 m (Frontiers in Genetics, 2024). The species needs areas where continuous snow cover stays below about 15–20 cm.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
Pallas's cat is a specialist ambush hunter of pikas, voles, gerbils and ground squirrels. It stalks or waits at burrow exits and also hooks rodents out of shallow burrows with its paws (Wikipedia; National Zoo). ADW describes three hunting modes: stalking, flushing and ambushing. Its weapons are ordinary small-cat claws and bite on a 4 kg frame. In Sanjiangyuan, China, its diet is dominated by plateau pikas and small rodents (Animals, 2025). It is effective against animals of a few hundred grams and weak in absolute terms against anything larger.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Short legs, a heavy coat and a stocky build make it a slow mover. ADW states that running "is an unlikely means of escape from large terrestrial or aerial predators". It is sure-footed among rocks, scree and ravines, and its low-set ears let it peer over a rock showing only a small part of its head. Agility is therefore well below the lithe small cats on the roster.

### Endurance
It survives harsh continental winters and high altitude, and radio-tracked males in central Mongolia used home ranges of 20.9–207.0 km² (Wikipedia). But it is a sit-and-wait hunter that avoids open ground, and winter mortality is high. Stamina is moderate: excellent tolerance of cold and thin air, not long-distance pursuit.

### Senses
Large, forward-directed eyes with round pupils that contract to small discs in sunlight (Wikipedia; National Zoo). It hunts mostly at dusk, dawn and night. A 2026 comparative eye study examined its eye structure alongside the lion and clouded leopard but did not report special acuity figures. Senses are scored as good felid vision and hearing without a documented specialization.

### Intelligence and tactics
Den use is strategic: one Mongolian study recorded 101 dens (winter, summer and maternal dens) chosen near rocky cover or ravines (Wikipedia). It spends most of its time in the "safe" rocky and ravine habitats and avoids open steppe (ADW). When pursued it bolts into a crevice, or flattens and freezes beside rocks. This is careful, risk-averse behavior rather than flexible tactical fighting.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Males' ranges overlap one to four females. It shares its habitat and much of its prey with red foxes and corsac foxes; in Sanjiangyuan the diets of Pallas's cats and red foxes overlapped strongly (0.81) but differed in the proportions of shared prey (Animals, 2025).

### Fighting and aggression
When excited or scared it gives a yelp like a small dog, along with growls (National Zoo). A captive male became more aggressive and territorial at the start of the breeding season (Wikipedia). It does not seek fights with larger animals and depends on hiding.

### Environmental strengths
- Cold, dry, rocky montane steppe with crevices and marmot burrows for shelter.
- Camouflage against grey rock and dry grass.
- Tolerance of extreme cold and high altitude.

### Weaknesses
- Poor runner; cannot outrun foxes, wolves, dogs or raptors in the open.
- Small mass; no armor beyond thick fur.
- Deep snow restricts it.
- Unusually vulnerable to disease in captivity, especially toxoplasmosis in kittens.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** pikas, voles, gerbils, small birds and insects.
- **Mixed:** small animals of similar mass in rocky cover where it can ambush.
- **Bad:** foxes and wolves in open terrain, eagles, dogs, and any fast or much larger opponent.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Rock-Peer Ambush** — its low-set rounded ears let it look over a rock or ridge while exposing only a sliver of its head, so it can watch pika colonies and burrow exits unseen before pouncing. The same posture helps it avoid being spotted by foxes and eagles.
2. **Crevice Vanish** — when threatened it bolts into a rock crevice or den, or flattens and freezes beside rocks, relying on its grey coat for camouflage instead of speed. In rocky terrain this makes it hard to catch; in open ground it is exposed.

### Unique traits
1. **Steppe Winter Coat** — up to 9,000 hairs per cm², with underfur about 40 mm and guard hairs up to 69 mm long, and belly fur about twice as long as the fur on its back, suited to winters on the steppe and the Tibetan Plateau.
2. **Round-Pupil Cat** — unlike most small cats with slit pupils, its pupils close to small round discs in bright light, a feature it shares with the cougar, jaguarundi and cheetah.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Rocky montane grassland, shrubland, scree slopes and ravines across Central Asia, from the Caucasus, Iran and Afghanistan through Kazakhstan, Mongolia, southern Siberia and western China to the Himalaya and Tibetan Plateau. It needs areas where continuous snow cover stays below about 15–20 cm, and shelters in rock crevices, old marmot burrows and sometimes juniper cavities.

### Diet and ecology
A specialist predator of small mammals, above all pikas, plus voles, gerbils, ground squirrels, young marmots, small birds and insects (Wikipedia; Animals, 2025). In Mongolia's Sükhbaatar Province, Brandt's voles dominated its diet during a vole irruption in 2017–2020. It competes with red and corsac foxes and raptors for the same prey.

### Social structure
Solitary. Home ranges in central Mongolia were 7.4–125.2 km² for 16 females and 20.9–207.0 km² for nine males, shrinking in winter. Males overlap several females and sometimes other males.

### Reproduction and life history
Litters of two to six kittens are born from late April to late May. Kittens open their eyes at about two weeks, weigh 500–600 g at two months, start hunting at about five months and reach adult size at six to seven months (Wikipedia). In European zoos, first-year mortality from all causes was 71.59% in 2006–2016, with toxoplasmosis the leading infectious cause (Journal of Zoo and Wildlife Medicine, 2020).

### Conservation
Listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List since 2020, reflecting its wide range. Some populations are threatened by poaching, poisoning of pikas and voles in rodent-control campaigns, herding dogs, habitat fragmentation from mining and roads, and continuing trade in skins and body parts. It was once hunted for more than 10,000 skins a year. It is listed as Near Threatened on the Mongolian Red List.

### Special features
- Extremely dense, long fur with longer fur on the underparts.
- Low-set, rounded ears for peering over rocks.
- Round pupils.
- Short, stocky legs and a flat, broad face.
- Heavy use of rock crevices and marmot burrows.

### Human interaction
Harmless to people. Threats come from poisoning of its prey, trapping, dogs and the fur and medicine trade. It is kept in about 60 zoos in coordinated breeding programmes, where kitten survival has been a long-standing problem.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its fur can have up to 9,000 hairs per square centimetre.
- When excited or scared it yelps like a small dog rather than meowing.
- Its pupils shrink to round dots, not slits, in bright light.
- It has been recorded at 5,073 m in the Himalaya and up to about 5,600 m on the Tibetan Plateau.
- One Mongolian study found its cats using 101 different dens.
- In European zoos, more than 70% of kittens died in their first year during 2006–2016, many from toxoplasmosis.
- Its short, low-set ears let it peek over a rock while showing almost none of its head.

### Concise site-ready summary
A stocky, heavily furred wild cat of Central Asia's cold, rocky steppes, Pallas's cat ambushes pikas and voles from behind rocks and hides rather than runs. Its dense coat and camouflage suit it to harsh winters, but at 4 kg with short legs it is slow and weak in absolute combat terms.

### Rich narrative profile
Pallas's cat looks larger than it is. The dense grey coat, broad flat face and low-set ears give it a round, fluffy shape, but underneath is a 4 kg cat about the size of a house cat, with short legs built for creeping between rocks rather than sprinting. It hunts pikas and voles by waiting at burrow exits, stalking, or flushing prey from cover, and it scoops rodents out of shallow tunnels with its paws.

Its strategy is concealment. It spends most of its time in rocky outcrops and ravines, chooses dens carefully and, when a fox, dog or eagle appears, it slips into a crevice or flattens and freezes against the rocks. Animal Diversity Web notes that it is probably a poor runner, which is why open steppe is so dangerous for it. Winter is the hardest season, and wild adults in one Mongolian study averaged only about 27 months of life.

On the absolute scale, Pallas's cat is a small predator with modest weapons. Its dense fur gives it a little more protection than a house cat and its cold tolerance is remarkable, but it lacks the speed and agility of most small cats. It rates below the house cat in Attack and far below the lynx and snow leopard that share some of its mountains.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Genus:** *Otocolobus manul* (IUCN, Wikipedia) versus *Felis manul* (ADW). The same species.
- **Lifespan:** 8–9 years in zoos versus a 27.1-month wild adult average; the canonical 8.5 is a potential lifespan and is flagged as such.
- **Mass:** the National Zoo's lower bound of 4 lb is below other sources; the 4.0 kg canonical male sits inside every source's range.
- **Speed, height and bite:** no measurements; all 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus House Cat (5 kg; A24 D16 Ag88 St48 I63 Sp60):** a similar-sized cat. Pallas's cat sits slightly below in Attack (21.0), slightly above in Defense (17.0, for its dense coat), far below in Agility (60.0, a documented poor runner) and a little above in Stamina (56.0) and Special (64.0).
- **Versus Lynx (24 kg; A48 D31 Ag85) and Snow Leopard (50 kg; A61 D42 Ag94):** mountain cats that share parts of its range; both are many times heavier and far better armed and more agile, so Pallas's cat is 27–40 points lower in Attack.
- **Versus Badger (13 kg; Ag52) and Honey Badger (14 kg; Ag61):** its Agility of 60.0 places it with these heavier, slower-footed animals rather than with the fast small cats.
- **Versus Red Fox (7.2 kg; A28 D25):** its main competitor and a documented predator; the fox's greater mass and speed keep it above Pallas's cat in Attack and Agility.
- **Anti-inflation check:** its famous fluffiness is reflected only in Protection (16.0), not in Defense or Toughness beyond a small margin.
