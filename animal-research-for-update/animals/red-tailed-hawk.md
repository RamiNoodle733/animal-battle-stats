# Red-tailed Hawk — *Buteo jamaicensis*

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Roster name:** Red-tailed Hawk
- **Resolved taxon:** *Buteo jamaicensis* (Gmelin, 1788), family Accipitridae.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime **adult female**, represented at **1.2 kg**, **58.0 cm total length**, and about **125 cm wingspan**. Females are the larger sex and therefore the appropriate canonical fighter.
- **Dimorphism:** Cornell reports males 690–1300 g and 45–56 cm, females 900–1460 g and 50–65 cm. ADW summarizes females as about 25% larger. Plumage is not reliably sex-diagnostic.
- **Population variation:** the species is geographically broad and highly polymorphic, with multiple subspecies and substantial regional plumage and body-size variation. The canonical values represent a large continental female, not an exceptional record bird.

## 4. Combat biology
### Weapons and offensive mechanics
The primary weapons are the feet. Red-tailed Hawks descend onto prey with legs extended, seize it with sharp curved talons, and use the enlarged hallux claw to oppose the forward toes. The beak is important for tearing and processing prey after capture but should not be modeled as the main killing system. Their broad wings support controlled approach and braking rather than the extreme high-speed stoop of a peregrine.

The prey record demonstrates practical weapon effectiveness. ADW reports that roughly 80–85% of diet can consist of small rodents and that eastern cottontail-sized mammals are taken. The U.S. Forest Service review describes prey up to jackrabbit size as potential prey and lists squirrels, prairie dogs, rabbits, pheasants and grouse among recorded items. A 2022 urban diet study recorded **1,348 prey items across 28 species**, with 86% mammals, 10% birds and 4% reptiles.

### Defense and durability
Defense is primarily avoidance: altitude, flight, broad visual surveillance and rapid departure. An adult is formidable to small animals but physically fragile compared with mammalian predators of similar or greater mass. Feathers provide insulation and limited superficial buffering, not armor. A damaged wing can catastrophically reduce mobility.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Broad rounded wings and a short broad tail suit soaring, circling, controlled descents and low-speed positional control. Cornell notes slow heavy wingbeats mixed with gliding/soaring and controlled hunting dives. This is excellent three-dimensional repositioning but should not be confused with falcon-class maximum speed.

### Endurance
Soaring reduces the energetic cost of aerial search, allowing prolonged patrol and territorial activity. Some populations migrate while others are resident. The species can sustain repeated hunting and territorial flights, but this report does not infer elite sustained flapping performance from soaring alone.

### Senses
Vision is the dominant combat sense. ADW describes extraordinarily keen vision that detects prey movement at great distances. Raptors generally possess specialized high-resolution visual systems, but species-specific behavioral acuity measurements for *B. jamaicensis* were not located in this run, so no unsupported numerical acuity claim is made. Hearing is useful but secondary to visual prey detection.

### Tactics and intelligence
The species commonly uses elevated perches to scan, then executes a controlled descent onto prey. It also soars while searching, adjusts hunting to habitat and prey availability, and vigorously defends territories. This is competent predatory decision-making and spatial learning, but there is no basis for corvid-level cognition.

### Fighting and predator defense
Adults can be aggressive in nest and territory defense. Cornell reports them chasing other hawks, eagles and Great Horned Owls, and courting pairs may lock talons aerially. Intraspecific conflict therefore includes aerial pursuit and talon presentation. Adult predation is limited compared with nest predation, but Great Horned Owls are important competitors and can be dangerous opponents.

### Environmental strengths
- Open fields, woodland edges and elevated perches favor visual detection and controlled aerial attack.
- Thermals and open air favor low-cost soaring and surveillance.
- Broad habitat tolerance supports tactical flexibility across rural and urban landscapes.

### Environmental limitations
- Dense enclosed vegetation reduces the value of broad-winged flight and long-range visual detection.
- Grounded combat removes most of the bird's positional advantage.
- Wing injury is disproportionately serious.
- Talon attacks require entering close range.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favorable:** rodents, small birds, reptiles and similarly small opponents vulnerable to an aerial talon strike; terrestrial opponents that cannot contest altitude.

**Unfavorable:** substantially larger mammalian predators, armored opponents that talons cannot penetrate, powerful grapplers if they secure the bird, and faster aerial specialists capable of dictating air combat.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits
### Special abilities
1. **Skyline Targeting** — long-range raptor vision paired with elevated perches and soaring lets the hawk detect and track small terrestrial prey before committing to attack.
2. **Talon Drop** — a controlled aerial descent with legs extended converts altitude and surprise into a two-footed gripping strike using sharp talons and the enlarged hallux.

### Unique traits
1. **Broad-Wing Control** — broad rounded wings and a short broad tail favor stable soaring, braking and low-speed attack placement rather than falcon-style maximum-speed pursuit.
2. **Continental Generalist** — extraordinary geographic, habitat and prey breadth lets the species switch among rodents, birds and reptiles and exploit human-modified landscapes.

## 8. Expanded profile
### Habitat and range
Red-tailed Hawks occupy much of North America and extend through parts of Central America and the Caribbean. They are especially associated with open country, fields, roadsides, woodland edges and landscapes combining high perches with exposed hunting ground. Some northern populations migrate while many southern populations are resident.

### Diet and ecology
They are generalist vertebrate predators. Mammals dominate many populations, especially rodents, but birds and reptiles can be important. USGS comparative work found both Red-tailed Hawks and Great Horned Owls to be generalists, with Red-tailed Hawks taking significantly more reptiles and having significantly greater mean prey mass in the datasets analyzed.

### Social structure
Adults are usually encountered singly or in territorial pairs rather than hunting cooperatively. Pair bonds organize breeding and territorial defense. Sociality therefore does not multiply the canonical individual's battle power.

### Reproduction and life history
Cornell reports one brood, generally **1–5 eggs**, **28–35 days incubation**, and **42–46 days nestling period**. Both sexes build or refurbish nests. Females perform most incubation while males provide much of the food, though males also incubate for shorter periods.

### Conservation and human interaction
The Red-tailed Hawk is one of the most familiar North American raptors and adapts well to agricultural, suburban and roadside mosaics where hunting perches are abundant. Human infrastructure can provide perches and nest sites but also exposes hawks to vehicle strikes, electrocution and other anthropogenic hazards.

### Major adaptations
Broad soaring wings, acute vision, sharply curved talons, a large hallux claw, reversible aerial-to-ground attack geometry, and generalist prey choice form a coherent predatory system. None should be converted into fictional bite pressure or falcon-level dive speed.

### Fun facts
- Females can be roughly a quarter larger than males.
- The familiar red tail is an adult feature; immatures have a differently patterned brown tail.
- A wild banded individual reached **30 years 8 months**, according to the USGS Bird Banding Laboratory.
- Red-tailed Hawks sometimes lock talons during aerial courtship.
- They readily hunt from utility poles and other human-made perches.
- Cornell's long-running campus pair has repeatedly raised young on a human structure in Ithaca, illustrating urban adaptability.

### Concise site summary
A broad-winged North American raptor that combines acute visual targeting, controlled aerial positioning and powerful talons. The Red-tailed Hawk is devastating to small terrestrial prey but remains a lightweight fighter whose defense depends much more on staying airborne than absorbing damage.

### Rich narrative profile
The Red-tailed Hawk is a patient aerial ambush predator rather than a pure speed specialist. It often wins the information battle first, watching from a pole, tree or soaring circle until movement exposes prey. Its broad wings then trade extreme falcon-like velocity for control: the bird can descend deliberately, extend both feet and place its talons onto a target with the enlarged rear claw helping close the grip. That combination lets a roughly kilogram-scale bird routinely kill small mammals and take prey as substantial as rabbits. The same anatomy has strict limits on an absolute roster. There is no shell, thick hide or large body mass behind the attack. If a much larger opponent survives the first contact and catches the hawk, the physical matchup deteriorates quickly. The correct ABS profile is therefore asymmetric: low-to-moderate absolute damage and durability, but exceptional agility, senses and positional control.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** Cornell's female range extends to 1.46 kg while ADW's older summary gives a narrower overall range. The canonical 1.2 kg sits well inside Cornell's sex-specific range and avoids an exceptional maximum.
- **Speed:** many secondary sites publish hawk speed numbers, but this run did not locate a defensible instrumented species-specific maximum. `speed_mps` stays 0.0.
- **Lifespan:** 30 years 8 months is a longevity record, not an average. The proposed 20.0-year field is explicitly conservative longevity context and should be revisited if a robust wild life-expectancy estimate is needed.
- **Vision:** exceptional prey-detection vision is well supported qualitatively, but behavioral acuity measurements found in fresh literature were for other raptor species. No numerical acuity is transferred to *B. jamaicensis*.
- **Talon dimensions:** morphometric ranges vary by population and measurement method. They are contextual weapon dimensions, not a force estimate.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes
- At 1.2 kg, Red-tailed Hawk must remain far below medium carnivores such as Red Fox in Raw Power and physical Defense despite its lethal efficiency against small prey.
- Its Attack exceeds Raven because the feet are specialized predatory weapons, but Raven remains decisively superior in general cognition.
- Agility can legitimately sit near the elite band because three-dimensional control is the category itself, not a proxy for body size or top speed.
- It should remain below Peregrine Falcon in pure high-speed aerial specialization while comparing favorably in broad-wing low-speed control.
- Special is driven by the combination of acute visual targeting and aerial strike geometry, not by double-counting talons already represented in Attack/Weaponry.
- Unsupported live-site speed, bite-force or placeholder scores were not used as anchors.
