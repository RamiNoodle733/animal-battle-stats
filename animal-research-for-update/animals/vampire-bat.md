# Vampire Bat (*Desmodus rotundus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Vampire Bat (common vampire bat)
- **Scientific name:** *Desmodus rotundus* (É. Geoffroy, 1810), family Phyllostomidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the common vampire bat of the American tropics and subtropics, not the hairy-legged or white-winged vampire bats, which feed mainly on birds.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult female, **0.035 kg** (35 g, unfed), **8.5 cm** head-body length, wingspan about 35–40 cm.
- **Sex choice:** females are larger than males. ADW states females are usually larger, and a morphometric study found female forearms averaged **61.8 mm** versus **58.5 mm** in males. Because the larger sex is also the one with the best-documented social behavior (food sharing), the canonical specimen is an adult female.
- **Population notes:** body mass swings sharply with feeding: a bat can take in blood equal to more than half its body weight in one meal, so the canonical mass is an unfed adult.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapon and offensive mechanics
The vampire bat's "weapon" is a surgical incision, not a damaging bite. It lands near a sleeping host, often a cow or horse, walks or hops to a spot where blood vessels run close to the skin, shaves away hair, and cuts a small wound with its razor incisors. Saliva containing anticoagulants keeps the wound bleeding while the bat laps. The goal is to feed without waking the host, not to injure it.

### Secondary weapons
Saliva is the real force multiplier. It contains **draculin**, a glycoprotein that inhibits clotting factors IXa and Xa; **desmoteplase**, a plasminogen activator that has been tested as a clot-busting stroke drug; and **desmolaris**, an inhibitor of factor XIa. Anticoagulant activity drops with repeated daily salivation and recovers after rest.

### Disease transmission
The common vampire bat is the main transmitter of rabies virus to livestock in Latin America, with an average of about 450 reported outbreaks per year over the past five decades. This is a serious real-world danger to hosts, but it does not act on the timescale of a single fight and is not scored as Attack.

### Defense and toughness
Almost none. The body is tiny and the wing membranes are fragile. The energy budget is so tight that a bat can starve if it fails to feed for 48–72 hours. Its defense is not being caught.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Unusually for a bat, it is agile on the ground. It walks on its wrists and feet at low speed and switches to a forelimb-driven bounding run at higher speed, reaching about 1.2 m/s on a treadmill. It can launch into flight from the ground with a vertical jump driven by the forelimbs, reaching 2.4 m/s in under 30 ms, which lets it escape a host that rolls over or kicks. In the air it is a competent, not elite, flier.

### Endurance
It commutes nightly between roost and hosts and can feed for up to about 30 minutes. But its reserves are small, it must feed most nights, and it cannot sustain high output for long without food.

### Senses
- **Infrared heat sensing:** pit organs around the nose leaf detect warm skin. Vampire bats lower the activation threshold of the TRPV1 heat channel to about **30 °C** in the trigeminal nerve through alternative splicing, turning it into an infrared sensor for warm blood vessels.
- **Echolocation and hearing:** like other phyllostomid bats, it echolocates to navigate at night.
- **Smell and vision:** used to locate hosts; no quantitative data were located for this report.

### Intelligence and tactics
Vampire bats are a classic example of reciprocal food sharing: bats that fed regurgitate blood to hungry roost-mates, including unrelated bats they have a history with. Experiments fasting 20 bats induced sharing on 48 days, and donations tracked past help more than relatedness. This is sophisticated social cognition, but its matchup relevance is limited to stealth, host choice and patience.

### Hunting behavior
A nocturnal parasitic feeder. It approaches sleeping mammals, especially livestock, feeds, and leaves before the host notices. Captive bats take in blood averaging **59.5% of body weight in under 30 minutes** and start excreting dilute urine within minutes of starting to feed to shed excess water and lighten the load before takeoff.

### Intraspecific fighting
Roost-mates may compete over feeding sites and social partners, but fights are small-scale and not a major feature of the species.

### Predator defense
Escape by flight, fast ground running, vertical jump takeoff, and roosting in dark caves, hollow trees, mines and buildings.

### Aggression and ferocity
It avoids fights. A bite is an attempt to feed quietly; a bat that wakes its host flees.

### Environmental strengths
- Dark, warm tropical and subtropical landscapes with livestock or wild mammals.
- Night-time approaches to sleeping hosts.
- Cluttered ground where a flier that can also run and jump has options.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny mass and fragile wings.
- No damaging weapon; its bite is designed not to be felt.
- Needs to feed almost every night; starves quickly.
- Poor in cold climates and in daylight.
- Any awake, alert opponent of real size can crush it.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** only in the narrow sense of feeding on large sleeping mammals, or escaping slow ground-bound opponents.
- **Mixed:** other tiny animals of similar size.
- **Bad:** essentially any alert vertebrate predator larger than itself, owls, snakes, cats, and anything that can pin it.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Heat-Seeking Nose** — pit organs on the nose detect infrared heat through TRPV1 channels tuned to about 30 °C, letting the bat find warm blood vessels close to the skin in darkness. It is one of the very few mammals with infrared sensing.
2. **Anticoagulant Bite** — enamel-free, razor-sharp incisors cut a small wound, and saliva containing draculin, desmoteplase and desmolaris keeps it bleeding while the bat feeds.

### Unique traits
1. **Running Takeoff** — one of very few bats that can run, using a forelimb-driven bounding gait, and it launches into flight from the ground with an explosive vertical jump.
2. **Blood-Sharing Bonds** — well-fed bats regurgitate blood to hungry roost-mates, including unrelated partners, a classic documented case of reciprocal food sharing.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Found from Mexico through Central America to much of South America, in tropical and subtropical forests, dry scrub, savanna and farmland. It roosts in caves, hollow trees, old mines, wells and buildings, and is most common where livestock are plentiful.

### Diet
Exclusively blood (sanguivory), mostly from mammals. Cattle are its preferred prey in many areas; horses, pigs, wild mammals and occasionally birds and people are also bitten.

### Ecology
The spread of cattle ranching has given vampire bats an abundant food supply. As the main reservoir of rabies for livestock in Latin America, the species has major economic and public health impact, and management programs target it in many countries.

### Social structure
Colonial. Females form long-term social bonds within roosts and groom and share food with regular partners. Food sharing depends on past reciprocal help and on kinship.

### Reproduction and life history
Females usually raise a single pup with a long period of maternal care, including regurgitated blood before the pup can forage. Wild lifespan is around 9 years; a captive individual reached 29.2 years.

### Conservation
Widely reported as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List because of its large range and abundance; the IUCN assessment page itself was not retrieved in this run. Locally, populations are culled to control rabies in livestock.

### Special features
- Infrared-sensing nose pits with TRPV1 channels tuned to about 30 °C.
- Enamel-free, razor-sharp upper incisors.
- Anticoagulant saliva (draculin, desmoteplase, desmolaris).
- Forelimb-driven running and vertical jump takeoff.
- Kidneys that shed water rapidly during a blood meal.

### Human interaction
Vampire bats occasionally bite people, and they are the main source of rabies outbreaks in Latin American livestock, with about 450 outbreaks reported per year on average. Their saliva has inspired medical research: desmoteplase was tested as a treatment for ischemic stroke.

### Genuine fun facts
- A vampire bat can drink blood equal to more than half its body weight in about 30 minutes.
- It starts urinating within minutes of beginning to feed to shed water from the meal.
- It can run on the ground using its wings as front legs, at up to about 1.2 m/s.
- It launches from the ground with a vertical jump that reaches 2.4 m/s in under 30 milliseconds.
- Its nose senses heat using a pain receptor retuned to trigger at about 30 °C.
- Well-fed bats regurgitate blood to hungry roost-mates, even unrelated ones.
- A captive vampire bat lived for 29.2 years.

### Concise site-ready summary
The common vampire bat is a 35 g blood specialist that finds warm skin with a heat-sensing nose, cuts it with razor incisors and keeps it bleeding with anticoagulant saliva. It can even run and leap into flight from the ground, but it has almost no fighting power and survives by stealth and escape.

### Rich narrative profile
The common vampire bat is one of the most specialized mammals on Earth. It lives on nothing but blood, which it takes from sleeping animals, most often cattle, across the American tropics and subtropics. A typical adult weighs about 35 grams and could sit in the palm of a hand, yet it feeds on animals that can outweigh it more than ten thousand times.

Every part of its feeding method is precise. Heat-sensing pits on its nose, powered by a heat receptor tuned to about 30 °C, pick out the warm spots where blood vessels lie close to the skin. It shaves the hair, cuts a tiny wound with enamel-free incisors kept razor-sharp, and laps the blood while its saliva's anticoagulants stop the wound from clotting. Its kidneys start shedding water within minutes so it can carry a meal worth more than half its body weight back into the air.

It also moves in ways other bats cannot. On the ground it runs with a bounding gait powered by its wings, and it can spring straight up into flight in a fraction of a second if a host stirs. Back at the roost, bats that fed share blood with hungry partners, a famous case of reciprocal help. None of this makes it a fighter. In any real battle it would be crushed or eaten; its strengths are in stealth, senses and escape, and its real-world danger comes from the rabies it carries to livestock.
