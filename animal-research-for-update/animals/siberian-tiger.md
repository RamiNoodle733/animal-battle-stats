# Siberian Tiger / Amur Tiger (*Panthera tigris*, Amur population)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Roster name:** Siberian Tiger
- **Preferred common name:** Amur tiger
- **Species:** *Panthera tigris*
- **Traditional population/subspecies name:** *Panthera tigris altaica* (Temminck, 1844)
- **Current taxonomy note:** The IUCN SSC Cat Specialist Group describes the Amur tiger as the northern mainland tiger population and notes an unresolved subspecies debate. The 2017 CCTF treatment grouped mainland populations as *P. t. tigris*, while traditional and some recent genetic treatments retain *P. t. altaica*. For ABS, “Siberian Tiger” means the living Amur population of the Russian Far East / northeast China, not a generic tiger.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime adult **male**, 190.0 kg, about 290.0 cm total nose-to-tail length and about 105.0 cm shoulder height. This deliberately represents a robust mature male without using record or historical trophy specimens.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** strong. Males are materially larger than females and are the appropriate individual-combat baseline. Institutional ranges commonly give males around 180–306 kg and 2.7–3.3 m total length, females around 100–167 kg and 2.4–2.7 m.
- **Population variation:** historical Amur males were reported heavier than many modern wild males. A widely cited 2005 synthesis gave about 176.4 kg for contemporary wild males and 215.3 kg for historical wild males; sampling and conflict-animal bias complicate interpretation. ABS therefore avoids the old 300 kg placeholder and record-sized claims.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
Modern institutional summaries span roughly 180–306 kg for adult males, but that upper bound should not be treated as a typical modern wild male. The historical/contemporary synthesis summarized by multiple zoological references gives a contemporary wild-male mean near 176.4 kg. A **190.0 kg** canonical prime male is a defensible strong-adult value while remaining far below exceptional maxima.

### Height
Male shoulder height is commonly reported near **99–107 cm**. Canonical: **105.0 cm**.

### Length
Male total nose-to-tail length is commonly reported around **270–330 cm**. Canonical: **290.0 cm total length**. This includes the tail and should not be confused with head-body length.

### Speed
Reliable field measurement of a species-specific maximum sprint speed is poor. A zoological source reports up to 60 km/h for Amur tigers, while another institutional page repeats 45 mph. Because these are institutional estimates rather than instrumented wild trials, ABS adopts the more conservative **60 km/h = 16.7 m/s** as a provisional top-speed field with **medium-low confidence**. Speed is not used as a substitute for Agility.

### Lifespan
Wild tiger lifespan is generally about 10–15 years. Oregon Zoo gives 10–14 years wild; Smithsonian gives 10–15. Canonical: **14.0 years** as a representative upper-adult lifespan rather than a captive maximum.

### Bite force
No defensible Amur-specific direct bite-pressure measurement in PSI was found. Hartstone-Rose et al. (2012) anatomically modeled *Panthera tigris* total bite force at approximately **703.74 kgf at the canine**, about **6.90 kN**, with higher modeled values at more posterior tooth positions. That is modeled force, not pressure, and is not converted to PSI without contact area.

**Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0 (unresolved, not literal absence of bite).** The live 1,050 PSI figure is not accepted as verified evidence.

### Natural weapons
- **Claws:** tiger claws can reach about **10 cm**, are retractile, and remain protected/sharp during normal locomotion. Broad forelimbs and a functional dewclaw aid prey retention.
- **Canines:** long conical canines deliver throat/nape killing bites. Exact Amur-specific representative exposed crown length varies by skull and measurement convention, so no single centimeter value is promoted here without a specimen-level dataset.
- **Forelimbs/paws:** massive shoulders, broad paws and retractile claws are central to grappling, destabilizing and holding prey.
- **Dentition:** carnassials slice flesh after the initial killing bite; the tongue’s keratinized papillae aid meat removal but are not treated as a combat weapon.

### Defensive structures
No armor or shell. Defense comes from 190 kg body mass, dense musculature, robust skeleton, thick winter pelage and subcutaneous fat adapted to severe cold. Fur and fat are meaningful thermal defenses but weak substitutes for armor against penetrating weapons.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons and offensive mechanics
The Amur tiger is a large grappling ambush predator. A successful attack combines explosive acceleration, a body-weight collision, forelimb hooks, retractile claws and a killing bite. Broad shoulders and forelimbs help arrest and turn ungulate prey while the hindquarters continue driving the body forward. The bite is then directed toward the throat or neck when anatomy and prey position permit.

This combination matters more than any single internet “bite PSI” number. A tiger can repeatedly reposition its bite while maintaining contact with claws and forelimbs, giving it a multi-weapon close-range system.

### Hunting and predation behavior
Peer-reviewed Russian Far East diet work found medium-to-large ungulates dominate Amur tiger biomass. Kerley et al. (2015) found wild boar, roe deer, sika deer and red deer collectively supplied **86.7% of biomass consumed** across three sites. A Sino-Russian border study found wild boar and sika deer together represented **83% of consumed biomass**. GPS/kill-site work also documents predation on large ungulates and occasional bears.

The tiger typically closes distance under cover, attacks explosively, grapples and seeks a lethal neck/throat hold. Failed hunts are common, so stealth and positioning are central rather than optional bonuses.

### Intraspecific fighting
Adults are primarily solitary and territorial. Male home ranges can overlap several females and may be extremely large in low-prey landscapes. Territorial disputes between adults can be serious or fatal. The species therefore has real experience with similarly armed conspecific opponents, although avoidance and scent marking reduce the frequency of direct fights.

### Defense and durability
A 190 kg male has strong absolute trauma tolerance compared with medium carnivores, but lacks dedicated armor. Dense muscle, robust forequarters, thick hide/fur and body mass provide protection, while mobility and deterrence prevent some damage. Defense should remain substantially below heavily armored or multi-ton animals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The tiger is capable of rapid acceleration, pouncing, lateral correction, climbing and swimming. Retractile claws preserve traction tools, padded feet aid quiet approach, and powerful hindlimbs support leaps. It is much more agile than megafauna of similar or greater combat threat, but its size prevents the near-instantaneous turning of small cats.

### Endurance and stamina
This is a burst-oriented ambush predator, not a cursorial pursuit specialist. However, “low stamina” is too simplistic: Amur tigers patrol enormous territories and repeatedly travel through snow and rugged forest. Marwell reports average male home ranges around 1,379 km² in the low-prey Russian Far East. That demonstrates substantial sustained locomotor capacity, but not sustained high-output sprinting or wrestling. Combat stamina is therefore moderate-strong, below wolves, wild dogs and other pursuit specialists.

### Senses
Hunting relies heavily on vision and hearing, with scent important for territorial communication. Low-light performance, acute hearing and close-range whisker/tactile feedback support nocturnal/crepuscular ambush. Senses are strong but not treated as a rare Special mechanism.

### Intelligence and tactics
Tigers show flexible route choice, prey selection, stalking, ambush timing and kill handling. Solitary hunting demands independent decisions rather than group coordination. Intelligence is above a simple reflex predator but below the roster’s great apes, cetaceans and highly flexible social problem-solvers.

### Ferocity
A healthy adult male is willing to tackle dangerous ungulates and defend territory against other tigers. Amur diet records include occasional bears, but bear predation is not evidence that a representative tiger routinely dominates every large bear. Ferocity is high without being used to inflate raw physical power.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense forest, broken terrain, snow and low light strongly favor stealth.
- Thick winter coat and fat permit effective operation in severe cold.
- Excellent swimming prevents water from being an automatic barrier.
- Snow and cover can amplify the value of camouflage and approach discipline.

### Environmental limitations
- Open terrain removes much of the ambush advantage.
- Deep water against specialized marine animals is a severe disadvantage despite competent swimming.
- Prolonged high-output pursuit erodes its strongest burst advantage.
- Heat can penalize the heavily insulated Amur phenotype more than cold does.

### Major weaknesses
1. No armor against tusks, horns, massive bites or crushing impacts.
2. Killing system is close-range, requiring the tiger to enter the opponent’s weapon envelope.
3. Burst acceleration is stronger than prolonged chase output.
4. Solitary tactics offer no cooperative rescue or flanking support in the one-animal ABS baseline.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favored:** similarly sized or smaller terrestrial mammals lacking armor or long-reach weapons, especially where cover enables a surprise grapple.

**Difficult:** heavily armored crocodilians, very large bears, bovids with dangerous horns, giant suids, multi-ton herbivores, and opponents whose reach/armor prevents safe access to the neck.

**Poor:** aquatic specialists in deep water and megafauna whose mass advantage is several-fold.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Snow-Shadow Ambush** — disruptive striping, padded feet, careful stalking and strong low-light senses let the tiger close distance quietly before committing its explosive grapple. Cover and twilight materially increase first-strike probability.
2. **Four-Point Grapple** — massive forelimbs, broad paws, retractile claws and dewclaws can hook and hold prey while the jaws reposition for a neck or throat bite, allowing multiple weapons to function as one control system.

### Unique traits
1. **Taiga Giant** — the Amur population combines very large felid body size with thick seasonal fur, flank/belly fat and paw insulation suited to subzero forest conditions.
2. **Solitary Ungulate Specialist** — one animal independently locates, approaches, controls and kills prey commonly around the size of a human or larger, without pack assistance.

## 8. Expanded profile content

### Habitat and range
The Amur population occupies temperate forests of the Russian Far East and northeast China, especially the Sikhote-Alin region and cross-border landscapes. Its habitat includes mixed broadleaf/conifer forest, mountainous terrain and severe snowy winters. WCS reports recent growth in northeast China, while Russian and Chinese cross-border monitoring continues to refine abundance estimates.

### Diet and ecology
Large ungulates are the ecological core of the diet. Wild boar, red deer, sika deer and roe deer dominate major studies. Smaller mammals and occasional bears broaden the diet, but should not distract from the central fact that viable tiger populations require abundant medium-to-large prey.

### Social structure
Adults are mainly solitary. Females raise cubs alone; male ranges can overlap several females. Scent marking, scratches and vocal/chemical communication reduce unnecessary encounters while maintaining territorial spacing.

### Reproduction and life history
Females mature around three to four years, males somewhat later. Gestation is roughly 3.5 months and litters are commonly several cubs. Juvenile mortality is substantial. Wild adults generally live about 10–15 years.

### Conservation
The tiger species is **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List. The Amur population suffered a severe twentieth-century bottleneck but recovered substantially under protection. Current estimates vary with geography, year and method. WCS reports 70 tigers within Northeast China Tiger and Leopard National Park in 2024, including animals whose territories overlap Russia. A 2025 winter-track study estimated 43–46 adults/subadults plus 12 cubs in one 5,400 km² southwest Primorye survey unit. These are regional figures, not interchangeable global totals.

### Adaptations
- Thick seasonal pelage and fat for severe cold.
- Extra paw fur and broad padded feet for snow, stealth and impact buffering.
- Retractile claws that remain protected until needed.
- Strong forequarters for grappling large prey.
- Stripes that disrupt body outline in broken forest light.
- Strong swimming ability.

### Human interaction
Primary threats include poaching, prey depletion, habitat fragmentation, roads and conflict with people/livestock. Conservation therefore depends on protecting both tigers and their prey base, maintaining connected forest, and rapidly managing conflict animals.

### Genuine fun facts
- Amur tigers now occupy a much smaller, more easterly range than the old name “Siberian tiger” suggests.
- Individual stripe patterns are distinctive enough to aid photographic identification.
- Tigers readily swim, unlike the common stereotype that cats universally avoid water.
- Russian Far East studies repeatedly identify wild boar and deer as key prey.
- Male Amur home ranges can become enormous where prey density is low.
- The population recovered from an extremely small twentieth-century remnant after legal protection and anti-poaching work.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Amur tiger is a cold-adapted, solitary heavyweight cat built around stealth, explosive acceleration and close-range control. A prime male combines roughly 190 kg of muscle with retractile claws, powerful forelimbs and a neck-targeting bite. Its attack and agility are elite among terrestrial predators, but lack of armor and burst-oriented hunting keep Defense and Stamina below the roster’s heaviest and most endurance-specialized animals.

### Rich narrative profile
An Amur tiger wins by deciding when contact begins. In broken forest it advances quietly on padded feet, using vegetation, shadow and its striped coat to reduce detection. The final attack is not simply a bite. The hindquarters accelerate nearly 200 kg of cat into the target, the forelimbs catch and destabilize it, claws preserve purchase, and the jaws search for a neck or throat position. This integration of stealth, impact, grappling and bite delivery makes the tiger much more dangerous than any one isolated measurement implies.

Its weaknesses are equally important to ABS normalization. The tiger is not armored. It must get close enough to horns, tusks, jaws or claws to use its own best weapons, and it cannot sprint indefinitely. Against smaller unarmored terrestrial opponents, that trade usually favors the tiger. Against a saltwater crocodile, rhinoceros, hippopotamus, elephant or other opponent whose armor, reach or mass changes the geometry of contact, the tiger’s superb pound-for-pound athleticism cannot erase the absolute gap.

### Source conflicts and uncertainty
1. **Taxonomy:** *P. t. altaica* remains widely used, while the 2017 IUCN CCTF collapses mainland populations into *P. t. tigris*. CatSG says taxonomy remains under review. ABS records the Amur population explicitly so biological identity remains stable whichever subspecies convention wins.
2. **Mass:** old trophy-era figures and broad zoo summaries overstate what should be used as a representative modern wild male. The 190 kg canonical male is intentionally below exceptional values.
3. **Speed:** 60–72+ km/h claims are common, but strong instrumented Amur-specific maximum-speed evidence was not found. The canonical 16.7 m/s is provisional and lower-confidence.
4. **Bite:** modeled force is available; reliable PSI is not. No force-to-pressure conversion is made.
5. **Conservation counts:** regional counts differ by geography, year and survey method and must not be added casually as if they were synchronized independent populations.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Saltwater Crocodile:** Tiger is far more maneuverable on land, but its 190 kg body, lack of armor and mammalian bite/claw system justify Attack 84.0 and Defense 61.0 remaining below the researched 500 kg crocodile’s Attack 92.0 / Defense 83.0.
- **Versus Serval:** Tiger must dominate absolute Raw Power, Attack, Defense and Weaponry because 190 kg versus 14 kg is an enormous real combat gap. Serval can remain exceptional in Agility because maneuverability is not raw force.
- **Versus Sea Lion / Seal:** Tiger has superior terrestrial weapon delivery and grapple lethality; pinnipeds retain better aquatic maneuverability and dive/endurance specializations.
- **Versus Shoebill / Secretary Bird:** impressive bird weapon mechanics do not compress the absolute attack gap against a 190 kg big cat.
- **Proportional-strength check:** no score is awarded merely because a tiger feat is impressive relative to body mass.
- **Speed/agility check:** provisional 16.7 m/s speed is scored separately from turning, balance, acceleration and grapple control.
- **Double-counting check:** stealth/camouflage chiefly supports Special and Tactics; it does not inflate Raw Power or Defense.
- **Bite-force check:** the old 1,050 PSI placeholder is rejected. The research file retains only modeled force in newtons/kgf and `bite_force_psi: 0.0` unresolved.
