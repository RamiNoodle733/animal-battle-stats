# Dhole (*Cuon alpinus*) research report

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Dhole / Asiatic wild dog
- **Scientific name:** *Cuon alpinus* (Pallas, 1811)
- **Taxon represented:** living species *Cuon alpinus*, family Canidae. The staging recommendation does not anchor to the live site's placeholder combat values.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature **male**, 18.0 kg. Males are only modestly larger than females, but the male is appropriate under the larger/fighting-sex rule.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** modern anatomical literature describes low dimorphism, with males commonly about 15–20 kg and females 10–13 kg. PBS gives 15–20 kg males and about 10–13 kg females. ADW reports a broader 17–21 kg species mass range. Geographic variation is meaningful, including coat length/color and body proportions across northern, Himalayan and tropical populations.
- **Population/taxonomic caution:** older literature recognizes multiple subspecies, but subspecific boundaries have varied among authorities. This report scores the species rather than assuming one historical subspecies represents the entire roster entry.

## 2. Physical measurements and functional anatomy

### Mass and dimensions

A representative adult male of **18.0 kg** is defensible within the repeatedly reported 15–20/21 kg male range. ADW reports about **90 cm head-body length**, **40–45 cm tail**, and **50 cm shoulder height**. A modern osteoarchaeological review likewise characterizes males as 15–20 kg and females as 10–13 kg. The canonical body-length field below uses **90.0 cm head-body**, not head-to-tail length, because that matches the clearest institutional measurement definition.

### Speed

Zoo de Granby states that dholes can reach **55 km/h**, equivalent to **15.3 m/s**, while emphasizing endurance rather than sprint specialization. This is an institutional husbandry/natural-history value rather than a published instrumented maximum, so confidence is moderate rather than high. It is preferable to the live placeholder's unsupported 36 km/h.

### Lifespan

Zoo Taiping reports **10–15 years**. EOL's ADW-derived record gives **16 years average in captivity**, while secondary summaries often distinguish shorter wild longevity. Because a defensible wild representative mean was not located in this run, the canonical value is conservatively **12.5 years**, the midpoint of the zoo's 10–15 year range, with moderate confidence and an explicit captive/wild-context caveat.

### Dentition and bite

Dholes are unusual among living canids in having **40 teeth**, rather than the typical 42, with one fewer lower molar on each side and a specialized lower carnassial morphology. This supports efficient meat shearing, but it does **not** establish bite pressure. No traceable, species-specific bite-pressure measurement or defensible PSI estimate with contact area was verified. Therefore `bite_force_psi = 0.0`. The site's legacy 100 PSI is rejected.

### Defensive structures

There is no armor, shell, thick hide, or specialized impact shield. Protection is ordinary mammalian skin, fur, skeleton and musculature for a lean 18 kg canid. Survival depends much more on mobility, group defense, awareness and avoidance than passive protection.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offensive mechanics

The dhole's primary individual weapon is its dentition. Its relatively short, robust muzzle and specialized carnassials are suited to repeated gripping, tearing and meat-shearing. Unlike a large cat's kill method, dhole success against large ungulates is primarily a **cooperative pursuit-and-dismantling system**, not one overpowering throat bite. Pack members can pressure prey from multiple directions, attack hindquarters/flanks, and exploit openings once prey is destabilized.

The 2014 Journal of Zoology synthesis examined **8,816 prey records from 24 studies at 16 sites**. Sambar, chital and wild boar contributed almost two-thirds of food biomass; larger packs ultimately took larger prey. The accessible prey spectrum was 30–235 kg, with sambar significantly preferred in that synthesis. A 2020 Southeast Asian study found muntjac of roughly 20–28 kg dominant and selected in its two landscapes, showing that prey preference is geographically variable and that Indian prey patterns should not be universalized.

### Defense and toughness

An individual dhole has modest absolute durability. At about 18 kg it is vulnerable to horns, hooves, large cats, bears and larger canids. Its defense is primarily evasive movement, spacing, group harassment and the ability to disengage. This keeps Protection and Defense far below large predators even though a pack can be dangerous.

### Locomotion, maneuverability and stamina

Dholes are cursorial canids with strong sustained movement. A 2020 telemetry study in Khao Yai found adult males traveling about **19.3 km/day** and adult females about **10.2 km/day**, with mean male home range **132.9 km²** versus **64.6 km²** for females. Earlier Mudumalai work recorded pack home ranges of **83.3 and 54.2 km²**. This is excellent evidence for sustained ranging, but it does not justify the live site's near-ceiling stamina placeholder. Daily movement, pursuit capacity and fight-duration endurance are related but not identical.

### Senses and communication

Dholes rely on strong canid olfaction, hearing and vision. Their distinctive whistles are especially useful for maintaining cohesion in forest cover, which is why the species is often called the whistling dog. Cornell describes a variety of calls and whistles used to maintain pack contact. This is combat-relevant chiefly through coordination rather than as a direct weapon.

### Intelligence and tactics

Pack hunting, cooperative breeding, flexible prey selection, spatial ranging and contact communication support strong tactical intelligence. However, evidence does not justify crow/primate/dolphin-level problem-solving scores. The correct ABS placement is high among carnivores for coordination but not near the roster-wide cognitive ceiling.

### Fighting, predation and aggression

Dholes hunt socially and can kill prey much larger than one individual. Pack size varies dynamically. In Mudumalai, two monitored packs ranged from 4–18 and 4–25 individuals. Their predation is best understood as cumulative pursuit, repeated biting, flanking and rapid exploitation of a compromised prey animal. Individual combat capability must not inherit the full power of a pack.

### Environmental strengths

- Forest, forest-edge, scrub and grassland mosaics with adequate ungulate prey.
- Dense cover where whistles help maintain cohesion.
- Terrain allowing repeated pursuit, flanking and group repositioning.
- Access to water and prey-rich habitat patches.

### Environmental limitations and weaknesses

- No armor and only moderate individual mass.
- A lone dhole loses its central pack-coordination advantage.
- Large cats, bears and heavily armed ungulates can kill an individual quickly if contact is forced.
- Open, obstacle-free one-on-one combat reduces the value of pack tactics.
- Habitat fragmentation, prey depletion and disease exposure from domestic dogs are major real-world vulnerabilities.

### Matchup archetypes

**Favorable:** smaller or similarly sized unarmored animals that can be pressured by mobility and repeated bites; slower opponents that cannot punish entries consistently.

**Unfavorable:** heavily armored animals, very large solitary predators in one-on-one combat, large horned/hoofed ungulates with decisive counterstrikes, and opponents whose mass makes an 18 kg individual's bites insufficient for rapid incapacitation.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits

### Special abilities

1. **Whistle Relay** — Far-carrying whistle/contact calls help separated pack members maintain cohesion in dense habitat, improving coordinated pursuit and regrouping.
2. **Relay Pursuit** — Cooperative chases let pack members pressure, flank and repeatedly re-engage prey, converting moderate individual size into high group hunting efficiency.

### Unique traits

1. **Forty-Tooth Shearing Set** — Dholes have 40 teeth and a distinctive lower carnassial configuration, an unusual canid specialization associated with efficient meat shearing.
2. **Pack-First Provisioning** — Cooperative breeding includes adults provisioning pups and mothers, reflecting unusually strong pack integration that supports group persistence.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range

Historically the dhole occupied a huge portion of Asia across alpine, temperate, tropical and subtropical systems. Current populations are fragmented mainly across South and Southeast Asia and parts of China. Cornell's recent niche-model work notes confinement to roughly a quarter of historical range and strong dependence on protected landscapes. Habitats include tropical dry and moist deciduous forest, evergreen/semi-evergreen forest, thorn forest, scrub/grassland mosaics and high-elevation steppe, provided prey and cover are sufficient.

### Diet and ecology

Dholes are primarily carnivorous, especially predators of medium-sized ungulates. The prey base varies regionally. The large 2014 synthesis emphasized sambar, chital and wild boar, while 2020 Laos/Cambodia work found muntjac dominant. This conflict is ecological rather than necessarily methodological: prey availability and habitat differ across the species' range.

### Social structure and life history

Dholes live in social packs/clans with cooperative hunting and pup care. Pack size is fluid rather than a fixed number. Mudumalai packs varied from 4 to 18 and 4 to 25. ADW reports gestation around 60–62 days and sexual maturity at about one year, with subordinate pack members helping feed the breeding female and pups.

### Conservation

The dhole is **Endangered**. Cornell reports severe historical range contraction, small isolated populations and threats including habitat loss/fragmentation, persecution, prey depletion, competition and disease. A recent Cornell-linked model summarizes a current estimate around **2,000–2,200 adults**, while older IUCN-era figures differ, so population numbers should be dated rather than treated as timeless constants.

### Major adaptations

- Lean cursorial body for sustained terrestrial travel.
- Cooperative hunting and flexible pack organization.
- Distinctive whistle/contact-call system in dense habitats.
- Specialized 40-tooth carnassial arrangement.
- Broad ecological flexibility across Asian forest and forest-mosaic systems.

### Human interaction

Dholes generally avoid people, but livestock conflict, persecution and domestic-dog disease transmission remain conservation issues. Their dependence on large connected landscapes and wild prey makes human land-use change especially consequential.

### Genuine fun facts

- The dhole is the only living species in the genus *Cuon*.
- It is often called the **whistling dog** because of its distinctive contact calls.
- Its 40-tooth dentition differs from the usual 42-tooth pattern of most canids.
- A four-year Mudumalai study observed pack membership fluctuating dramatically rather than staying fixed.
- A modern prey synthesis pooled 8,816 prey records, showing how extensively dhole diet has been studied across multiple Asian landscapes.
- GPS work in Thailand found adult males traveling almost twice as far per day as females in that study.

### Concise site-ready summary

The dhole is an Endangered Asian pack-hunting canid built around endurance, coordination and repeated tearing attacks rather than overwhelming individual force. A mature male weighs roughly 18 kg, yet packs can exploit much larger ungulate prey through pursuit, flanking and whistle-assisted cohesion. Individually it is lightly protected and vulnerable to larger predators, so its strongest ABS advantages are agility, stamina and tactics rather than raw power.

### Rich narrative profile

A dhole is dangerous for reasons that are easy to score incorrectly. It is not a miniature wolf with magically amplified strength, and a lone 18 kg animal cannot borrow the combined force of a pack. Its specialization is systemic: efficient cursorial movement keeps pressure on prey, contact calls help maintain social cohesion in forest, and multiple hunters can create openings that no one animal could force alone. This makes the species a useful ABS calibration test. Raw Power and Defense must remain modest on an absolute roster, while Endurance, Maneuverability and Tactics can legitimately be very high.

Its ecology also resists one-number simplification. Indian studies emphasize sambar and chital, whereas Southeast Asian work can find muntjac dominant. Pack sizes fluctuate, home ranges vary with sex and landscape, and current populations survive across fragmented ecological settings. The proposed profile therefore treats the canonical male as representative rather than exceptional and separates directly measured movement from weaker maximum-speed claims.

## 11. Confidence, conflicts and future field proposals

**High confidence:** taxonomy; male-larger tendency; approximate adult male mass; pack hunting; ungulate-centered diet; sociality; endurance-oriented ranging; 40-tooth specialization; Endangered status.

**Moderate confidence:** exact canonical shoulder/body length across geographic populations; 55 km/h maximum; 12.5-year canonical lifespan.

**Low/unsupported:** any claimed bite-force PSI; exact maximum sprint performance from controlled measurement; a universal prey-weight preference across all landscapes.

Conflicts are retained rather than flattened. ADW's 17–21 kg range differs from modern sex-separated 15–20 kg male / 10–13 kg female summaries. Prey studies differ between India-wide synthesis and Southeast Asian landscapes. These differences likely reflect sampling, sex and ecology rather than one source simply being wrong.

### Future field proposals

1. Instrument adult males and females with high-rate GPS/IMU collars during natural pursuits to separate maximum sprint speed, acceleration, turning and sustained pursuit velocity.
2. Obtain ethical in-vivo bite-force measurements in rehabilitation/husbandry contexts and report force in newtons; derive pressure only if tooth contact area is measured.
3. Quantify recovery after natural chase bouts using heart-rate/body-temperature biologgers.
4. Test whistle transmission and localization in different vegetation densities to quantify the coordination benefit.
5. Build a sex- and region-stratified morphometric dataset so canonical mass/length values can be updated without conflating populations.

## 12. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Coyote:** Dhole is somewhat heavier and more specialized for cooperative pursuit. Attack 42.0 vs Coyote 38.0 is a modest gap, not a pack-size multiplier. Stamina and Tactics are higher because species-specific movement and cooperative-predation evidence is stronger.
- **Versus Cougar:** Cougar remains substantially higher in Attack and individual lethality. A dhole pack may challenge larger carnivores, but the ABS canonical individual must not receive pack-level raw power.
- **Versus African Wild Dog / Gray Wolf:** final fine-grained ordering should be revisited once all three are normalized from equivalent evidence. Do not assume similar social hunting implies equal mass, bite, stamina or durability.
- **Versus Crow:** Dhole's social tactics are strong but do not justify crow-level experimental cognition. Intelligence 78.0 preserves that distinction.
- **Inflation check:** no physical score is raised because dholes can collectively attack prey many times one individual's mass. The group effect is concentrated in Tactics/Special and described explicitly as context-dependent.
- **Double-counting check:** the 55 km/h value informs Speed; Agility is based on maneuvering/repositioning; Stamina is based on sustained movement and pursuit ecology. These are not treated as interchangeable.
