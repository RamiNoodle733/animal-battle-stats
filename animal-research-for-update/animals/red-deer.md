# Red Deer (*Cervus elaphus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Red deer (male: stag or hart; female: hind)
- **Scientific name:** *Cervus elaphus* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the red deer of Europe, the Caucasus, Anatolia, Iran and the Atlas Mountains, plus introduced herds elsewhere. It is not the North American elk or wapiti (*Cervus canadensis*), which genetic evidence treats as a separate species and which the site already covers.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature stag in hard antler of about **180 kg**, **120 cm** at the shoulder and **210 cm** head-body length. This represents a well-fed continental European stag; it is above typical Scottish hill and Iberian stags and well below the heaviest Carpathian and Caspian animals.
- **Sex choice:** stags are much larger than hinds (hinds about 120–170 kg in continental summaries, 63–120 kg in Britain), carry the antlers and do the fighting.
- **Population variation:** body size varies more than in almost any other deer. Corsican red deer weigh about 80–100 kg, deer in poor habitats as little as 53–112 kg, British stags 90–190 kg, wild adult Iberian stags about 114–151 kg, and continental stags typically 160–240 kg. Claims of Carpathian stags up to 500 kg are exceptional and not used.

- **Antlers:** stags only, regrown every year and cast in March or April. Typically about **71 cm** long and **1 kg**, large sets up to **115 cm** and **5 kg**; growth can reach **2.5 cm a day** in velvet. Larger stags form a "crown" of tines at the top; UK stags commonly reach up to 16 points.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Autumn stags grow a neck mane and thicker neck muscles, which help absorb antler pushes.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
A stag's weapons are branched antlers backed by a heavy, maned neck. Rut fights are pushing and twisting contests with locked antlers until the weaker animal breaks away, and the tines can wound: around one-fifth of rutting males show signs of injury. Against predators, antlers and hard forward kicks with the front hooves are used by both sexes; large hinds make a stand and strike with their forelegs. The antlers are carried for more than half the year, from late summer to early spring, so stags are unarmed for part of the year.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Long-legged and agile, living in forest, open moor and mountain country. There is no reliable top-speed measurement.

### Endurance
During the rut, harem-holding stags rarely feed and can lose up to 20% of their body weight while roaring, herding hinds and fighting for weeks. Stags that start the rut in poor condition are less likely to last through the peak of the breeding season. This shows sustained effort, but also a real fatigue cost.

### Senses
Herds keep one or more members watching for danger while others eat and rest, and hinds bark when alarmed. Hearing and smell are presumed keen, as in other deer, but no quantitative sensory data were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Stags assess rivals before fighting. Roaring comes first, and deeper roars (lower formant frequencies) signal a larger body; if neither backs down, the two walk in parallel to size each other up, and only then might they fight. Dark ventral hair patches also signal rutting effort. Out of the rut, stags without antlers gather in bachelor groups, and hinds use constant vocal contact to keep calves close.

### Sociality and hunting
Mostly single-sex groups outside the rut; females form herds of up to 50 after the rut. Grazes grasses and dwarf shrubs such as heather and bilberry, taking tree shoots and other woody browse mainly in winter.

### Fighting and aggression
The rut runs from late September into November in Britain. Stags roar, thrash vegetation and fight for access to hinds, holding harems of up to about 20. Breeding success peaks at about eight years of age.

### Predator defense
Aside from people and dogs, the grey wolf is the most dangerous predator, with brown bears occasionally taking red deer and lynx taking some. In the Dinaric Mountains, red deer appeared in 96% of wolf scats, and wolf predation was estimated to cause 7.8% red deer mortality, falling mostly on young animals. Studies in Hungary found wolves favour calves and weakened animals, and stags are less sensitive to predator presence than hinds with calves.

### Environmental strengths
- Mixed woodland, forest edges and glades.
- Open moor and mountain slopes with room to move and watch.
- Cool, temperate climates.

### Weaknesses
- No armor; the whole body is soft target apart from the skull and antlers.
- Antlers are absent from spring to late summer.
- Rut exhaustion and weight loss leave stags weaker afterward.
- Far lighter than the roster's large bovids and moose.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** medium predators such as a single wolf or lynx facing a healthy stag in hard antler; smaller herbivores.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized antelopes and deer; a lone big cat of similar mass.
- **Bad:** bears, tigers and lion-sized cats; wolf packs; heavy bovids, moose and elk.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Roar and Parallel Walk** — stags size up rivals first by roaring, where deeper roars mean a bigger body, then by walking side by side; many contests end without contact.
2. **Crowned Antler Clash** — branched antlers up to about 115 cm, often crowned with a cup of tines, lock with a rival's for pushing and twisting fights backed by a maned neck.

### Unique traits
1. **Africa's Only Deer** — through the Barbary stag of the Atlas Mountains, the red deer is the only deer species living wild in Africa.
2. **Fast-Growing Bone** — antlers regrow every year and can lengthen by about 2.5 cm a day while in velvet.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Most of Europe, the Caucasus, Anatolia, Iran, parts of western Asia and the Atlas Mountains of North Africa, in woodland, forest edges, moorland and mountains. Introduced to Australia, New Zealand, Argentina, Chile and other countries; in some of these it is treated as a pest, and it is listed among the world's 100 worst invasive species.

### Diet and ecology
Grazes grasses and dwarf shrubs such as heather and bilberry and takes woody browse such as tree shoots when other food is short in winter. Red deer are the main prey of wolves across much of central and eastern Europe and a key large herbivore in forest and moorland systems.

### Social structure
Single-sex groups for most of the year; hind herds of up to 50 after the rut, with calves kept close by constant calls. Stags form bachelor groups once antlers are cast. In the rut, mature stags hold harems.

### Reproduction and life history
Hinds mature at about two years. Gestation is 240–262 days, producing one calf of about 15 kg that joins the herd after two weeks and is weaned at about two months. Calves stay with their mothers for almost a year. Stags reach peak breeding success at about eight years.

### Conservation
Least Concern and widespread. Populations have grown in much of Europe: France's rose from about 30,000 in 1970 to about 160,000 in 2014, while North African populations have continued to decline. Reintroductions have restored red deer in parts of Portugal and the United Kingdom.

### Special features
- Branched antlers, typically 71 cm and 1 kg, up to about 115 cm and 5 kg.
- Antler growth up to 2.5 cm per day.
- Autumn neck mane in stags of many populations.
- Deep rutting roar that signals body size.
- Sharp front hooves used for forward kicks by both sexes.

### Human interaction
The most prestigious quarry of medieval hunting and still a major game species; farmed for venison and antler velvet in New Zealand, China and Russia. It is the largest wild land mammal in Ireland and the United Kingdom. Red deer appear in European cave art up to about 40,000 years old.

### Genuine fun facts
- A red deer stag's antlers can grow about 2.5 cm in a single day.
- The Barbary stag makes the red deer the only deer native to Africa.
- Stags that hold harems barely eat during the rut and can lose a fifth of their body weight.
- Deeper roars signal bigger stags, and rivals use them to decide whether to fight.
- Red deer are the largest wild land mammals in Britain and Ireland.
- Around one-fifth of rutting stags show signs of injury from fights.
- France's red deer population grew more than five-fold between 1970 and 2014.

### Concise site-ready summary
The red deer is Europe's classic antlered stag, a fast, agile 180 kg forest and mountain deer whose crowned antlers can reach over a metre. Stags size each other up by roaring before they lock antlers, and although their autumn fights are fierce, the lack of armor and a lighter frame keep them below elk and moose in raw fighting power.

### Rich narrative profile
A mature red deer stag in autumn is one of Europe's great wildlife sights: a russet-brown body of around 180 kg, a shaggy neck mane, and a spread of branching antlers that may measure more than a metre and end in a crown of tines. He roars across the glen at dawn and dusk, and the pitch of that roar tells other stags how big he is. When two evenly matched stags meet, they walk side by side to size each other up before crashing antlers together in a pushing, twisting struggle.

The rut is exhausting. Harem-holders stop eating, lose up to a fifth of their weight and pick up wounds; about one stag in five shows signs of injury. For the rest of the year red deer live in single-sex groups, keeping sentinels on watch and relying on speed and agility to escape wolves, the predator that kills the most calves and weakened adults in central and eastern Europe.

On the ABS scale the red deer is a mid-weight, mobile fighter. It sits between the reindeer and the North American elk in size and Attack, with antlers that make it dangerous in close quarters but no armor to take heavy blows. Its best assets are agility, senses and the ritualized assessment that lets stags avoid fights they would lose.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** regional means range from about 100 kg (Scottish hill stags) to over 200 kg (continental); 180 kg is a roster representative, not any single population's mean.
- **Taxonomy:** older sources, including ADW, lump elk with red deer; elk weights are excluded here.
- **Speed:** no reliable measurement; popular figures rejected.
- **Lifespan:** the 10–13 year wild span lacks a primary citation.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Elk (320 kg; A62 D47 Ag59):** the elk is nearly twice as heavy with larger antlers, so it keeps higher Size, Raw Power, Attack and Defense; the red deer gets higher Agility (65.0 vs 59.0) as the lighter, more nimble forest deer.
- **Versus Reindeer (170 kg; A55 D46 St93 Sp80):** similar mass. The red deer's heavier, crowned antlers give slightly higher Attack (57.0 vs 55.0), while the reindeer keeps far higher Stamina and Special for its migration endurance and cold-climate features, and slightly higher Defense for its dense coat.
- **Versus Moose (550 kg; A69 D50):** the moose is three times heavier and remains well ahead in Attack, Size and Raw Power; the red deer is more agile.
- **Versus Gray Wolf (50 kg; A55 D41):** a healthy stag in hard antler matches a single wolf in Attack and Defense, but wolf packs, which the red deer faces in reality, are its main natural killer.
