# Tarantula Hawk (*Pepsis grossa*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Tarantula hawk (tarantula hawk wasp)
- **Scientific name:** *Pepsis grossa* (Fabricius, 1798), used as the reference species for the genus *Pepsis* (Pompilidae: Pepsinae). Until C.R. Vardy's 2002 revision this species was known as *Pepsis formosa*, including the form *P. formosa pattoni*.
- **Exact animal represented:** a large New World tarantula hawk of the genus *Pepsis*. The live site photo shows an unidentified *Pepsis* sp. photographed near Bogotá, Colombia, walking in active search of spiders. *P. grossa* is the best-studied large species (sting, venom, hunting and aposematism) and its range reaches northern South America, so it serves as the measurement and behavior reference. Old World *Hemipepsis* tarantula hawks are not the represented animal.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult female *P. grossa*, **about 4.5 cm body length and about 1 g**. Only females hunt and only females carry a sting.
- **Sex choice:** females are both larger and the only armed sex. Wikipedia (citing Vardy 2002) gives female body length 30–51 mm versus 24–40 mm for males. The NPS Grand Canyon species page notes that males have no stinger and that females have curled antennae while males have straight ones.
- **Population and species notes:** *Pepsis* is a large genus (at least 250 species in South America alone), and many species are hard to tell apart. *P. grossa* has orange-winged (xanthic), black-winged (melanic) and, in northern South America, dark-based amber-winged forms. *P. thisbe* is a similar common species in the southwestern United States and supplies much of the published field biology.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- No species-specific published mass for *P. grossa* was located.
- Godfrey et al. (2021, Proceedings B) weighed 39 hymenopteran species on an analytical balance; their sample spanned body masses from 0.22 mg to **more than 900 mg**, and *Pepsis thisbe* was the largest species in the sample.
- **Canonical `weight_kg`: 0.001 (about 1 g)** for a large adult female. This rounds the >0.9 g large-*Pepsis* measurement and is not a population mean.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

- Genus maximum about 6.5 cm (Wikipedia, Tarantula hawk).
- NPS: *P. thisbe* at Grand Canyon grows up to about 2 in (about 5 cm).
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 4.5** for a representative large female, below the 5.1 cm species maximum.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Speed
- No instrumented flight or running speed for any *Pepsis* species was located. Females fly low over the ground when searching and also hunt on foot. **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement, not "motionless").

### Lifespan
- Schmidt (2004) concludes that tarantula hawks have long adult lives that are nearly free of predation, but the abstract gives no numeric longevity. Punzo (1994) recorded 41 days from egg to emerged adult for *P. thisbe* under laboratory conditions.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 0.0**, because no defensible numeric adult or whole-life longevity was located.

### Sting and venom
- **Sting length:** NPS gives a 1/4 in (about 7 mm) sting for female *P. thisbe*. Wikipedia (citing Starr 1985 and Schmidt et al.) gives up to 12 mm for female *P. grossa*. Wilson et al. (2018, PeerJ) dissected a *Pepsis* sp. sting of **14.33 mm**, the longest actual sting of the 39 non-velvet-ant wasps they measured; relative to its 14.35 mm mesosoma the sting ratio was about 1.0.
- **Pain:** rated 4 of 4 on the Schmidt sting pain index; intense pain typically lasts about five minutes (Wikipedia, citing a New York Times feature quoting Schmidt).
- **Vertebrate toxicity:** Schmidt (2004) gives mouse LD50 values of **65 mg/kg** for *P. formosa pattoni* (now *P. grossa*) and **120 mg/kg** for *P. thisbe*, and states the defensive value rests entirely on pain. Schmidt (2019, Toxins) tabulates 90 mg/kg for *P. grossa* and 120 mg/kg for *P. thisbe*. By comparison the bullet ant in the same table is 1.4 mg/kg.
- **Venom volume:** a milking study of the related *Hemipepsis ustulata* collected droplets of about 1 microlitre, with 4–8 droplets obtainable (White 2016).
- **Effect on spiders:** large Pepsinae such as *Pepsis* can keep spiders immobilized for months; tarantulas in one study survived eight months of paralysis (Toxins 2021 review).
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** The mandibles are used for digging and handling; no force measurement exists.

### Defensive structure
- Chitinous exoskeleton with long, hooked-clawed legs. No measurement of cuticle strength was located. Defense relies on flight, warning coloration and odor, and the sting.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The primary weapon is the female sting: a long, curved lancet (7–14 mm in published measurements) backed by a large venom supply. Against arthropods, especially tarantulas, the venom is a precision paralytic. Punzo (1994) found that more than 80% of stings on *P. thisbe* prey went through the soft membrane between the sternum and the base of the first leg, straight toward the spider's central nerve mass. The result is paralysis that can last months, which is decisive in any arthropod matchup.

Against vertebrates the same sting is a pain weapon rather than a killing weapon. Its lethality to mice (65–120 mg/kg) is among the lowest of all stinging insects, so a 1 g wasp cannot deliver a toxic dose to a large animal. What it can deliver is some of the most intense pain recorded for any insect. The mandibles and hooked claws grapple and drag prey but have negligible absolute force.

### Hunting sequence
Wikipedia's *P. grossa* account (citing field studies) describes the typical sequence: the female locates an occupied burrow by vision or scent, enters or provokes the spider until it leaves, grooms, then uses her antennae to make the spider rear into its threat posture. She seizes a leg, stings between the leg base and the sternum, and then drags the paralyzed spider to a burrow, lays one egg on it and seals the chamber. Punzo found that the time a female needed to approach and paralyze a spider fell significantly as she gained experience.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Tarantula hawks fly strongly, search in low flights just above the ground, and also walk rapidly with jerky, wing-flicking movements. They must close with a tarantula while avoiding its fangs, then drag a spider many times their own mass. No flight speed or maneuver data were located, so agility is scored from behavior and body plan, conservatively below the hornet's hovering flight.

### Endurance
Females may dig a burrow, search at length and drag heavy prey overland. Schmidt (2004) reports long adult lives. Punzo recorded a bimodal daily activity pattern in *P. thisbe*, with hunting peaks at 08:00–09:59 and 16:00–17:59 and a lull when air temperature exceeded 38 °C. Sustained output is therefore real but heat-limited and small in absolute terms.

### Senses
Large compound eyes and antennae guide prey location; antennation is a fixed step in the hunting sequence. The species may detect occupied burrows by the scent of the silk curtain at the entrance.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior is largely a fixed sequence, but it is precise and it improves with experience (Punzo 1994). Females choose larger spiders for fertilized (female) eggs and smaller ones for unfertilized (male) eggs. Males defend hilltop perches for hours while waiting for females. *P. grossa* also forms mixed-species, mixed-sex aggregations that Schmidt interprets as defensive.

### Fighting and aggression
Tarantula hawks are described as docile toward people and rarely sting without provocation. Toward tarantulas the female is a committed attacker that routinely engages a much heavier armed opponent.

### Predator defense
Schmidt (2004) states that the pain confers near absolute protection from vertebrate predators, supported by warning coloration, a strong odor and a large mimicry complex (flies, beetles, moths, grasshoppers and other wasps copy them). Recorded predators are few: greater roadrunners, kingbirds and the American bullfrog. Roadrunners beat the wasp against the ground before eating it.

### Environmental strengths
- Open, sunny deserts and dry scrub where tarantulas burrow.
- Three-dimensional escape by flight.
- Warm conditions, though extreme midday heat suppresses activity.

### Weaknesses
- About 1 g of body mass: negligible absolute force against any vertebrate.
- Venom has very low vertebrate toxicity; a large animal suffers pain, not disablement.
- A clean bite, peck or crush ends the fight.
- Males are unarmed.
- Activity drops in extreme heat and in cold.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** tarantulas and other large spiders; most arthropods of similar or greater mass that can be stung at a nerve center.
- **Mixed:** heavily armored arthropods without an exposed soft membrane.
- **Bad:** virtually every vertebrate in a sustained fight. The sting can make a small predator break off, but it cannot win by damage.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Nerve-Center Sting** — the female drives her long sting through the soft membrane between a spider's sternum and first leg (over 80% of recorded stings in *P. thisbe*), producing paralysis that can last months. Against arthropods this ends the fight in one strike.
2. **Blinding Pain Venom** — a sting rated 4 of 4 on the Schmidt pain index that incapacitates people for minutes while carrying very low lethality (mouse LD50 65–120 mg/kg). Against vertebrates it is a deterrent that can force a disengagement, not a killing weapon.

### Unique traits
1. **Tarantula Specialist Parasitoid** — each female hunts and paralyzes tarantulas to feed a single larva, routinely overpowering an armed spider far heavier than herself.
2. **Warning-Color Immunity** — metallic blue-black body, bright orange wings and a strong odor advertise the sting so well that few animals eat tarantula hawks, and many harmless insects mimic them.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
*Pepsis* is confined to the Americas, from Utah and Kansas south to Argentina, with at least 250 species in South America. In the United States tarantula hawks are most common in the southwestern deserts. *P. grossa* ranges from the southern United States through Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean to northern South America. Tarantula hawks as a group (including *Hemipepsis*) occur on every continent except Europe and Antarctica.

### Diet and ecology
Adults drink nectar. In Big Bend Ranch State Park, Texas, four plants supplied 73.6% of adult *P. grossa* feeding: two milkweeds, Mexican buckeye and honey mesquite, and the wasps are important milkweed pollinators. Larvae are carnivorous parasitoids: each develops on a single paralyzed tarantula, feeding while avoiding vital organs for as long as possible. In Texas the preferred prey of *P. grossa* is *Aphonopelma hentzi*.

### Social structure
Solitary hunters. Males defend hilltop perches to meet females, and *P. grossa* forms mixed-species, mixed-sex aggregations that appear defensive and may help in finding resources and mates.

### Reproduction and life history
A female lays one egg on each paralyzed spider in a sealed burrow. Fertilized eggs become females and are usually placed on larger (often female) spiders; unfertilized eggs become males. The larva feeds for weeks, spins a cocoon and pupates. Punzo recorded 41 days from egg to adult for *P. thisbe* in the laboratory, and eggs and larvae died under very dry (10% relative humidity) conditions.

### Conservation
No IUCN Red List assessment of *P. grossa* was located, so it is treated here as Not Evaluated. The species is widespread and common in parts of its range. Its prey tarantulas and its nectar plants are its main ecological dependencies.

### Special features
- Long, curved sting with a large venom supply.
- Paralytic venom able to immobilize spiders for months.
- Hooked tarsal claws for grappling and dragging prey.
- Aposematic coloration and odor.
- Female-only sting; males are harmless.

### Human interaction
Stings happen almost only when a wasp is handled or stepped on. Aside from allergic reactions, the sting is not medically dangerous, and redness can last up to a week after the pain fades. In 1989 New Mexico schoolchildren voted *P. formosa* (now *P. grossa*) the official state insect.

### Genuine fun facts
- The tarantula hawk's sting is rated 4 on the Schmidt pain index, level with the bullet ant, yet its venom is roughly 45 to 65 times less lethal to mice (LD50 65–90 versus 1.4 mg/kg).
- Entomologist Justin Schmidt described the pain as "blinding, fierce, shockingly electric".
- One *Pepsis* sting dissected in a 2018 study measured 14.33 mm, the longest actual sting of 39 non-velvet-ant wasps measured.
- Tarantulas paralyzed by large spider wasps have survived eight months of paralysis.
- Only females sting; males spend their days guarding hilltop perches.
- The greater roadrunner is one of the few animals that eats them, beating each wasp against the ground first.
- Many flies, beetles and moths survive by copying the tarantula hawk's orange-and-blue warning colors.

### Concise site-ready summary
The tarantula hawk is a 1 g spider wasp whose female hunts tarantulas many times her size, paralyzing them with a precise sting to feed her larva. Its sting ranks among the most painful of any insect but is barely toxic to vertebrates, so it dominates arthropod matchups and deters predators while doing little real damage to large animals.

### Rich narrative profile
The tarantula hawk is a specialist built around one weapon. A female *Pepsis grossa* is about 4.5 cm long and weighs roughly a gram, yet her whole life's work is hunting tarantulas that can outweigh her many times over. She finds an occupied burrow by sight or scent, drives or provokes the spider into the open, and makes it rear into its threat posture. Then she seizes a leg and slips her long sting into the soft membrane beside the first leg, the route taken in more than eight of ten recorded attacks. The spider may stay paralyzed for months while her single larva feeds on it.

That precision is why the wasp tops the roster's small-animal Special tier. In an arthropod matchup, one clean sting ends the contest, and field work shows that experienced females complete the approach and paralysis faster than novices. The same sting doubles as a defensive shield. Justin Schmidt rated its pain at the top of his scale, and he found that the pain alone gives the wasp near-complete immunity from vertebrate predators: bright warning colors, a strong smell and a crowd of mimics reinforce the message.

The limits are just as clear. The venom is among the least lethal of all stinging insects, with mouse LD50 values of 65–120 mg/kg, and a 1 g body cannot overpower anything that is not an arthropod. A lizard, bird or mammal that ignores the pain can crush the wasp in one bite. On the absolute roster scale the tarantula hawk therefore keeps a very low Attack and Defense, capable Agility, and one of the highest Special scores among tiny animals.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** no direct *P. grossa* mass was found; 1 g is anchored to the >900 mg largest-*Pepsis* measurement and could be off by a few tenths of a gram.
- **LD50:** 65 mg/kg (Schmidt 2004, Wilson et al. 2018) versus 90 mg/kg (Schmidt 2019 table) for the same species; both confirm very low vertebrate lethality.
- **Sting length:** 7 mm (NPS, *P. thisbe*), up to 12 mm (*P. grossa*), 14.33 mm (one *Pepsis* sp.) reflect species and measurement differences.
- **Species identity:** the site photo is a Colombian *Pepsis* sp.; the report uses *P. grossa* as the reference species by instruction and because its range reaches Colombia.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hornet (A10.0 D5.0 Ag79.0 St39.0 I43.0 Sp76.0):** the tarantula hawk is heavier, with a sting roughly two to three times longer, so it sits slightly above the hornet in Attack and Defense. The hornet keeps higher Agility (hovering flight) and Intelligence (eusocial coordination). Special is higher for the tarantula hawk because its pain rating and long paralysis outclass the hornet's sting, even though hornet venom is more lethal to mammals.
- **Versus Bullet Ant (A18.0 D2.0 Ag62.0 St32.0 I44.0 Sp96.0):** both share the top pain rating, but the bullet ant's venom is about 50 times more lethal to mice (1.4 versus 65–90 mg/kg) and its pain lasts far longer. The bullet ant therefore keeps higher Attack and Special; the tarantula hawk gets higher Agility (flight), Stamina and Defense from its larger body.
- **Versus Goliath Birdeater (A16.0 D12.0 Ag61.0 St35.0 I31.0 Sp90.0):** in real life tarantula hawks prey on tarantulas, so the wasp is the favored side of this archetype. That edge comes from Agility, sting placement and paralysis, not from absolute damage: a 150 g spider with 2 cm fangs has more raw damage potential against a vertebrate, so it keeps slightly higher Attack and Defense while the wasp is higher in Agility, Stamina and Intelligence. The two Special scores sit close together (88.0 versus 90.0).
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholder values are not used. A famous pain sting does not justify double-digit Defense or a vertebrate-scale Attack.
