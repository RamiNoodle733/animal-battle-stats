# Guanaco (*Lama guanicoe*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Guanaco
- **Scientific name:** *Lama guanicoe* (Müller, 1776)
- **Site taxon:** *Lama guanicoe*, matching the current roster schema.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature **territorial male**, **120.0 kg**, **115.0 cm shoulder height**, **200.0 cm total length**. Guanacos show little or no consistent body-size sexual dimorphism, but mature territorial males are the primary fighting sex and possess significantly enlarged canines.
- **Population variation:** adults span about 90-140 kg and 90-130 cm at the shoulder, with smaller northern and larger southern populations. Canonical values represent a robust southern/Patagonian adult without using a record individual.

## 2. Physical measurements and ranges

Animal Diversity Web synthesizes adult mass at **90-140 kg**, shoulder height **90-130 cm**, and length **190-215 cm**. A 2023 morphometric study of five adult Peruvian guanacos estimated mean live mass **109 ± 13.8 kg**, showing that smaller northern animals fit inside the broader range. ADW reports no meaningful sex difference in overall size or coloration, but males have significantly enlarged canines.

### Natural weapons
- **Male canines:** enlarged relative to females and explicitly associated with injurious male-male combat. No defensible standardized canine-length value was found, so none is fabricated.
- **Bite:** males bite opponents at the legs, hindquarters and neck during fights.
- **Kicks/charges:** long-legged ungulate kicking and charging are meaningful secondary weapons. Maternal guanacos use threatening, charging and kicking against predators.
- **Chest and neck wrestling:** territorial males ram chests and wrestle with their necks during contests.
- **Spitting:** can reach about 2 m according to the ADW synthesis. It is primarily a disruption/social weapon, not a major tissue-damage mechanism.

### Defenses
The guanaco has no armor, horns or antlers. Defense is based on alertness, group vigilance, running escape, long-legged kicking, social warning, and tolerance of cold, dry and high-elevation environments. Cooperative vigilance can detect predators earlier, but it is not structural protection.

## 4. Combat biology

### Offense and fighting
Guanacos are not passive livestock analogues. Mature males compete for resource-defense territories and breeding access. Documented aggressive behavior includes threats, pursuit, biting at legs/hindquarters/neck, chest ramming, neck wrestling and spitting. Wounds to the neck, legs and body are commonly observed in territorial males, establishing that contests can be genuinely injurious.

The main damaging tools are teeth and kicks rather than a specialized horn or antler system. A territorial male's enlarged canines improve close-range weaponry, but a roughly 120 kg camelid remains far below large carnivores and megafauna in absolute damage output.

### Locomotion and agility
Long legs and an open-country body plan favor fast escape, balance and economical terrestrial travel. Guanacos occupy plains, shrublands and mountain terrain. Agility is therefore good for a medium-large ungulate, but no unverified maximum sprint is allowed to inflate the score. Close wrestling also shows adequate balance and body control.

### Endurance
Populations may be sedentary or migratory. In La Payunia, more than 15,000 guanacos participate in the longest known ungulate migration in South America. Territorial males also spend about 65% of observed activity time foraging during the territorial season. These facts support strong sustained field endurance, but not the live site's near-ceiling 91.7 without direct physiological justification.

### Senses and antipredator behavior
Vision, hearing and olfaction support open-country vigilance. A peer-reviewed antipredator study concluded that grouping improves predator avoidance chiefly through **cooperative vigilance**, increasing the probability of detecting an approaching predator early. Terrain and vegetation structure alter flight decisions.

### Intelligence and tactics
The species has a structured and flexible social system: family groups led by territorial males, solitary territorial males, bachelor groups, female groups and mixed non-territorial aggregations. Territorial males show strong site fidelity, with a 10-year Chilean study finding 73% of tracked males returned to the same territory location between years. This supports spatial memory and context-sensitive social behavior, but not primate/corvid-level problem solving.

### Predation and predator defense
Pumas are major predators. During the severe 1995 winter in Torres del Paine, 74% of 45 located carcasses were puma kills, yet adult mortality remained surprisingly low at about 1% for males and 2% for females in that sample. Other Patagonian work has found puma prey selection can vary by site and can favor males. Adults therefore possess effective vigilance/escape defenses without being predator-proof.

Mothers can threaten, spit, charge and kick predators, and maternal aggressiveness is associated with offspring survival. The species' best defense against a large predator is early detection plus escape, not standing combat.

### Environmental strengths
- Open steppe and slopes where sight lines favor early predator detection.
- Cold, arid and high-elevation environments where guanacos are physiologically adapted.
- Group contexts where cooperative vigilance improves detection.
- Broken terrain where sure-footed movement can aid escape.

### Environmental weaknesses
- Dense cover shortens warning distance and helps ambush predators.
- No armor or heavy cranial weapon.
- Close grappling against a large carnivore is dangerous.
- Long limbs are vulnerable to bites and destabilization.
- Fences are an anthropogenic entanglement hazard documented in mortality studies.

### Matchup archetypes
**Strong against:** smaller unarmored terrestrial animals that must enter kick/bite range; similarly sized herbivores lacking comparable mobility or willingness to contest; opponents vulnerable to repeated kicks and displacement.

**Weak against:** large ambush carnivores, heavily armored animals, horned/tusked ungulates with superior penetration/reach, and much larger opponents able to absorb kicks and dominate a clinch.

## 7. Abilities and traits

### Special abilities
1. **Canine Clinch** - mature territorial males use enlarged canines while biting an opponent's legs, hindquarters and neck during close contests, adding cutting injury to chest and neck wrestling.
2. **Cooperative Watchline** - social groups improve predator detection through shared vigilance, allowing earlier flight or defensive response when companions are present.

### Unique traits
1. **Territorial Veteran** - mature males repeatedly defend feeding territories, with strong year-to-year site fidelity and a documented repertoire of escalating aggressive behaviors.
2. **Patagonian Hardliner** - physiology and behavior support survival and movement across cold, dry, windy, high-elevation and seasonally harsh South American landscapes.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and geographic range
Guanacos are native South American camelids occupying open and semi-open habitats from Peru through the Andes and Patagonian steppe to Tierra del Fuego. Populations use grassland, shrubland, semi-desert, mountain slopes and cold temperate environments. Body size varies geographically, with northern animals generally smaller than southern animals.

### Diet and ecology
They are herbivorous grazers and browsers consuming grasses, forbs and woody vegetation. As dominant native large herbivores in many Patagonian systems, they strongly influence vegetation and provide important prey biomass for pumas and scavengers.

### Social structure
The breeding system is resource-defense polygyny. A family group generally includes one territorial adult male, several females and offspring. Nonterritorial males can form bachelor groups, while solitary territorial males and large mixed aggregations also occur. Social organization varies with season, forage and migratory strategy.

### Reproduction and life history
Gestation is roughly 345-360 days and typically produces one precocial offspring, called a chulengo. Young can stand shortly after birth. Females reach sexual maturity around two years; males mature around two to four years but may not secure territories and breeding opportunities until later. Males commonly become territorial around four to six years in the ADW synthesis.

### Conservation
The species remains widespread compared with many large South American mammals, but historical hunting, livestock competition, fencing and habitat alteration have caused major local declines and fragmentation. Conservation context varies strongly by country and population, so site copy should avoid implying uniform abundance everywhere.

### Major adaptations
Long limbs, dense insulating coat, efficient herbivory, flexible migration, social vigilance and tolerance of arid/cold/high-altitude conditions allow guanacos to exploit environments ranging from Andean uplands to Patagonian steppe.

### Human interaction
Historically hunted for meat and hides and affected by competition with domestic livestock. Fences can cause direct mortality and fragment movement. Guanacos are also important conservation and tourism animals in protected Patagonian landscapes.

### Fun facts
- Guanacos are the wild South American camelid lineage from which domestic llamas derive.
- Adult males and females are similar in overall body size, but males carry enlarged combat canines.
- Territorial males may spit roughly two meters during aggressive encounters.
- A long-term Torres del Paine study found 73% of tracked territorial males returned to the same territory location between years.
- More than 15,000 guanacos participate in the La Payunia seasonal migration, described as the longest known ungulate migration in South America.
- Chulengos are highly precocial and may stand within minutes after birth.

### Concise site summary
A hardy wild South American camelid built for vigilance, distance travel and territorial combat. Mature males combine kicks, enlarged canines, ramming and neck wrestling with strong terrain awareness and social predator detection, but lack the armor and heavy weapons of the roster's top ungulate fighters.

### Rich narrative profile
The guanaco's combat reputation should sit between two misleading extremes. It is not a harmless, lightly built grazer, but it is also not a horned heavyweight. A mature territorial male spends years competing for space and reproductive access. Its fights can involve pursuit, chest impacts, neck wrestling and targeted bites to the legs, hindquarters and neck, with combat canines large enough that wounds are commonly observed on territorial males. Against smaller opponents, its height, long legs and kicking reach create meaningful danger.

Its strongest survival system operates before a fight begins. Guanacos use exposed terrain, alert senses and group vigilance to detect predators early. Their social organization is flexible, and territorial males can show remarkable fidelity to defended sites across years. In Patagonia, migration and environmental hardiness add a stamina advantage that should be reflected in ABS without turning low-cost long-distance survival into implausible near-maximal combat endurance.

The limiting factor is direct weapon quality. A guanaco has neither armor nor a horn, antler or tusk system. Once a puma or other powerful predator successfully closes and controls the body, the guanaco's options narrow sharply. Its ABS profile is therefore a mobile, enduring and tactically aware medium ungulate with credible close-range defenses, rather than a high-damage bruiser.

### Evidence conflicts and uncertainty
1. **Size:** broad 90-140 kg range reflects geographic variation. Canonical 120.0 kg is representative, not a record.
2. **Sex:** body size is not strongly dimorphic, but males have enlarged canines and conduct the main territorial fighting. Canonical male choice is behavioral/weapon-based rather than mass-based.
3. **Speed:** commonly repeated 55-56 km/h values were not supported strongly enough by a primary or instrumented source in this run, so `speed_mps` remains 0.0.
4. **Bite force:** no defensible PSI measurement found. The old 100 PSI is rejected.
5. **Lifespan:** 28 years is an upper typical wild value rather than an average age at death.
6. **Image:** exact species, high resolution and license are strong, but adulthood, sex and strict complete anatomy remain unverified.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- Guanaco's **Raw Power 37.0** must remain far below Gorilla (large great ape) and Grizzly Bear, and below heavily armed large bovids. Its athleticism does not justify pound-for-pound leakage.
- **Attack 38.0** recognizes enlarged male canines and kicking without treating a 120 kg unhorned herbivore as equivalent to Gray Wolf (55.0) or large predators.
- **Defense 35.0** stays below Gray Wolf (41.0) and far below Grizzly Bear (59.0). Avoidance is not armor.
- **Agility 63.0** can sit near Gray Wolf (66.0) because turning, balance and evasion are not pure size/power categories, but no unverified sprint number is used to drive it.
- **Stamina 78.0** is high due to migratory ecology but below Gray Wolf (89.0), whose sustained travel/pursuit evidence is stronger.
- **Intelligence 58.0** is appropriate for flexible ungulate social and territorial behavior while remaining well below Gorilla (89.0) and Gray Wolf (78.0).
- **Special 45.0** is deliberately modest. Cooperative vigilance and environmental hardiness are useful but not comparable to venom, electricity or extreme specialized sensory systems.
