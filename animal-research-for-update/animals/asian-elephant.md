# Asian Elephant (*Elephas maximus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Asian elephant (also Asiatic elephant)
- **Scientific name:** *Elephas maximus* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the Asian elephant species *Elephas maximus* across its mainland and island populations (Indian, Sri Lankan, Sumatran and Bornean forms), not the African savanna elephant *Loxodonta africana*, which has its own roster entry.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male (bull) of about **4,500 kg** and **275 cm** shoulder height, carrying tusks. This is a representative prime mainland bull, not a record captive or historical giant.
- **Sex choice:** males are much larger than females. ADW gives males 240–300 cm shoulder height and 3,500–6,000 kg, versus females at 195–240 cm and 2,000–3,500 kg. Only males carry true tusks (elongated second upper incisors); females have small tushes or none.
- **Population variation:** tusk possession varies sharply between populations. In Sri Lanka only about 7% of males carry tusks, and tuskless adult males (called *makhnas* in India) are common in some regions. The canonical bull is a tusker because tusks are a real part of the species' male fighting kit, but a makhna should be treated as a lower-Weaponry variant. Island forms such as the Bornean elephant are smaller than mainland bulls and should not define the canonical mass.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- ADW: males **3,500–6,000 kg**, females **2,000–3,500 kg**.
- Smithsonian's National Zoo: adults on average **2,750–5,420 kg** (6,000–12,000 lb). The Zoo's own bull, Spike, weighed **13,250 lb (about 6,010 kg)**, a well-fed captive male rather than a representative wild animal.
- **Canonical:** **4,500.0 kg** prime wild bull, inside both institutional ranges and below captive heavyweights.
- **Confidence:** High for the range, moderate for the single canonical value.

### Height
- ADW: males **240–300 cm** at the shoulder.
- National Zoo bull Spike: **10.5 ft (about 320 cm)** at the shoulder, a large captive individual.
- **Canonical:** **275.0 cm** shoulder height.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Length
- No head-body length figure was captured from an institutional source in this research pass. Commonly published head-body spans for large bulls fall around 5.5–6.4 m, excluding the tail.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 600.0 cm** head-body, provisional.
- **Confidence:** Low until a primary morphometric source is attached. Trunk and tail conventions differ between sources.

### Speed
- Hutchinson et al. (2003, *Nature*) timed **42 Asian elephants** over **188 trials** with photosensors and video, and recorded speeds up to **6.8 m/s (about 25 km/h)**. The gait keeps a walking footfall pattern with no aerial phase, but the centre of mass bounces in a way that meets a biomechanical definition of running.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 6.8 m/s.**
- **Confidence:** High; this is an instrumented measurement, unusual for a large mammal.

### Lifespan
- Myanmar timber-elephant records (semi-captive working animals) gave a median survival of about **42 years** in a large comparative study reported by National Geographic.
- Nature Communications (2018) found wild-caught Myanmar elephants had shorter median lifespans than captive-born ones, and a separate analysis predicted a median lifespan in optimal working conditions of about **30.8 years for males** versus **44.7 years for females**.
- Commonly quoted potential wild lifespans run to roughly 60–70 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 60.0 years**, matching the long-lived-potential convention used for the African Elephant report so the two elephants compare cleanly. Median survival is lower, and males die younger than females.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-low. Truly wild long-term survival data are limited.

### Bite and natural weapons
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Elephants do not fight by biting, and no defensible bite-pressure measurement exists. `0.0` means no reliable measurement, not "no bite".
- **Tusks:** elongated second upper incisors in males. In Sri Lanka, tusks of the minority of tusked males grow up to about **1.8 m** and **35 kg**. Representative mainland tusks are shorter than African bull tusks.
- **Other weapons:** trunk strikes and grabs, head-butting and shoving with multi-tonne mass, and trampling with the feet.

### Defensive structure
- No armor or shell. Defense comes from very thick skin, enormous body depth, a massive skull and several tonnes of mass. Protection is lower than true armor but whole-body survivability is extreme.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The Asian bull's offense is built on mass. A 4.5-tonne animal can shove, butt, pin and trample almost any terrestrial opponent, and a tusked bull adds a stabbing and levering weapon backed by that whole body. The trunk is a strong, precise limb that can strike, grab and throw. Against predators the practical threats are a charge, a tusk thrust, a trunk blow and crushing under the feet.

Tusks are smaller and less universal than in African savanna bulls. Many bulls in Sri Lanka and some mainland regions are tuskless makhnas, which still fight effectively with head, trunk and mass but lose the penetrating weapon.

### Musth
Adult bulls enter musth, a periodic state of raised testosterone, temporal-gland secretion, urine dribbling and sharply increased aggression. In one captive study, serum testosterone averaged **41.2 ± 2.8 ng/ml** during the April–September musth period versus **7.6 ± 1.0 ng/ml** for the rest of the year. Wild bulls in musth fight other males far more often, and captive bulls in musth become extremely dangerous to handlers. In contests the larger bull usually wins when both are in the same state, but a smaller bull in musth can beat a larger bull that is not.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The instrumented 6.8 m/s maximum is respectable for a multi-tonne animal but slow next to predators and antelopes. Elephants cannot jump, turn slowly at speed and never have all four feet off the ground. They move well through dense forest, climb steep slopes and cross rivers. Agility is therefore low on the absolute scale but not negligible.

### Endurance
Asian elephants spend most of the day feeding and moving; WWF notes they need around **150 kg of food per day** and can spend more than two thirds of the day feeding. They travel long distances between forest patches, but heat load limits prolonged hard exertion and they rely on water, shade and mud.

### Senses
Hearing, smell and touch are excellent. Wild Asian elephants in southern India responded differently to recorded tiger and leopard growls at night: they retreated quietly from tiger growls but reacted more aggressively to leopard growls, matching the real danger each cat poses to calves. Elephants also use low-frequency rumbles, and part of that energy travels through the ground as seismic waves that may carry information. Eyesight is comparatively modest.

### Intelligence and tactics
The Asian elephant is one of the best-studied non-primate thinkers. In a 2006 *PNAS* study at the Bronx Zoo, an Asian elephant passed a mirror mark test, the standard test of mirror self-recognition. In a 2011 *PNAS* study, 12 elephants at the Thai Elephant Conservation Center solved a cooperative rope-pulling task, waited up to 45 seconds for a partner and did not pull when the partner had no access to the rope. This supports top-tier learning, memory and social problem solving.

### Sociality and predator defense
Females and young live in family groups led by older cows; adult bulls live alone or in loose bachelor groups and visit family groups when in musth. When threatened, adults form a defensive ring around calves and face the danger. Tigers opportunistically kill calves, for example among crop-raiding family groups around Bandipur and Wayanad, but a healthy adult bull has no regular natural predator.

### Fighting and aggression
Bull contests escalate from displays to shoving, tusk clashes and chases. Musth fights can cause serious or fatal injuries. Toward people, conflict is severe: Indian government figures cite roughly **500 human deaths a year** from elephants, with 587 in 2019 and 628 in 2023, mostly where crops and settlements overlap elephant range. This reflects habitat overlap as much as temperament, so it is used only as context for Ferocity.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense tropical forest and grassland mosaics where mass and trunk dexterity dominate.
- Rivers and swamps: elephants wade and swim well.
- Steep forested hills that slow heavier-footed ungulates less than people expect.

### Weaknesses
- Low absolute agility and no jumping; slow to turn at speed.
- Heat and water dependence limit prolonged exertion.
- Many bulls lack tusks, removing the penetrating weapon.
- Large target; calves are vulnerable to tigers.
- Weaker eyesight than hearing and smell.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** any terrestrial predator or herbivore that must close to contact, including big cats, bears, bovids and rhinoceroses well below its mass.
- **Mixed:** the African savanna elephant, which is heavier with larger tusks; very large aquatic or armored opponents in water.
- **Bad:** opponents it cannot reach (fliers, deep-water animals), and extreme ceiling anchors such as Megalodon or large whales in their own environment.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Musth Surge** — periodic testosterone-driven state in adult bulls that sharply raises aggression and fighting drive; a smaller bull in musth can defeat a larger one that is not. In a matchup it names a real escalation of commitment, not extra strength from nowhere.
2. **Trunk Strike and Grip** — the boneless, highly muscular trunk can strike, grab, lift and throw, giving the elephant a precise close-range tool on top of its tusks and mass.

### Unique traits
1. **Tusker or Makhna** — only males carry tusks, and in some populations most males are tuskless makhnas; the species' weapon kit varies more between individuals than in almost any other roster animal.
2. **Mirror-Aware Mind** — the Asian elephant is one of the few animals shown to pass a mirror mark test, alongside strong evidence for cooperative problem solving.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Asian elephants live in 13 range countries across South and Southeast Asia, from India and Sri Lanka through Myanmar, Thailand, Malaysia and Indonesia. They use dense tropical evergreen forest, dry and wet deciduous forest, scrubland and grassland, often moving between forest cover and open feeding areas.

### Diet and ecology
They are bulk herbivores that eat grasses, bark, roots, leaves, stems and fruit, needing around 150 kg of food a day. By opening forest paths, dispersing seeds and shaping vegetation, they act as important ecosystem engineers. Crops near forest edges attract them and drive conflict with people.

### Social structure
Family groups of related females and their young are led by an older matriarch. Adult bulls leave their birth group and live alone or in loose associations, joining family groups mainly to breed. Communication uses rumbles, trumpets, touch and scent.

### Reproduction and life history
Females give birth to a single calf after a long pregnancy of close to two years, and calves depend on their mothers and aunts for years. Males reach full body size and competitive status late, typically in their twenties or later, which is why prime bulls are the canonical specimen.

### Conservation
Endangered on the IUCN Red List, a status held since 1986, after declines of at least 50% over three generations. An IUCN-compiled 2018 set of national estimates totalled roughly 48,000–52,000 wild elephants, with wide uncertainty. Habitat loss and fragmentation, conflict with people, and poaching for ivory and other body parts are the main threats. Removing tuskers through poaching also skews sex ratios in some populations.

### Special features
- Only males carry tusks; many males are tuskless makhnas.
- Highly muscular, boneless trunk used for feeding, touch, smell, drinking and fighting.
- Instrumented running speed up to 6.8 m/s without a true aerial phase.
- Low-frequency rumbles, part of which travel through the ground.
- Musth in adult bulls.
- Very large brain and strong evidence for self-recognition and cooperation.

### Human interaction
Asian elephants have been captured and trained for timber work, transport, ceremony and war for thousands of years, and many still live in captivity in Myanmar, Thailand and India. Human-elephant conflict kills about 500 people a year in India alone, and around 100 elephants die in retaliation each year. Mitigation focuses on corridors, early-warning systems, crop protection and barriers.

### Genuine fun facts
- A 2003 *Nature* study timed Asian elephants at up to 25 km/h and found they "run" with a bouncing body but never lift all four feet at once.
- An Asian elephant at the Bronx Zoo passed a mirror mark test, one of very few animals to do so.
- In a cooperative rope task, elephants waited up to 45 seconds for a partner before pulling.
- Only about 7% of Sri Lankan males carry tusks.
- Wild Asian elephants react differently to tiger and leopard growls, retreating quietly from tigers but confronting leopards.
- An adult can eat around 150 kg of food in a day.
- The trunk contains 17 major muscles made of up to 150,000 muscle fascicles; the popular "40,000 muscles" figure counts these bundles differently.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Asian elephant is a multi-tonne forest giant whose prime bulls combine crushing mass, tusks, a powerful grasping trunk and the testosterone-fuelled aggression of musth. Slightly smaller and less heavily tusked than its African cousin, it is still one of the roster's strongest land animals, and it pairs that power with some of the best-documented intelligence of any non-primate.

### Rich narrative profile
A mature Asian bull weighs around four and a half tonnes and stands close to three metres at the shoulder. That mass is the core of its fighting ability. It can shove over trees, pin an opponent and trample with feet that carry a tonne each, and a tusked bull adds a stabbing weapon driven by the whole body. The trunk works like a strong, precise extra limb, able to deliver a blow or seize a limb. Almost nothing on land can absorb a committed charge from an animal this heavy.

The species is not simply a smaller African elephant. Tusks are shorter and far less universal: many males, especially in Sri Lanka, carry none at all and fight with head, trunk and bulk. Asian elephants live mostly in forest and forest-grassland mosaics rather than open savanna, and they are skilled at climbing wooded slopes, crossing rivers and moving quietly through cover. Their measured top speed of about 25 km/h is enough to catch a running person but far below any predator's sprint.

Its mind is its other great weapon. Asian elephants have passed the mirror mark test, cooperated in rope-pulling experiments and discriminated between the growls of tigers and leopards according to the danger each poses. In musth, adult bulls become intensely aggressive, and a smaller bull in musth can drive off a larger rival. For ABS purposes the Asian elephant sits just below the African savanna elephant in size, power and weaponry, close to it in cognition, and well above rhinoceros and bovid heavyweights in overall defense.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Length:** no primary head-body measurement captured; 600 cm is provisional.
- **Lifespan:** median survival (about 42 years in Myanmar timber elephants) is far below the 60-year potential used here for comparability with the African Elephant report.
- **Tusks:** tusk possession ranges from rare (Sri Lanka) to common in some mainland populations; the canonical bull is a tusker.
- **Human deaths:** reflect habitat overlap and crop raiding, not a simple measure of aggression.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus African Elephant (5,500 kg):** the Asian bull is about 1,000 kg lighter with shorter, less universal tusks, so Size, Raw Power, Weaponry, Attack and Defense sit a few points lower (Attack 89.0 vs 92.0, Defense 90.0 vs 94.0). Measured speed is essentially the same, so Agility is similar (46.0 vs 45.0). Intelligence is a deliberate tie at 88.0 because the strongest self-recognition and cooperation evidence comes from this very species.
- **Versus Rhinoceros (southern white, 2,300 kg):** the elephant is roughly twice the mass with a more versatile weapon kit (tusks, trunk, feet) and far higher cognition, so it stays above the rhino in Attack (89.0 vs 86.0), Defense (90.0 vs 82.0) and Intelligence. The rhino keeps higher Agility and Speed.
- **Versus Hippopotamus (1,800 kg):** the hippo's specialized killing bite keeps its Attack at 90.0, one point above the Asian elephant, while the elephant's much greater mass gives it higher Defense.
- **Versus Siberian Tiger:** tigers kill calves, but a prime bull is far outside tiger prey size; the elephant is much higher in Defense and Size, far lower in Agility.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the elephant is not scored near 100 in Attack or Defense; those ceilings belong to marine giants and Megalodon-scale anchors.
