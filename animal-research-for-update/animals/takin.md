# Takin (*Budorcas taxicolor*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Takin (also "cattle chamois" or "gnu goat"); subspecies include the Mishmi, Bhutan, Sichuan and golden takins
- **Scientific name:** *Budorcas taxicolor* Hodgson, 1850
- **Exact animal represented:** the takin of the eastern Himalayas and the mountains of central and southwestern China, treated as one species with regional forms that differ in coat color and size.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature bull of about **320 kg**, **120 cm** at the shoulder and **210 cm** head-body length, with full horns. This is a prime bull inside the commonly reported 300–350 kg male range, not an exceptional animal.
- **Sex choice:** bulls are larger and are the sex that spars for mates. A GPS-collar study notes adult males are about 40% heavier than adult females; reported female weights run about 250–280 kg at the top end.
- **Population variation:** the Bhutan (national animal of Bhutan), Mishmi, Sichuan and golden takins differ mainly in coat color, from dark brown to golden. Few wild animals have been weighed, and body data rely heavily on zoo animals and handbooks.

### Horns and bite
- **Horns:** both sexes; they start near the midline of the head, run parallel to the skull, then turn outward, back and up to a short point. Ridged at the base. Usually about **30 cm**, up to **64 cm** (Wikipedia, ADW). San Diego Zoo gives up to 90 cm, which conflicts with the other sources and is treated as an outlier.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Takins do not fight by biting; no measurement exists.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. A stocky, deep-chested body and a long, shaggy double coat: hair from about 3 cm on the flanks in summer to 24 cm under the head in winter. A 2025 study of golden takin hair found thick, air-filled cores in the overcoat hairs and a finely crimped undercoat suited to insulation. The skin secretes an oily, strong-smelling substance that sheds rain and fog.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The takin fights with thick, ridged horns set on a massive head and a stocky 320 kg body. Bulls spar head-to-head for dominance in the summer rut and bellow during fights. The horns curve outward and back, so the most dangerous use is a butting, tossing strike: in a documented case of maternal infanticide among captive golden takins, a mother tossed her newborn into the air with her head and horns, leaving punctures in its belly. The horns are shorter and less of a spear than a sable's or an oryx's, and the takin lacks the fused boss of a musk-ox or buffalo.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Short, strong legs end in large two-toed hooves with well-developed spurs, giving grip on steep, rocky and forested slopes. Takins are capable climbers and use raised surfaces readily in zoos; they stand on their hind legs to feed on leaves more than 3 m up and have jumped 1.8 m from a standing start in captivity. They move quickly into dense undergrowth when alarmed. No sprint data exist.

### Endurance
Takins make seasonal migrations, climbing to the tree line above 4,300 m in spring and summer and descending into forested valleys in winter. GPS collars on golden takins showed crepuscular activity peaks and the lowest movement rates in winter. Their insulating double coat and oily skin suit cold, wet mountain weather.

### Senses
Herd members detect danger and warn others with a loud "cough". No sensory thresholds are published.

### Intelligence and tactics
The main tactic is collective: an alarm cough sends the herd running into dense underbrush, where the animals lie down to avoid being seen. Both sexes use the scent of their own urine to signal dominance. Groups gather for days at salt licks and hot springs, suggesting a learned use of key sites.

### Sociality and hunting
Family groups of about 20; herds of up to 300 gather high on the slopes in summer and break into groups of 10–35 in cooler months. Older bulls are mostly solitary outside the rut. Takins browse leaves, bamboo shoots, grasses and flowers and depend on mineral licks.

### Fighting and aggression
Bulls spar head-to-head in July and August. No systematic record of attacks on people was located; the takin's reaction to disturbance is usually to hide. The infanticide observations show that adults can inflict fatal horn wounds on small targets.

### Predator defense
Few natural predators other than bears, wolves, leopards and dholes. Calves are darker, which helps hide them. Defense is concealment in thick cover, herd vigilance and the bulk and horns of adults.

### Environmental strengths
- Steep forested valleys, bamboo thickets and rocky alpine grassland from about 1,000 to 4,500 m.
- Cold, wet, foggy weather that its coat and oily skin shed.
- Dense undergrowth for hiding and close-range defense.

### Weaknesses
- No armor; body protection is mostly hair.
- Horns shorter and less lethal than a buffalo's or oryx's.
- Heavy build limits sustained speed on open ground.
- Tends to hide rather than confront.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** wolves, dholes and leopards attacking a healthy adult alone; smaller mountain animals that must close in to fight.
- **Mixed:** bears and other heavy predators; similar-sized bovids such as the musk-ox.
- **Bad:** big cats and bears with a large size or weapon advantage; heavy horned bovids such as buffalo and yaks; open, flat ground where it cannot use slopes or cover.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Cough and Vanish** — one loud alarm cough sends the whole herd into dense undergrowth, where the animals lie down and disappear from view.
2. **Oil-Slick Coat** — the skin secretes an oily, bitter-smelling substance that sheds rain and fog, over a double coat with air-filled overcoat hairs for insulation.

### Unique traits
1. **Bee-Stung Moose Face** — a massive head with a long, arched, swollen-looking nose that led biologist George Schaller to compare the takin to a "bee-stung moose".
2. **Mountain Ox-Goat** — one of the largest and stockiest members of the goat and sheep subfamily (Caprinae), with the bulk of a small ox, standing up to 1.4 m tall yet able to climb and to feed standing on its hind legs.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Forested valleys, bamboo thickets and rocky, grass-covered alpine zones from about 1,000 to 4,500 m in Bhutan, northeastern India (Arunachal Pradesh), Myanmar and central and southwestern China, including the Qinling Mountains of Shaanxi where the golden takin lives.

### Diet and ecology
Browses and grazes leaves, grasses, bamboo shoots and flowers, feeding mostly in the early morning and late afternoon. Salt is an important part of the diet, and groups may stay at a mineral deposit for several days.

### Social structure
Family groups of about 20, gathering into summer herds of up to 300; bulls are mostly solitary outside the rut. Herds are made up of cows, kids, subadults and young males.

### Reproduction and life history
Mating takes place in July and August. After about eight months, a single kid of 5–7 kg is born; it can follow its mother within three days, eats solid food by about two months and grows horns at about six months. Maturity comes at two to two and a half years.

### Conservation
Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List and considered Endangered in China. Threats are overhunting and habitat destruction; takin horns have been sold openly in markets in Myanmar, with 89 sets seen during surveys from 1999 to 2006. A 2010 study of Mishmi takins suggested the species is especially vulnerable to climate change.

### Special features
- Horns in both sexes that sweep out, back and up from the midline of the head.
- Long, arched, swollen-looking nose.
- Oily, weatherproof skin secretion.
- Two-toed hooves with strongly developed spurs.
- Long double coat, up to 24 cm under the head in winter.

### Human interaction
The takin is the national animal of Bhutan, and takins are kept in a reserve at Thimphu. It is hunted illegally for meat and horns in parts of its range, and its horns turn up in wildlife markets.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its nose earned it George Schaller's description of a "bee-stung moose".
- The takin is Bhutan's national animal.
- Its skin oozes an oily substance that works like a raincoat in mountain storms.
- Takins stand on their hind legs to reach leaves more than 3 m up.
- A takin sleeps with its head resting on its outstretched front legs, like a dog.
- Summer herds can reach 300 animals high on the mountain slopes.
- Zoo takins have jumped 1.8 m straight up from a standing start.

### Concise site-ready summary
The takin is a stocky, 320 kg mountain bovid from the Himalayas and western China, with thick swept-back horns, a bulbous nose and an oily, weatherproof coat. It prefers to hide in dense cover after an alarm cough, but its bulk, horns and sure-footed climbing make it a tough, well-rounded mountain brawler.

### Rich narrative profile
The takin looks assembled from parts: the horns of a wildebeest, the nose of a moose, the bulk of a small bison and the climbing habits of a goat. A mature bull weighs around 320 kg and stands up to 1.4 m tall, with a heavy head, a deep chest, short strong legs and big spurred hooves that grip rocky slopes. Its long coat, thick and shaggy in winter, is slicked with an oily secretion that sheds the rain and fog of its mountain forests.

Takins spend the warm months high on the slopes in large herds, climbing to the tree line above 4,300 m, and move down into forested valleys for winter. They feed at dawn and dusk, gather for days at salt licks, and when danger appears one animal coughs loudly and the herd crashes into thick undergrowth and lies down out of sight. Bears, wolves, leopards and dholes are their only natural enemies.

On the ABS scale the takin sits beside the musk-ox as a mid-weight mountain and cold-climate bovid. It is slightly less heavily armed than the musk-ox, which has a fused horn boss and a practiced defensive ring, but it is more agile on steep ground. It remains far below heavy bovids such as the gaur or water buffalo in size and power, and far below the mountain goat in climbing agility.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 300–350 kg bulls vs a 322 kg captive record vs ADW's 150–400 kg range; 320 kg sits inside the bull range.
- **Horn length:** 30–64 cm (Wikipedia, ADW) vs up to 90 cm (San Diego Zoo); the 90 cm figure is not used.
- **Lifespan:** only zoo-based averages were found.
- **Speed and danger to people:** no measured speed and no systematic attack records were located.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Musk Ox (320 kg; A64 D61 Ag50 St72 Sp74):** same body mass. The musk-ox keeps higher Attack, Defense and Special for its fused horn boss, qiviut coat and defensive ring; the takin gets higher Agility (58.0 vs 50.0) for climbing and jumping on steep slopes.
- **Versus Yak:** the yak has no research report yet; its live values are legacy placeholders and are not used as anchors. A wild yak bull is roughly twice the takin's mass and should end up well above it in Size, Raw Power, Attack and Defense.
- **Versus Mountain Goat (110 kg; Ag88 Sp78):** the mountain goat stays far ahead in Agility as a cliff specialist, while the takin's triple mass gives higher Attack (60.0 vs 51.0) and Defense (57.0 vs 42.0).
- **Versus Elk (320 kg; A62 D47):** equal mass; the elk keeps slightly higher Attack for its large antlers and rut aggression, while the takin's stocky frame and thick coat give higher Defense.
