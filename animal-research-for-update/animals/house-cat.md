# House Cat (*Felis catus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** House cat (domestic cat)
- **Scientific name:** *Felis catus* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the ordinary domestic cat, including free-roaming and feral cats, not a specific pedigree breed or any wild cat species. Domestic cats were bred from Near Eastern wildcat (*Felis silvestris lybica*) stock.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (domestic animal).
- **Status:** Living; domesticated and found worldwide with people, with large feral populations.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature intact adult male of about **5.0 kg**, a typical non-obese tomcat rather than a giant breed or an overweight pet.
- **Sex choice:** males are usually about 10–20% heavier than females, have broader heads and are the sex that fights most over territory and mates. Estimated bite force in feral cats is also higher in males.
- **Population variation:** body size varies with breed, diet and neuter status. Large pedigree breeds and obese pets are much heavier than typical cats; feral cats are often lighter. Neither extreme is used as the baseline.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Standard summaries (ADW and Britannica) give typical adult mass of about **4–5.5 kg**, with males heavier.
- A study of 568 stray and feral cats found body mass was the strongest influence on bite force, with males larger than females.
- **Canonical:** **5.0 kg** mature male.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Height
- Summaries give about **23–25 cm** at the shoulder.
- **Canonical:** **25.0 cm** shoulder height for a mature male.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Length
- ADW gives an average length of **76.2 cm**; this figure includes the tail. A separate head-body measurement was not verified in this pass.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 76.0 cm**, explicitly total length including the tail. Head-body length alone is shorter; migration should flag this convention difference.
- **Confidence:** Moderate for the figure, low for comparability with head-body values used elsewhere.

### Speed
- Pet-care pages commonly quote up to **48 km/h (30 mph)**, but no instrumented measurement or institutional source was found.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- Companion cats commonly live about **12–18 years**, with a median near 14 years reported for a large UK sample; free-roaming cats usually live much shorter lives.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 14.0 years**, representing a typical owned cat.
- **Confidence:** Moderate; strongly dependent on indoor versus outdoor life.

### Bite and claws
- A study of 568 stray and feral cats estimated bite force with a lever model at **206.2 ± 44.7 N** in males (n = 168) and **171.9 ± 29.3 N** in females (n = 120). These are force estimates from skull measurements, not pressure measurements.
- A veterinary review of bite forces in dogs and cats cites a calculated domestic cat estimate of about **73 N at the canines and 118 N at the carnassials**.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Force in newtons cannot be converted to PSI without a contact area, and no pressure measurement exists.
- **Claws:** sharp, fully retractable, hooked claws on all four feet; the dewclaw on each forefoot adds grip.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. A loose, flexible skin and fur, a very flexible spine and fast reflexes are its only physical protection.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The house cat is a small ambush hunter with the same basic kit as small wild cats: stalking, a short explosive rush, a grab with retractable claws, and a killing bite to the nape of small prey. In a fight with another cat it also rakes with the hind claws while gripping with the forefeet. Its weapons are very effective against rodents, birds, lizards and insects, which is why free-roaming cats kill vast numbers of small animals. Against anything much larger than a rabbit, however, its 5 kg body cannot deliver serious damage.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Cats are digitigrade, with a very flexible spine and powerful hind legs that produce fast acceleration and high jumps. They climb well, balance on narrow supports, and use a righting reflex to turn feet-down when falling. This makes them among the most maneuverable animals of their size, although absolute speed and reach are small.

### Endurance
Cats are sprinters, not endurance runners. Hunting is based on waiting, stalking and a brief rush; they tire quickly in long chases and spend much of the day resting.

### Senses
Hearing is exceptionally broad: laboratory testing found a range of about **48 Hz to 85 kHz**, one of the widest of any mammal. Eyes with a reflective tapetum lucidum work in very low light, and long whiskers help at close range and in the dark. Smell is good but secondary to hearing and vision when hunting.

### Intelligence and tactics
Cats learn quickly from reward, remember hunting sites, and adjust to living alongside people, dogs and other cats. They are solitary hunters but can live in colonies where related females cooperate over kittens. Tactics are mainly patience, concealment and timing rather than coordinated group action.

### Sociality and hunting
Free-roaming cats hunt alone. They take whatever small prey is available; un-owned cats cause most of the wildlife losses attributed to cats in the United States.

### Fighting and aggression
Tomcats fight over territory and access to females with loud threat displays, arched back and raised fur to look larger, then biting, grappling and hind-claw raking. Cats defend themselves ferociously when cornered, but usually retreat or climb away from larger threats.

### Environmental strengths
- Cluttered terrain with cover, fences, trees and high perches.
- Low light and night conditions.
- Confined spaces where a larger opponent cannot use its size.

### Weaknesses
- Very small absolute size and power.
- No armor or thick hide.
- Poor endurance in long chases.
- Weapons cannot seriously injure large animals.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rodents, small birds, lizards and other animals well under its own mass.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized animals with their own weapons, such as small mustelids or snakes.
- **Bad:** any medium or large predator, including foxes, coyotes and larger cats; large herbivores; venomous animals.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Righting Reflex** — when a cat falls, its balance organs and flexible spine let it rotate to land feet-first, reducing injury and allowing it to recover position almost instantly.
2. **Ultrasonic Ears** — cats hear from about 48 Hz up to about 85 kHz, far above human hearing, which lets them locate rodents by their high-pitched sounds and detect threats early.

### Unique traits
1. **World's Most Common Cat** — bred from Near Eastern wildcat stock and now living with people on every inhabited continent, the house cat is by far the most numerous member of the cat family.
2. **Island Invader** — feral cats are listed among the world's 100 worst invasive alien species and have contributed to at least 14% of bird, mammal and reptile extinctions on islands.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Worldwide, wherever people live, from city apartments to farms, deserts, tropical islands and sub-Antarctic islands. Feral cats live independently of people in many of these places.

### Diet and ecology
Obligate carnivores. Pet cats eat prepared food, but free-roaming cats hunt small mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and insects, and scavenge. A 2013 study estimated that free-ranging cats in the contiguous United States kill about **1.3–4.0 billion birds and 6.3–22.3 billion mammals** each year, most of them killed by un-owned cats.

### Social structure
Solitary hunters with flexible social lives. Free-roaming cats can form colonies around food sources, where related females may nurse and guard each other's kittens. Males hold larger ranges and compete for females.

### Reproduction and life history
Cats can breed more than once a year, with litters of several kittens. Kittens learn hunting partly by watching their mother. Sexual maturity can come within the first year, which allows unmanaged populations to grow quickly.

### Conservation
Not applicable as a wild species; it is a **Domesticated** animal. Its conservation relevance is as a threat: feral and free-ranging cats are listed by IUCN among the 100 of the World's Worst Invasive Alien Species, and on islands they are implicated in at least 14% of bird, mammal and reptile extinctions and threaten at least 175 vertebrate taxa.

### Special features
- Fully retractable claws.
- Very flexible spine and powerful hind legs.
- Righting reflex when falling.
- Hearing range of about 48 Hz to 85 kHz.
- Reflective tapetum lucidum for low-light vision.
- Long, sensitive whiskers.

### Human interaction
Cats have lived with people for thousands of years. Genetic work on nearly 1,000 cats places their origin in Near Eastern wildcats, around the first farming villages of the Fertile Crescent, and a cat buried with a person in Cyprus about 9,500 years ago is among the earliest evidence of the partnership. Today cats are among the most popular pets in the world, and their outdoor hunting is a major wildlife management issue.

### Genuine fun facts
- Cats can hear sounds up to about 85 kHz, far above the human limit near 20 kHz.
- A cat buried beside a person in Cyprus about 9,500 years ago is one of the oldest signs of cats living with people.
- Genetic testing of 979 cats traced domestic cats to Near Eastern wildcat stock.
- In one feral-cat study, males had an estimated bite force of about 206 N, around a fifth more than females.
- Free-ranging cats in the contiguous US may kill billions of birds and mammals each year.
- A cat's claws are fully retractable and stay sharp because they are sheathed while walking.

### Concise site-ready summary
The house cat is a 5 kg pocket predator with the same toolkit as small wild cats: stealth, a lightning pounce, retractable claws and a precise killing bite, backed by superb balance and ultrasonic hearing. It is deadly to mice and birds, but on the absolute scale it is a lightweight that cannot hurt large animals.

### Rich narrative profile
The house cat is the most familiar predator on Earth. Bred from Near Eastern wildcat stock and carried by people across the globe, it still hunts the way small wild cats do: it watches, creeps, waits, then explodes into a short rush and pins prey with its claws before a quick bite to the neck. Very flexible spine, strong hind legs, a righting reflex and hearing that reaches about 85 kHz make it one of the most capable small hunters anywhere.

That efficiency shows up in the numbers. Free-ranging cats are estimated to kill billions of birds and mammals each year in the United States alone, and feral cats on islands have helped drive many species to extinction. In a fight, cats can be fierce: an angry tomcat fights with bites, forefoot grabs and raking hind claws, and a cornered cat is hard to handle.

For ABS the key rule is absolute scale. Pound for pound the cat is formidable, but a 5 kg animal has very little absolute power, reach or durability. It therefore sits far below the leopard and well below the 10–17 kg wild cats such as the bobcat and caracal in Attack and Defense, while scoring high in Agility and Senses, where small size is not a handicap.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Leopard (60 kg; A60 D43):** the brief's anchor. At 5 kg the house cat sits far below in Attack (24.0) and Defense (16.0) despite a similar hunting style.
- **Versus Bobcat (10 kg; A44), Serval (14 kg; A41) and Caracal (17 kg; A44):** these wild cats are two to three times heavier with larger canines and stronger kills on prey up to small antelope or deer fawns, so they stay 17–20 points higher in Attack. Their Agility (82.0–92.0) brackets the house cat's 88.0. Serval keeps higher Senses (87.0 vs 84.0) for its specialized hearing.
- **Versus Red Fox (7.2 kg; A28 D25) and Raccoon (6.5 kg; A30 D29):** slightly heavier mesocarnivores sit a few points above the cat in Attack and Defense; the cat's claws keep it close in Weaponry.
- **Versus Ferret (1.5 kg; A17 D10):** the cat is more than three times heavier and better armed, so it sits above.
- **Versus Stoat:** the live stoat values are legacy placeholders (for example Attack 2.7, Defense 36.8) with no research report, so they are not used as anchors. A researched stoat, at a small fraction of a cat's mass, should sit below the house cat in Attack and Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "fierce for its size" is scored under Ferocity (58.0), not Attack.
