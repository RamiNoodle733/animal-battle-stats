# Red-Eyed Tree Frog (*Agalychnis callidryas*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Roster label:** Red-Eyed Tree Frog
- **Resolved taxon:** *Agalychnis callidryas* (Cope, 1862), order Anura. Current databases differ at family rank, with Phyllomedusidae increasingly treated as a family and older literature placing phyllomedusines within Hylidae. This report uses **Phyllomedusidae** while retaining that taxonomic caveat.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime **adult female**, **0.014 kg** and **7.0 cm snout-vent length (SVL)**. Female is selected because females are materially larger than males, making the female the stronger representative fighter even though males perform most direct reproductive contests.
- **Dimorphism:** Animal Diversity Web gives a species range of 6-15 g and 4-7 cm and explicitly notes females are larger. Field-guide material summarized by AmphibiaWeb gives females to about 77 mm and males to about 59 mm. Adult males additionally develop vocal structures and nuptial pads.
- **Population variation:** geographic differentiation is real rather than cosmetic noise. A 2016 mate-choice study found strong population genetic structure coincident with geographic differentiation in colour pattern and body size in Costa Rica. Flank patterning also varies geographically. Canonical values therefore represent a robust adult female, not a universal population mean.

## 2. Physical measurements and defensible ranges

### Mass and body length
Animal Diversity Web reports **6-15 g** and **4-7 cm** overall, with females larger. Institutional/field-guide accounts commonly place adult females around 6-8 cm and males around 5 cm. The canonical fighter is therefore set at **14 g / 7.0 cm SVL**, near the upper representative female range without using an exceptional record.

### Height
A frog has no stable standardized standing height because posture changes dramatically between a flattened rest, crouch, climb and jump. **`height_cm = 0.0`** is preferred to a fabricated posture-dependent measurement.

### Speed and locomotion
No defensible instrumented species-specific maximum travel speed was located. Red-eyed tree frogs are nocturnal, arboreal and saltatorial, with long limbs, webbed digits and expanded adhesive toe discs. They walk/climb with precise branch control and can jump rapidly when escaping, but a jump distance or generic frog speed should not be converted into a maximum m/s without direct evidence. **`speed_mps = 0.0`.**

### Lifespan
Animal Diversity Web reports about **5 years average in the wild** and reports of **8+ years** in captivity. Henry Vilas Zoo gives 4-5 years. **`lifespan_years = 5.0`** is the best representative wild value.

### Bite force and weapons
No credible standardized species-specific bite-force PSI measurement was found. The mouth is suited to capturing small arthropods, not inflicting serious absolute trauma. **`bite_force_psi = 0.0`.**

Natural offensive tools are limited to a small mouth, tongue-assisted prey capture, forelimb restraint at prey scale, and muscular hindlimbs. The species has **no venom, poison, claws, horns, armor, stinger or major crushing bite**.

### Defensive anatomy
Defense is primarily behavioral and positional. The frog's green resting posture conceals bright flank/foot colors, while sudden exposure of red eyes, blue/yellow flanks and orange feet can function as a startle/deimatic display. Its permeable amphibian skin provides almost no mechanical armor and creates dehydration vulnerability.

## 4. Combat biology

### Offensive mechanics
This is a tiny insectivore. Hunting is close-range and visually guided: it approaches or waits for small arthropods, then captures them with a rapid mouth/tongue strike. Against roster animals substantially larger than itself, its jaws and limbs produce negligible absolute damage. The female's larger body increases reach and force slightly relative to males but does not change the full-roster scaling problem.

### Fighting and reproductive contests
Males call in wet-season breeding aggregations and may physically compete for access to females, including scrambling and wrestling around amplexus. That behavior establishes willingness to engage conspecifics but does not justify a high absolute Attack or Raw Power score. The canonical female is larger, while the male is the more routinely combative sex, an important dimorphism tradeoff recorded rather than hidden.

### Defense and toughness
The frog survives by avoiding clean contact, not by absorbing it. Camouflage during daytime rest, nocturnal activity, adhesive climbing, rapid jumps and a startle display are useful against visually hunting predators. Soft permeable skin and tiny body mass make it extremely vulnerable to crushing, tearing, desiccation and one clean hit from most vertebrate opponents.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Arboreal locomotion is the strongest direct battle-relevant attribute. Long limbs, webbing and enlarged toe discs support controlled movement on leaves and branches. Small mass permits rapid reorientation and escape through vegetation. This is high **maneuverability**, not evidence of high absolute top speed.

### Endurance and physiology
The species is an ectothermic nocturnal amphibian. It can forage and move through vegetation for nightly activity, but there is no evidence for exceptional sustained high-output exercise. Heat and dehydration are major constraints. Stamina therefore remains moderate-low despite competent routine arboreal activity.

### Senses
Large eyes are suited to nocturnal visual detection, and acoustic communication is central to breeding. Vision, hearing and substrate/tactile cues are useful in dense vegetation. The spectacular red iris is not treated as a supernatural sensory boost.

### Tactics and cognition
Adult behavior is effective but relatively stereotyped: conceal by day, forage at night, use vegetation and adhesive feet for positioning, and jump away from threats. There is no evidence for mammal- or corvid-like flexible combat problem solving. The species' famous risk-sensitive hatching behavior belongs to **embryos**, not the canonical adult, so it is documented as life-history biology rather than incorrectly inflating adult Intelligence or Special.

### Predation and predator defense
Adults eat small insects and other arthropods and are preyed upon by snakes, birds, bats and other rainforest predators. The green dorsum provides concealment when the frog tucks its colored surfaces away. When disturbed, sudden exposure of contrasting colors may momentarily disrupt a predator's visual tracking, buying time for a jump.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense humid rainforest vegetation with leaves, branches and vertical escape routes.
- Night conditions that suit its normal activity cycle.
- Small-scale clutter where adhesive digits and low mass improve repositioning.
- Matchups against tiny arthropods that fall within normal prey scale.

### Environmental weaknesses
- Dry, hot or exposed environments where water loss becomes dangerous.
- Flat open ground that removes the value of arboreal grip and concealment.
- Large opponents able to kill with one strike, bite, stomp or crush.
- Armored or chemically defended prey that cannot be safely swallowed.
- Cold conditions that suppress ectothermic performance.

### Matchup archetypes
**Best against:** tiny soft-bodied insects and similarly minute prey in humid vegetation, especially where vertical positioning lets the frog initiate or evade contact.

**Worst against:** almost any medium or large vertebrate, armored arthropods too large to swallow, ranged/aerial attackers, and opponents that can force contact in open or dry terrain.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Adhesive Canopy Grip** - expanded toe discs and arboreal limb control let the frog cling to leaves and branches, maintain unconventional angles and escape vertically where many terrestrial opponents cannot follow.
2. **Chromatic Startle Flash** - a resting frog conceals much of its bright coloration, then sudden movement exposes red eyes, blue/yellow flanks and orange feet; this deimatic contrast can interrupt a predator's visual attack long enough for escape.

### Unique traits
1. **Female Size Advantage** - females are materially larger than males, so the canonical combat specimen is female even though males perform more reproductive wrestling and calling contests.
2. **Risk-Sensing Embryos** - embryos can distinguish vibration patterns associated with snake attacks and hatch early to escape, a remarkable life-history adaptation that is documented here but deliberately not counted as an adult combat power.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The species occupies humid lowland and premontane forests from southern Mexico through Central America into northwestern/northern Colombia, generally near ponds or slow/still water needed for reproduction. ADW reports occurrence from sea level to about 1,250 m, while recent Colombian work records the broader species near similar low-to-mid elevations. Adults are primarily arboreal and nocturnal.

### Diet and ecology
Adults are insectivores that consume small arthropods such as moths, flies, crickets and other insects. They are both predators and prey in rainforest food webs. Their reliance on humid vegetation and breeding water links terrestrial canopy/understory ecology with aquatic tadpole development.

### Social structure and reproduction
Adults are not cooperative hunters. Breeding occurs mainly in the rainy season. Males call and compete around breeding sites; females select mates and pairs enter amplexus. Eggs are deposited in gelatinous clutches on vegetation overhanging water. A 2024 biomechanical study measured *A. callidryas* clutches averaging about **40 eggs** in its sample, consistent with ADW's approximately 40-egg average. After several days, hatchlings drop into water and continue development as tadpoles.

### Embryonic escape hatching
This species is a classic model for developmental behavioral plasticity. Experiments show embryos use the temporal and frequency structure of substrate vibrations to distinguish dangerous snake attacks from benign disturbances and can hatch prematurely to escape. Early hatching itself carries aquatic predation costs, so the response is risk-sensitive rather than automatic. A 2024 comparison reported about **77% escape success** for prematurely hatching *A. callidryas* embryos under the studied conditions.

### Conservation
Current conservation references list *A. callidryas* as **Least Concern**, but the population trend has been reported as decreasing. Habitat alteration, disease and collection/trade remain relevant concerns. CITES trade documentation lists *A. callidryas* in **Appendix II**, reflecting regulation of international trade rather than an Endangered status.

### Adaptations
- Enlarged adhesive toe discs for arboreal movement.
- Long limbs and saltatory escape locomotion.
- Green daytime concealment posture.
- Contrasting deimatic/startle coloration.
- Nocturnal activity that reduces heat and dehydration exposure.
- Risk-sensitive embryo hatching triggered by predator-associated vibrations.

### Human interaction
The species is globally recognizable and common in zoological collections and the amphibian pet trade. Its popularity creates education value but also makes responsible sourcing important. Amphibian skin is permeable and sensitive, so unnecessary handling can cause stress and contamination even though this species is not poisonous.

### Genuine fun facts
- Females are substantially larger than males, reaching roughly 7-7.7 cm in well-supported field-guide accounts.
- The frog is brightly colored but **not poisonous**.
- Geographic populations differ in flank coloration and body size, and experimental females have shown preference for local males.
- Embryos can use vibration patterns to decide whether to hatch early during a snake attack.
- Eggs are laid above water rather than directly in it; hatchlings fall into the pond after hatching.
- The iconic red eyes can be hidden during rest as the frog compresses its body and tucks brightly colored limbs close to the torso.

### Concise site-ready summary
The red-eyed tree frog is a tiny nocturnal canopy specialist whose battle identity is almost entirely positional. A robust adult female weighs only about 14 grams, so its bite and raw power sit near the roster floor, but adhesive toe discs, rapid jumping and precise branch control produce excellent agility. Green camouflage and a sudden flash of red, blue and orange help it evade predators rather than withstand them.

### Rich narrative profile
The red-eyed tree frog is an important anti-inflation test for ABS. Its locomotor design is spectacular at its own scale: long limbs bridge gaps in vegetation, expanded toe discs maintain purchase on smooth leaves, and a tiny body can reverse direction or disappear into clutter quickly. Those facts justify a high Agility score. They do not justify high Raw Power, Attack or physical Defense. At fourteen grams, the canonical female can be catastrophically injured by opponents that barely notice her mechanically.

Its best defensive sequence begins before contact. During daylight rest the frog presents mostly green surfaces and hides the brighter parts of its body. If discovered, sudden exposure of red eyes, blue-and-yellow flanks and orange feet can startle a visual predator while the frog jumps away. That is a real matchup-changing mechanism, but it buys time rather than absorbing damage.

The species' most famous behavioral research actually concerns its embryos. Developing frogs can read vibrations through an egg clutch and hatch early during a snake attack. That is extraordinary sensory decision-making for an embryo, but the canonical ABS fighter is an adult. Treating embryo escape-hatching as adult Intelligence would be double-counting a different life stage, so this report records it as a unique biological trait while keeping adult cognition conservative.

### Source conflicts and uncertainty
- **Family rank:** Hylidae/Phyllomedusinae persists in older literature, while many modern databases recognize Phyllomedusidae. This report uses Phyllomedusidae and records the disagreement.
- **Body size:** institutional values overlap but are not identical. The 14 g / 7.0 cm female is a representative robust fighter, not a claimed population mean.
- **Lifespan:** wild figures cluster around 4-5 years, while captive reports can exceed 8 years. The canonical field is wild-representative.
- **Speed:** generic web speed values are rejected because no defensible instrumented species-specific maximum was recovered.
- **Startle function:** bright coloration is widely interpreted as deimatic/startle signaling, but precise effectiveness varies by predator and context. It is not treated as toxin or armor.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus American Bullfrog (*Lithobates catesbeianus*):** the 0.45-0.50 kg bullfrog is more than thirty times heavier and is a documented vertebrate predator, so Red-Eyed Tree Frog Attack **3.0** must remain far below Bullfrog **14.0**. Red-Eyed Tree Frog can nevertheless exceed Bullfrog in Agility because adhesive arboreal control is a distinct maneuverability advantage.
- **Versus Golden Poison Frog (*Phyllobates terribilis*):** both are tiny frogs, but *A. callidryas* lacks batrachotoxin. It must remain vastly below the Golden Poison Frog's Special **99.0** and chemical Defense contribution. Its higher body mass does not compensate for that missing toxin system.
- **Versus Hummingbird/Raven-scale fliers:** arboreal frog agility is excellent in vegetation but does not imply comparable aerial mobility or travel speed.
- **Anti-leakage check:** adhesive grip and startle coloration raise Maneuverability/Abilities/Special, not Raw Power. Embryo vibration sensing is not added to adult Intelligence. Small-body proportional jumping strength is not converted into high absolute Attack.
