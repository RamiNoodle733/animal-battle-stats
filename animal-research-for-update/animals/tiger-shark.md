# Tiger Shark (*Galeocerdo cuvier*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Tiger shark
- **Scientific name:** *Galeocerdo cuvier* (Péron & Lesueur, 1822), the only living member of the genus *Galeocerdo* and the family Galeocerdonidae (older sources place it among the requiem sharks, Carcharhinidae)
- **Exact animal represented:** the single living tiger shark species of tropical and warm-temperate seas worldwide. The live site photo shows a free-swimming adult-sized tiger shark in the Bahamas.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **4.0 m total length and about 500 kg**.
- **Sex choice:** females grow larger. Wikipedia notes that mature females are often over 3.7 m while mature males rarely reach that size; the largest males reach about 4 m, while exceptional females exceed 5 m. The Florida Museum gives a common adult length of 325–425 cm and a common weight of 385–635 kg. A 4.0 m, 500 kg female sits in the middle of that common adult range, not at record size.
- **Population notes:** growth rates vary by region, with some juvenile populations growing close to twice as fast as others. Young tiger sharks carry bold dark bars and spots that fade with age. A reported 7.4 m, 3,110 kg individual is far larger than any scientifically measured specimen and is not used.

### Speed
- Nakamura et al. (2011) put accelerometers on four free-ranging tiger sharks: they swam continuously at mean speeds of **0.5–0.9 m/s** and made burst swims that may represent prey pursuits.

### Lifespan
- Wikipedia: how long tiger sharks live is unknown, but they can live longer than 12 years.
- Natanson et al. (2023), using ages validated by Kneebone et al. (2008): median age at maturity 11.6 years for females and 9.5 years for males. A mature 4 m female is therefore well into her teens.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 0.0**, because no validated typical or maximum lifespan was read. Adults routinely exceed 12 years.

### Bite and dentition
- Teeth are broad, strongly serrated and have a distinctive sideways-hooked cusp; upper and lower teeth are similar in shape (Florida Museum). Relative to body size they are shorter than a white shark's but nearly as broad at the root, and suited to slicing hard-surfaced prey such as turtle shells (Wikipedia).
- A broad, heavily calcified jaw and a nearly terminal mouth support large-prey feeding. Teeth are replaced continuously.
- Head shaking turns the serrated teeth into a saw that removes chunks from much larger animals (Florida Museum).
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No species-specific bite measurement was located.

### Defensive structure
- Tough skin covered with dermal denticles, large body mass and cartilaginous skeleton. No armor. Gill slits, eyes and the underside remain vulnerable.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The tiger shark's weapon is a broad, heavy jaw lined with serrated, hook-cusped teeth. It can bite through the shells of large sea turtles and remove chunks from large animals by shaking its head. Adults regularly eat sea turtles (found in up to 20.8% of stomachs in some studies), dolphins, dugongs, seals, seabirds, rays and other sharks, and they scavenge whale carcasses. Wikipedia describes its food spectrum as the widest of any shark.

In a matchup, this combination of mass, bite and cutting teeth is enough to maim or kill most marine animals short of great whales and orcas. It is below the great white in size and bite specialization but above the bull, mako and oceanic whitetip in weight and hard-prey cutting ability.

### Hunting method
Tiger sharks are ambush and search predators. Accelerometer data show continuous swimming with repeated "yo-yo" dives through the water column, which Nakamura et al. interpreted as a prey-searching strategy, with burst swims during all phases. Dark stripes and a dark back camouflage the shark against dark backgrounds, and it approaches slowly before a short burst. At Kure Atoll, tiger sharks arrived at albatross fledging colonies before peak fledgling numbers; their strike efficiency was low, but once an interaction began, success was high, because the sharks kept attacking.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Long pectoral fins provide lift for maneuvering and the long upper tail lobe supports bursts. The tiger shark is heavier-bodied and less agile than the mako or thresher; its strength is a stealthy approach and a short burst, not sustained high-speed chasing.

### Endurance
Tiger sharks swim continuously. Acoustic tagging in the Bahamas recorded adults and sub-adults along the east coast of the United States, showing large-scale partial migrations, and Hawaiian sharks tagged off O'ahu migrate seasonally to Maui. Reported depths reach 350 m (Florida Museum) and approach 900 m (Wikipedia).

### Senses
Tiger sharks have electroreceptive ampullae of Lorenzini on the snout, a lateral line along the flanks and a reflective layer behind the retina that improves vision in low light. The full shark sensory suite supports hunting in murky water and at night.

### Intelligence and tactics
The species shows timing and flexibility: aggregating at seasonal food pulses such as albatross fledging, forming diffuse mating aggregations off Maui, and exploiting provisioning sites. At Fuvahmulah in the Maldives, 40 mature females showed a size-based dominance hierarchy with social behaviors such as giving way, parallel swimming and two newly described antagonistic behaviors, submission and pushing away. Flexibility is real but well below cetacean-level cognition.

### Aggression and human interaction
The International Shark Attack File lists 142 confirmed unprovoked attacks by tiger sharks (39 fatal), second only to the white shark (351, 59 fatal) and ahead of the bull shark (119, 26 fatal). The Florida Museum groups the white, tiger and bull sharks as "The Big Three" because they are large, share water with people and have teeth that shear rather than hold. Even so, attacks remain rare relative to human use of the water.

### Predators
Adult tiger sharks have few predators. Orcas kill them, biting off fins and eating the organs.

### Environmental strengths
- Warm coastal waters, reefs, seagrass beds, lagoons and river mouths.
- Murky or low-light water where its senses and camouflage help.
- Surface to deep water, with dives approaching 900 m.

### Weaknesses
- No armor; gills, eyes and belly are vulnerable.
- Relatively slow and not built for long high-speed chases.
- Cannot match orcas, great whales or the largest crocodilians in mass and power.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** sea turtles and other shelled prey, seals, dolphins, rays, smaller sharks and most medium marine animals.
- **Mixed:** great white sharks, large crocodiles in shallow water, fast agile fish that avoid the approach.
- **Bad:** orcas, sperm whales and other marine megafauna; anything that can attack from a position the shark cannot reach.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Shell-Saw Bite** — broad, serrated teeth with a sideways hook, set in a heavily calcified jaw and worked by side-to-side head shaking, slice through sea-turtle shells and carve chunks from animals far larger than the shark.
2. **Stripe-Shadow Ambush** — a dark striped back hides the shark against dark water or reef; it approaches slowly, then bursts in, and keeps striking after misses until it connects.

### Unique traits
1. **Ocean's Widest Menu** — the widest food spectrum of any shark, from crustaceans, fish and seabirds to sea turtles, dolphins, dugongs and whale carcasses, plus inedible objects such as cans and sacks.
2. **Tiger-Striped Giant** — bold bars and spots on young sharks fade with age on a body that commonly reaches 3.3–4.3 m and 385–635 kg.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Tropical and warm-temperate seas worldwide, especially coastal waters, reefs, lagoons, seagrass beds and river mouths, with regular trips into open ocean and deep water. Fuvahmulah Island in the Maldives hosts the largest known aggregation of tiger sharks.

### Diet and ecology
A generalist apex predator and scavenger. Prey includes sea turtles, rays, other sharks, bony fishes, seabirds, dolphins, dugongs, seals, squid, crustaceans and carrion. Juveniles take smaller prey; the diet broadens sharply at about 2.3 m. Dolphins and dugongs shift their habitat use when tiger sharks are abundant, so the shark shapes the behavior of its prey.

### Social structure
Mostly solitary, but tiger sharks gather at predictable food pulses and at mating aggregations. Mature females at Fuvahmulah form a size-based dominance hierarchy.

### Reproduction and life history
The tiger shark is ovoviviparous: eggs hatch inside the mother and fully developed young are born live, in litters of 10 to 80 pups. Newborns measure 51–76 cm. Females reach median maturity at 261.4 cm FL and 11.6 years, males at 258.9 cm FL and 9.5 years (western North Atlantic). Females are reported to breed once every three years.

### Conservation
Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List, mainly because of fishing and finning pressure.

### Special features
- Serrated, sideways-hooked teeth shaped for cutting hard prey.
- Broad, heavily calcified jaws and a nearly terminal mouth.
- Ampullae of Lorenzini and lateral line.
- Reflective retinal layer for low-light vision.
- Long upper tail lobe for bursts and long pectoral fins for lift.

### Human interaction
Second only to the white shark in confirmed unprovoked attacks and fatalities. Hawaii typically records three to four shark bites a year, a very low rate given the number of people in the water. Tiger sharks are also a major dive-tourism draw at provisioning sites such as Fuvahmulah and the Bahamas.

### Genuine fun facts
- Tiger sharks have been found with cans, plastic bottles and burlap sacks in their stomachs.
- Adult sea turtles have made up as much as 20.8% of stomach contents in some tiger shark studies.
- At Kure Atoll, tiger sharks arrive at albatross colonies about four weeks before peak fledging, when young birds are most vulnerable.
- Their stripes fade as they grow, so the oldest tiger sharks are often the least striped.
- Tagged tiger sharks swim continuously, rising and diving through the water column in a yo-yo pattern.
- Tiger sharks have been recorded at depths approaching 900 m.
- Orcas are among the few animals that prey on adult tiger sharks.

### Concise site-ready summary
The tiger shark is a 4 m, 500 kg generalist predator with serrated, sideways-hooked teeth that saw through sea-turtle shells. A stealthy, persistent hunter that eats almost anything, it ranks second only to the white shark in attacks on people, though it lacks the speed of lamnid sharks and is outclassed by orcas.

### Rich narrative profile
The tiger shark is the ocean's great generalist. A mature female commonly reaches around 4 m and 500 kg, and she will eat almost anything: fish, rays, seabirds, dolphins, dugongs, other sharks, whale carcasses and, most famously, sea turtles. The key is her teeth. Each is broad, finely serrated and hooked to one side, and a shake of the head turns a row of them into a saw that cuts through shell and bone.

Tiger sharks hunt by patience rather than raw speed. Tagged sharks swim continuously, rising and falling through the water column in a yo-yo pattern that researchers read as a search strategy. The dark striped back blends with deep water or reef, letting the shark close in slowly before a short burst. When it misses, it tries again: at albatross colonies in Hawaii, strike efficiency is low, but repeated attacks make predation successful overall. Tiger sharks also time their movements to seasonal food, arriving at fledging colonies ahead of peak numbers and gathering in diffuse aggregations during the mating season.

In a matchup, the tiger shark brings mass, cutting teeth and persistence. It is heavier than the bull and mako sharks and better equipped to cut hard prey, but it is less agile than lamnid sharks and smaller and less specialized than the great white. Orcas and the largest marine mammals remain out of its reach. On the absolute roster scale it sits in the elite marine-predator tier, just below the white shark in Attack and above most other sharks.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** weights of large adults vary widely (300–900 kg common in Wikipedia; 385–635 kg in the Florida Museum). The 500 kg canonical value is a mid-range estimate, not a measured mean.
- **Depth:** 350 m (Florida Museum) versus nearly 900 m (Wikipedia) reflects older versus newer tag data.
- **Speed:** no burst maximum was found; legacy speed claims are not used.
- **Lifespan:** no validated maximum age was read, so the field stays at 0.0 rather than guessing.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Great White Shark (A88.0 D68.0 Ag73.0 St91.0 I70.0 Sp87.0):** the white shark is heavier (900 kg canonical), faster, more specialized for large marine mammals and has the stronger sensory and endurance evidence. The tiger shark stays below it in every headline rating, most clearly in Agility.
- **Versus Mako Shark (A75.0 D53.0 Ag85.0 St88.0 I64.0 Sp86.0):** as the mako report anticipated, the heavier tiger shark with serrated shell-cutting teeth sits above the mako in Attack and Defense and well below it in Agility. The mako's warm muscles and measured speed keep its Stamina and Special higher.
- **Versus Bull Shark (A72.0 D52.0 Ag73.0 St69.0 I58.0 Sp82.0):** the tiger shark is about four times heavier and has broader cutting teeth, so it gains Attack, Defense and Stamina; the bull shark is more agile in shallow water, and its freshwater ability keeps its Special close.
- **Versus Oceanic Whitetip Shark (A71.0 D53.0 Ag70.0 St80.0 I60.0 Sp76.0):** similar requiem-style hunter, but the tiger shark is over three times heavier and eats harder prey, justifying higher Attack and Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholder values (including Agility above 85) are not used. A famous reputation for attacks does not justify great-white-level Attack or lamnid-level Agility.
