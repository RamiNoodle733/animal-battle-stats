# Yak (*Bos grunniens*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Yak (domestic yak)
- **Scientific name:** *Bos grunniens* Linnaeus, 1766, the domestic yak. The wild yak is treated as a separate species, *Bos mutus* Przewalski, 1883, following the 2003 ICZN ruling that allows that name for wild yaks.
- **Exact animal represented:** the domestic yak, the animal shown in the site's live photo (a horned black yak photographed near Khövsgöl, Mongolia). Wild yak facts are recorded separately below and are not used for the canonical specimen.
- **Status:** Living. Domesticated; the wild yak is Vulnerable.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male (domestic bull), **450.0 kg**, **130.0 cm** at the withers and **160.0 cm** body length (FAO trunk-length convention; see below).
- **Sex choice:** domestic bulls weigh **350–585 kg** against **225–255 kg** for cows (Wikipedia; ADW 350–580 kg), carry longer horns (48–99 cm vs 27–64 cm) and fight during the rut, so a bull is used.
- **Breed variation:** yak breeds differ greatly. FAO measurements of adult bulls in China range from about **235 kg** (Zhongdian) and **264 kg** (Tianzhu White) to **398 kg** (Plateau), **414 kg** (Maiwa) and **594 kg** (Jiulong). Mongolian yaks are mostly polled or dehorned, while Tibetan and Nepalese yaks are nearly all horned. The canonical 450 kg horned bull is a robust but ordinary working bull, not a record animal.

### Wild yak, recorded separately
- Wild yaks (*Bos mutus*) are much larger: adults weigh **0.5–1.2 t**, stand **160–205 cm** at the shoulder and measure **240–380 cm** head-body, with females about one-third the weight of bulls (Wikipedia). ADW gives an average male of about **1,000 kg**.
- FAO cites a wild bull of about **950 kg** and 190 cm at the shoulder, 1.5 times the weight of domestic bulls in the same area, and a Chang Tang sample of 53 bull horns averaging **75.7 cm** along the outer curve.
- FAO: wild yaks are alert and timid and stampede readily, but "if angered or cornered they are fierce and will attack an intruder"; lone bulls have attacked people on remote roads.
- A wild yak bull would rate well above this domestic bull in Size, Raw Power, Attack and Defense.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The yak fights like other wild cattle: head down, horns forward, mass behind. During the rut bulls bellow, scrape the ground with their horns, and compete directly by charging each other with lowered heads and sparring with their horns (Wikipedia). A 450 kg bull with 50–100 cm horns can gore and knock down medium predators. Domestic yaks, however, are bred for tractability and often avoid conflict; many Mongolian yaks have no horns at all.

### Defense and durability
Protection is better than for most hoofed animals of this size. The long hair skirt and dense undercoat pad the body, a thick layer of subcutaneous fat adds insulation and bulk, and the frame is heavy. None of this is armor, and wolves still kill yaks, especially the young, infirm or isolated.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Yaks are prized for moving over dangerous mountain terrain and marshy ground at high elevation, with rounded cloven hooves and sturdy legs. They are sure-footed rather than fast or nimble, and their bulk limits sudden turns.

### Endurance
Endurance at altitude is the yak's great strength. FAO records large chests (14–15 pairs of ribs), large lungs and a large heart: the heart of Zhongdian yaks averaged **1.3 kg**, against **0.87 kg** for local cattle and **0.84 kg** for yak-cattle hybrids. Yak hemoglobin has a high affinity for oxygen, and yaks lack the damaging whole-lung constriction of blood vessels that low oxygen triggers in cattle. Yaks normally live up to about **5,500 m** and are used as pack and riding animals with "high endurance for work," carrying heavy loads relative to their body weight. The limit is heat: yaks have few functional sweat glands, their heart rate and body temperature start to rise at about **16 °C**, and they are prone to heat exhaustion above about 15 °C.

### Senses
FAO describes the wild yak as having a very acute sense of smell and being highly alert. Domestic yaks are nervous of wild animals and people and readily take flight.

### Intelligence and tactics
Yaks rely on herd tactics: FAO notes that they prefer to group in large herds for protection, particularly against wolves, and in snowstorms stand pressed close together. Domestic yaks learn routes and handling well enough to work as pack and riding animals. Individual problem solving is not documented as a strength.

### Social structure and fighting
Wild yaks live in separate female and male herds for most of the year; bulls wander alone or in small groups and join female herds for the rut. Domestic herds are managed by herders, and bulls become aggressive and fight for dominance as the rut approaches.

### Predator defense
Wolves are the main predator of wild and domestic yaks. Snow leopards and Himalayan bears have been reported taking young or infirm wild yaks. Herding together is the main defense; cornered wild yaks attack.

### Environmental strengths
- High altitude, low oxygen and extreme cold, down to −30 to −40 °C.
- Snow, steep slopes, treacherous and marshy terrain.
- Poor-quality winter forage.

### Weaknesses
- Poor heat tolerance; performance drops above about 15 °C.
- Domestic yaks are often docile or polled.
- Not fast; limited agility for its mass.
- No armor on the neck and belly beneath the hair.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** wolves and medium predators in cold uplands; smaller animals that must face the horns; opponents that cannot cope with thin air or cold.
- **Mixed:** big cats and bears of similar weight, especially at lower altitude.
- **Bad:** larger bovids, rhinos and elephants, heavy predators such as tigers, hot lowland environments and aquatic fights.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Thin-Air Engine** — a large chest, large lungs, a heart about 1.5 times the mass of local cattle hearts and oxygen-hungry hemoglobin let the yak work hard where the air holds about a third less oxygen than at sea level.
2. **Lowered-Horn Charge** — bulls charge with heads down and spar with long, outward-sweeping horns, putting 450 kg behind the impact.

### Unique traits
1. **Ground-Sweeping Skirt** — long outer hair hangs below the belly, sometimes to the ground, over a dense winter undercoat and thick fat, protecting the yak in temperatures down to −40 °C.
2. **Grunting Ox** — yaks grunt instead of lowing like cattle, which gave the domestic yak its scientific name *grunniens*, "grunting."

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Domestic yaks live across the Himalayan region, the Tibetan Plateau, the Pamirs, Tajikistan, Mongolia and southern Siberia, mostly above 3,000 m in cold, semi-arid to semi-humid uplands; the further north, the lower the altitude at which they live. Small herds are kept in North America, Europe and New Zealand. Wild yaks survive on treeless uplands of the Tibetan Plateau between about 3,000 and 5,500 m, especially alpine tundra with grasses and sedges.

### Diet and ecology
Grazers of grasses and sedges, with herbs, dwarf shrubs, lichens and mosses. In snow they break through the cover to reach wilted grass. Yaks have a large rumen for fermenting poor forage. Wild yaks are an important prey species for wolves.

### Social structure
Wild yaks live in large female-and-young herds and smaller bull groups; bulls wander alone outside the rut. Domestic herds are managed by herders, with bulls kept for breeding and steers for work.

### Reproduction and life history
Mating occurs mainly from July to September. Gestation lasts about 257–270 days and produces a single calf, which can walk within about ten minutes of birth. Females usually calve every other year, first at three or four years of age.

### Conservation
Domesticated; the domestic yak is common across its range. The wild yak (*Bos mutus*) is Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, with the 2008 assessment suggesting no more than 10,000 mature individuals. Poaching is its most serious threat, followed by interbreeding and disease risk from domestic herds. A 2015 study found female wild yaks now depend on winter snow patches 20 times more than males, and use steeper terrain than in the past.

### Special features
- Large chest, lungs and heart for low-oxygen air.
- Hemoglobin with high oxygen affinity.
- Long hair skirt, dense undercoat and thick subcutaneous fat.
- Few functional sweat glands, conserving heat.
- Rounded hooves suited to steep and marshy ground.

### Human interaction
Yaks have long supplied milk, butter, cheese, meat, fibre and transport to Tibetan, Himalayan and Mongolian herders. Dried yak dung is an important fuel on the treeless Tibetan Plateau. Yak steers carry packs, are ridden and in some areas plough fields, and they accompany trekking and climbing expeditions. The yak genome, sequenced in 2012, shows expanded gene families linked to sensing, energy metabolism and hypoxia.

### Genuine fun facts
- *Bos grunniens* means "grunting ox": yaks grunt rather than moo.
- Przewalski named the wild yak *Bos mutus*, "mute ox," thinking it made no sound, but it does.
- A Zhongdian yak's heart averages about 1.3 kg, half again as heavy as a local cow's.
- Yaks start to overheat at about 16 °C.
- Yaks normally live as high as about 5,500 m.
- Wild yak bulls can weigh about a tonne, roughly one and a half times a domestic bull from the same area.
- Dried yak dung is often the only fuel available on the high Tibetan Plateau.

### Concise site-ready summary
The domestic yak is a 450 kg high-altitude ox with sweeping horns, a ground-length coat and a heart and lungs built for thin air. Bulls charge head-down in the rut and herds bunch against wolves, but domestic yaks are usually docile, slow to turn and badly suited to heat.

### Rich narrative profile
The yak is the cattle of the roof of the world. A domestic bull stands about 130 cm at the withers and weighs around 450 kg, with a hump over the shoulders, long horns sweeping outward and back, and a coat so long that it hangs in a skirt below the belly. Beneath the hair lies a dense undercoat and a thick layer of fat, and inside is an oversized heart and pair of lungs suited to air that holds a third less oxygen than at sea level.

As a fighter, the domestic yak is capable but not eager. Bulls charge and spar with lowered horns during the rut, and a 450 kg charge is dangerous to any wolf-sized predator. But domestic breeding has made most yaks tractable, many Mongolian yaks carry no horns at all, and their first response to danger is to bunch together or run. Their wild relatives are another matter: wild yak bulls weigh about a tonne, carry horns averaging over 75 cm and will attack intruders when cornered.

The yak's real advantage is where it lives. At 4,000 to 5,500 m, in temperatures that fall to −40 °C, it keeps working when lowland cattle struggle to breathe, which is why it scores high in Stamina and Special. Take it down to warm lowlands and it begins to overheat at 16 °C. On the absolute scale it sits among heavy hoofed animals like the musk ox and fighting bull, below the tonne-class bison, buffalo and gaur.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Length convention:** only FAO trunk length (shoulder point to pin bone) was found for domestic bulls; the canonical 160 cm is shorter than a nose-to-rump head-body length would be.
- **Mass:** domestic breeds range from about 235 to 594 kg; 450 kg is a mid-range horned bull and not typical of small breeds such as Mongolian yaks.
- **Heat threshold:** Wikipedia gives about 15 °C, FAO about 16 °C for rising heart rate and body temperature; both are reported.
- **Domestic vs wild:** wild yaks are twice as heavy and more aggressive; their facts are kept in a separate section and not used for the canonical ratings.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Bison (900 kg; Attack 72.0, Defense 68.0, Stamina 74.0):** the bison is twice as heavy and fights harder, so it stays well above in Attack and Defense; the yak's high-altitude physiology gives it higher Stamina (80.0) and Special (72.0 vs 55.0).
- **Versus Musk Ox (320 kg; Attack 64.0, Defense 61.0, Special 74.0):** the yak is heavier (Raw Power 66.0 vs 63.0) but the musk ox has a massive horn boss, a denser coat and a fiercer defensive ring, so it keeps slightly higher Attack and Defense. Both are cold specialists with similar Special scores.
- **Versus Gaur (1,000 kg; Attack 78.0, Defense 72.0):** the gaur is more than twice the mass with thick horns; the yak stays far below in Attack and Defense and above in Stamina and Special. A wild yak bull would sit much closer to the gaur, as the gaur report anticipated.
- **Versus Fighting Bull (520 kg; Attack 71.0, Ferocity 90.0):** the fighting bull is bred for aggression; the docile domestic yak is placed well below in Attack and Ferocity.
- **Versus Camel (550 kg; Stamina 91.0):** the camel's heat and water endurance tops the yak's; the yak dominates in cold and thin air instead.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholders (Stamina 92, Defense 69.8, Abilities 4.6) are not used. The yak's Special is high for real physiology, and its heat intolerance keeps Stamina below the roster's endurance leaders.
