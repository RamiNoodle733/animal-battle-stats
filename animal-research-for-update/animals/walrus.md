# Walrus (*Odobenus rosmarus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Walrus
- **Scientific name:** *Odobenus rosmarus* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** the walrus of Arctic and sub-Arctic seas, treated as one species. The site's live photo is a Pacific walrus bull (*O. r. divergens*), so the canonical specimen follows the Pacific form, with Atlantic data used for range.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult bull of about **1,200 kg** and **300 cm** long, with tusks of about **50–70 cm**.
- **Sex choice:** bulls are about one and a half times as heavy as cows, have longer, straighter and stouter tusks, much thicker neck and shoulder skin covered in bumps ("bosses"), and are the sex that fights with tusks for mates.
- **Population variation:** most Pacific bulls weigh 800–1,700 kg, with outsized animals near 2,000 kg; Atlantic bulls average about 900 kg. The largest reliably measured walrus was a 3.8 m bull of 1,883 kg. Older claims of 2,300 kg animals from hide weights could not be verified.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Wikipedia: Pacific males mostly **800–1,700 kg**, outsized males up to 2,000 kg; Atlantic males average about **900 kg**; females about two-thirds of male mass (Atlantic about 560 kg, Pacific about 800 kg).
- ADW: males **1,200–1,500 kg**, females 600–850 kg; species range 600–1,500 kg.
- SeaWorld: Pacific males **800–1,700 kg**, females 400–1,250 kg.
- McClain et al. (2015, PeerJ): heaviest verified walrus **1,883 kg** at 3.8 m.
- **Canonical:** **1,200.0 kg** adult Pacific bull, mid-range and well below verified maxima.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Height

- **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0** (not applicable).

### Length
- SeaWorld: Pacific males **2.7–3.6 m**; ADF&G: males 7–12 ft (2.1–3.7 m); ADW: males up to 320 cm.
- McClain et al.: most reliable maximum length 3.95 m.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 300.0 cm.**
- **Confidence:** High.

### Speed
- SeaWorld: normal swimming speed about **7 km/h**, with short bursts up to **35 km/h** (9.7 m/s).
- On land walruses turn their hind flippers forward and move on all fours, slowly.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 9.7 m/s** in water (burst), from a zoological institution rather than an instrumented trial.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-low.

### Lifespan
- Wikipedia: about **20–30 years** in the wild; ADW: 30–40 years; ADF&G and SeaWorld: up to 40 years. The captive record is 49 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 30.0**, where the wild ranges meet. Many bulls die from fight injuries, so male averages are probably lower.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Tusks, suction and bite
- **Tusks:** elongated upper canines in both sexes, growing for about 15 years and longer in males. They reach **1 m** and **5.4 kg** (Wikipedia), up to 100 cm in males and 80 cm in females (SeaWorld), averaging about **50 cm** (ADW). Bulls' tusks are longer, straighter and stouter.
- **Suction:** a vaulted palate and piston-like tongue produce oral pressures as low as **−87.9 kPa in air and −118.8 kPa underwater** (Kastelein and co-workers, cited by Wikipedia). Captive walruses sucked a 2.3 kg metal plug out of a pool floor.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Apart from the tusks, the teeth are flat and used for crushing and sucking out clams. No bite-force measurement exists, and suction pressure is not a bite.

### Defensive structure
- **Skin:** usually **2–4 cm** thick (ADW) and up to **10 cm** around the neck and shoulders of bulls (Wikipedia), where it protects against tusk jabs from rivals (SeaWorld). A bull's hide is about 20% of its body weight.
- **Blubber:** up to **10 cm** (SeaWorld) or 15 cm (Wikipedia) thick, and up to a third of body mass in winter.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The walrus's weapons are its tusks, driven downward by a massive neck and a body of more than a tonne. Bulls threaten by raising and turning their heads to show their tusks; when tusks are matched, they fight and stab each other, causing bruises, punctures and lacerations on the neck and shoulders. SeaWorld notes that many males die each year from injuries received in breeding-season fights. Against predators, adults use the tusks as weapons: walruses have fatally injured polar bears that followed them into the water, and are believed to have fought off orcas. Walruses occasionally kill and eat seals, including bearded seals of up to about 200 kg, and rarely seabirds. The weakness is delivery: the tusks strike mainly downward and forward, and on land the walrus is slow and heavy.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
In water a walrus swims with sinuous whole-body strokes powered by the hind flippers, steering with the fore flippers; it cruises at about 7 km/h and bursts to about 35 km/h. When feeding it hovers head-down, rests its tusks on the seabed like sledge runners and fans sediment away with a flipper. On land and ice it can walk on all four flippers, better than true seals, and it uses its tusks to haul itself out onto ice.

### Endurance
Walruses feed in dives that usually last about 5–10 minutes (ADW gives 5–20; SeaWorld documents one of 25 minutes), mostly in water shallower than 80 m, although dives beyond 500 m have been recorded in Smith Sound. They shift with the seasonal sea ice across the Bering and Chukchi Seas, rest on ice floes or beaches between feeding trips, and can sleep afloat by inflating air sacs in the throat. They are built for sustained foraging over wide areas rather than deep, long dives like an elephant seal.

### Senses
The mystacial vibrissae are the walrus's standout sense: 400–700 stiff whiskers in 13–15 rows, up to 30 cm long, richly supplied with nerves and able to tell apart shapes 3 mm thick and 2 mm wide. Walruses actively orient and position these whiskers, including asymmetric head turning while sweeping for food. Vision is used at close range but the seabed is often murky, so touch dominates.

### Intelligence and tactics
Walruses solve the problem of buried food with several techniques: beating sediment away with the right or left flipper, blasting it with a water jet from the mouth, and rooting with the muzzle, with a measurable preference for the right flipper. Dominance is mostly settled by tusk display without fighting. Herds respond to threats by rushing to the water together. These show flexible foraging and social signalling, not complex predatory tactics.

### Sociality and hunting
Highly gregarious. Walruses haul out in dense herds of hundreds to thousands on ice floes and beaches. Hierarchies are based on age, body size and tusk length. The walrus is a benthic forager on more than 60 genera of invertebrates, especially clams, which it sucks out of their shells; an adult eats about 25 kg of small benthic animals per day (ADW).

### Fighting and aggression
Bulls compete fiercely in the breeding season, and cows also jab each other with tusks over haul-out space. Adults defend calves, and a cornered or wounded walrus is described as a formidable opponent even for a polar bear. But the default response of a herd to disturbance is flight into the sea, which can itself kill hundreds to thousands of animals in stampedes.

### Predator defense
Only orcas and polar bears prey on walruses, and both mostly take calves, injured or weak animals. Polar bears usually rush beached herds and eat those crushed in the stampede; direct attacks on healthy adults are rare. Orcas regularly attack walruses, sometimes without injury to themselves, but walruses are believed to defend themselves successfully by counterattacking.

### Environmental strengths
- Shallow Arctic shelf seas with pack ice, where it forages, rests and escapes.
- Water, where a polar bear loses its advantage and walrus tusks become more dangerous.
- Crowded haul-outs, where numbers and bulk deter land predators.

### Weaknesses
- Slow and clumsy on land; charges are short.
- Tusks mainly strike downward, with limited reach to the sides.
- Short dives compared with deep-diving seals.
- Herds panic into stampedes that crush calves and small animals.
- Calves and injured adults are vulnerable to orcas and bears.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** land predators that follow it into water, medium seals, animals that must attack its thick-skinned neck and shoulders.
- **Mixed:** polar bears on land or ice; large bulls of its own kind.
- **Bad:** orca pods, much larger marine animals, fast agile predators that avoid the tusks, and fights far from water.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Ivory Tusk Stab** — tusks up to about 1 m, driven down by more than a tonne of body, stab rivals and predators; walruses have fatally injured polar bears in the water.
2. **Vacuum Suction Feeding** — a vaulted palate and piston-like tongue produce mouth pressures as low as −118.8 kPa underwater, enough to suck clams from their shells and a 2.3 kg plug out of a pool floor.

### Unique traits
1. **Whisker Carpet** — 400–700 stiff, nerve-rich whiskers up to 30 cm long can tell apart shapes just 3 mm thick in dark, murky water.
2. **Tusk-Walker Hide** — walruses haul themselves onto ice with their tusks, and bulls carry neck and shoulder skin up to 10 cm thick that shields them from tusk jabs.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Arctic and sub-Arctic seas around the North Pole. The Pacific walrus lives in the Bering and Chukchi Seas, summering north of the Bering Strait around Wrangel Island and the coasts of Siberia and Alaska. The Atlantic walrus ranges from the Canadian Arctic across Greenland, Svalbard and the Barents and Kara Seas. It uses shallow continental shelf waters close to sea ice or coasts where it can haul out.

### Diet and ecology
A benthic forager on more than 60 genera of marine animals, mainly clams, plus snails, worms, crabs, shrimp, sea cucumbers and slow fish, occasionally seals and seabirds. It locates prey with its whiskers, clears sediment with water jets and flipper strokes, and sucks the soft parts out of shells. Feeding on the seabed stirs large areas of sediment.

### Social structure
Gregarious, forming herds of hundreds to thousands on ice floes and beaches. Hierarchies depend on age, body size and tusk length, and the strongest bulls with the largest tusks dominate.

### Reproduction and life history
Females begin breeding at about 6–7 years and usually give birth every two to three years, the lowest reproductive rate of any pinniped. Calves weigh 45–75 kg at birth, can swim immediately, and stay with the mother for about two years, the longest mother-calf bond among pinnipeds. Males are mature at about 8–10 years but rarely win mates before about 15 years, when they are fully grown.

### Conservation
Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List (2016). Commercial hunting in the 18th–20th centuries cut populations severely; the Pacific population was estimated at about 129,000 in 2006. The main modern threat is loss of summer sea ice, which forces walruses onto crowded coastal haul-outs where disturbance-caused stampedes can kill hundreds to thousands in a year. Indigenous subsistence hunting continues under management.

### Special features
- Tusks in both sexes, up to about 1 m and 5.4 kg.
- 400–700 whiskers in 13–15 rows.
- Neck and shoulder skin up to 10 cm thick in bulls; blubber up to 10–15 cm.
- Oral suction as low as −118.8 kPa underwater.
- Throat air sacs for floating and sleeping upright in water.
- Can walk on all four flippers.

### Human interaction
Walrus meat, fat, skin, bone and ivory have been central to many Arctic Indigenous cultures, and walrus ivory was a prized trade good in medieval Europe. Aircraft, boats, tourists and hunters can trigger deadly stampedes at haul-outs, so managers now protect major haul-out sites from disturbance.

### Genuine fun facts
- The name *Odobenus* means "tooth-walker", after walruses using their tusks to pull themselves out of the water.
- A walrus can suck the meat out of a clam and even sucked a 2.3 kg metal plug out of a pool floor.
- Its whiskers can tell apart objects only 3 mm thick.
- A bull's hide can make up about 20% of its body weight.
- Walruses sleep floating upright by inflating air sacs in their throats.
- Walruses show a preference for the right flipper when digging for food.
- Mother and calf stay together for about two years, longer than any other pinniped pair.

### Concise site-ready summary
The walrus is a 1.2-tonne Arctic giant armed with ivory tusks up to a metre long, protected by skin up to 10 cm thick and blubber, and equipped with a carpet of super-sensitive whiskers. Slow on land but dangerous in water, it can fatally wound a polar bear, though herds usually flee from danger.

### Rich narrative profile
A walrus bull is a mountain of wrinkled brown skin: around 1,200 kg, three metres long, with a broad whiskered muzzle and two ivory tusks that can reach a metre. Its hide alone makes up about a fifth of its weight, and on the neck and shoulders it thickens to 10 cm, a shield against the tusks of rival bulls that fight for mates every breeding season and leave each other scarred and sometimes dying.

Most of its life, though, is spent feeding. A walrus dives to the seabed, rests its tusks on the mud like sledge runners, sweeps the bottom with hundreds of sensitive whiskers, blasts sediment away with jets of water or strokes of a flipper, and sucks clams out of their shells with suction strong enough to pull a metal plug from a pool floor. Between feeding trips it hauls out on ice floes or beaches in herds of thousands, levering itself up with its tusks.

On the ABS scale the walrus is a heavy tank with a genuinely dangerous weapon. Polar bears prefer to stampede herds and pick off the crushed and weak, because a healthy adult walrus in the water can kill a bear. Its Defense and Raw Power rank among the roster's large marine mammals, but its slowness on land, short reach and tendency to flee keep its Attack and Agility well below the elite predators.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** hide-based claims of 2,300 kg are unverified; 1,883 kg is the verified maximum. The canonical 1,200 kg is a typical bull.
- **Blubber:** SeaWorld gives up to 10 cm and Wikipedia 15 cm; ADW's 25.4 cm figure is an outlier and is not used.
- **Diving:** most dives are shallow (under 80 m); 500 m dives in Smith Sound depend on local seabed depth.
- **Speed:** 35 km/h bursts come from SeaWorld without method detail.
- **Lifespan:** 20–30 years (Wikipedia) vs 30–40 years (ADW); 30 is a compromise and bulls likely die younger.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Elephant Seal (3,000 kg; A64 D76 Ag42 St90 I58 Sp82):** as the elephant seal report anticipated, the tusked walrus gets the better weapon (Weaponry 70.0 vs 52.0; Attack 67.0 vs 64.0) while the far heavier elephant seal keeps higher Defense, Raw Power and much higher Stamina for its extreme dives and migrations. The walrus walks on four flippers and is lighter, so Agility is slightly higher (43.0).
- **Versus Leopard Seal (420 kg; A70 D55 Ag78 St70 I68 Sp74):** the leopard seal is a fast, agile active predator with big jaws and stays above the walrus in Attack and Agility. The walrus is nearly three times heavier with thick neck skin and blubber, so Defense (74.0 vs 55.0) and Raw Power are much higher, as the leopard seal report expected.
- **Versus Polar Bear (500 kg; A78 D72 Ag62 St88 I73 Sp77):** polar bears prey mostly on walrus calves and stampede victims, and walruses can kill bears in the water. The bear keeps higher Attack, Agility, Stamina and Intelligence as the roster's Arctic hunter; the walrus is slightly higher in Defense and Raw Power from its mass and hide.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the legacy Attack 1.7 and Agility 8.3 were placeholders and are replaced; the legacy Protection 94 is reduced to 70.0, because thick skin and blubber are strong but not armor-plate or elephant-level protection.
