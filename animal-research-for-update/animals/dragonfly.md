# Dragonfly research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Site animal:** Dragonfly
- **Standardized taxon for this report:** Common green darner, *Anax junius* (Drury, 1773), family Aeshnidae, order Odonata. The live page currently identifies only the infraorder `Anisoptera`; a species-level canonical taxon is necessary for defensible physical fields. *A. junius* is selected as a large, widespread, well-studied North American dragonfly with unusually strong migration and sensory literature.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male common green darner. Males are the preferred fighting specimen because they actively patrol breeding habitat and engage in aerial territorial/reproductive interactions. This is not a claim that males are universally larger than females.
- **Dimorphism:** mature males typically have a green thorax and bright blue abdominal markings; females are generally duller brown/orange to variable blue. NPS gives adult abdomen length 68-78 mm and wingspan 9-10 cm without a sex-specific size range.
- **Population/life-history variation:** migratory and resident generations differ strongly in movement ecology. A migration maximum must not be treated as routine local flight performance.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Body size
NPS describes adult *A. junius* as a large-bodied darner with a 68-78 mm abdomen and 9-10 cm wingspan. University of Minnesota independently describes the species as about 3 inches long. A secondary synthesis reports roughly 7-10 cm overall length and about 1 g mass, but the mass estimate is much weaker than the NPS linear measurements.

**Representative adult male recommendation:**
- mass: **0.0010 kg (1.0 g)**, low-to-moderate confidence because a strong primary sex-specific adult mass series was not found
- total body length: **7.6 cm**, moderate-high confidence
- wingspan: **9.5 cm**, high confidence for representative placement within NPS range
- standing height: not biologically useful for this insect and not reliably measured

### Flight speed
A 2019 telemetry study tracked migrating *A. junius* and recorded a maximum estimated wind-assisted groundspeed of **77 km/h (21.5 m/s)** and a maximum estimated single-day movement of **122 km**. This is not a clean self-powered airspeed maximum. Therefore the canonical `speed_mps` remains **0.0** rather than converting a wind-assisted groundspeed into intrinsic top speed.

### Lifespan
Animal Diversity Web reports adults likely live several weeks to a little over a month and gives an average adult lifespan of about **1 month**. This report uses **0.083 years (1 month)** as the adult canonical lifespan, while explicitly separating the much longer aquatic nymphal development from adult life.

### Bite force and weapons
No defensible *A. junius* bite-force pressure measurement was found. `bite_force_psi` remains **0.0**. Adult dragonflies seize prey with the legs in flight and process it with robust biting mouthparts, but internet PSI claims are not acceptable evidence.

Natural weapons and capture structures:
- six spiny legs form an aerial prey-catching basket
- biting mandibles process captured arthropod prey
- four independently controlled wings provide exceptional interception geometry and rapid repositioning
- large compound eyes dominate the head and provide broad visual coverage and specialized high-acuity regions

### Defensive structures
The chitinous exoskeleton gives basic arthropod protection, but at roughly one gram the adult has extremely little absolute armor or trauma tolerance against larger roster animals. Flight and detection are the real defenses.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offensive mechanics
The adult is an aerial insect predator. It approaches flying prey on an interception course, captures the target with its legs, and bites while airborne or perched. Its practical offense is therefore not raw force but precision delivery against insects and similarly tiny targets. Against vertebrate-scale opponents, the damage ceiling is negligible.

Dragonfly interception research on other anisopteran species is relevant to the flight-control mechanism but is not silently treated as an *A. junius*-specific measurement. Motion-capture work in *Plathemis lydia* found predictive head rotations and steering consistent with internal models of both body and prey motion. Earlier pursuit experiments likewise found dragonflies steering toward prey interception points rather than simply chasing the prey's current location.

### Defense and durability
Absolute defense is very low. The exoskeleton protects against minor contact and the insect can evade attacks in three dimensions, but the thin abdomen, delicate wings, and tiny mass make mechanical damage severe. Loss or major damage to a wing is a major combat limitation.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
This is the dragonfly's strongest conventional combat category. ADW notes that the four wings move independently, permitting forward and backward flight, hovering, and rapid directional changes. The species is a strong flier but weather-sensitive. Strong wind can blow individuals off course.

### Endurance
Common green darners are exceptional insect migrants. Radio-tagged animals in a 2006 study advanced an average 58 ± 11 km over 6.1 ± 0.9 days, averaging 11.9 ± 2.8 km/day, with migration days separated by stopovers. A 2019 tracking study recorded up to 122 km in a day. Stable-isotope work across 852 wing samples and 21 years of citizen-science data showed a multi-generational annual migration averaging more than 600 km, with some inferred movements exceeding 2,500 km. These feats justify very high insect-scale endurance but not vertebrate-like continuous fighting endurance. Stopovers and weather dependence matter.

### Senses
Vision is elite. Sherk's adult-eye work found specialized foveal regions and, specifically in migratory *A. junius*, large dorsal ommatidia covering about one third of the eye surface and viewing a narrow cross-dorsal region. Dragonfly target-tracking neurobiology also demonstrates highly specialized small-target motion processing. This is a major matchup advantage against tiny moving prey.

### Intelligence and tactics
Dragonflies should not be given corvid-like general intelligence. However, their sensorimotor control is much more sophisticated than a simple reflex stereotype. Peer-reviewed interception studies show predictive target tracking, stable foveation, and course construction. The Intelligence rating therefore rewards specialized tactical computation while remaining far below the Crow's experimentally broad learning and memory profile.

### Hunting and fighting behavior
Adults hunt flying insects including flies, gnats, moths, butterflies, mayflies, damselflies, mosquitoes, and smaller dragonflies. Males patrol aquatic breeding areas. In combat terms the species favors detection, interception, rapid capture, and immediate disengagement rather than grappling with large opponents.

### Environmental strengths
- open air and pond margins where three-dimensional flight is available
- warm daylight conditions supporting flight-muscle performance
- target-rich airspace with small flying arthropods

### Environmental limitations
- cold, rain, and strong adverse winds reduce effective flight
- confined spaces remove much of the maneuverability advantage
- water immersion is dangerous to the aerial adult despite aquatic juvenile history
- wing damage sharply reduces mobility

### Matchup archetypes
**Good:** mosquitoes, flies, small moths, small flying insects, other tiny arthropods that can be intercepted and physically controlled.

**Bad:** virtually any substantially larger vertebrate or robust arthropod that cannot be controlled by the leg basket; web-builders or ambush predators that negate open-air maneuvering; opponents in tight terrain where flight geometry is constrained.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings

- **Attack: 8.0** - highly efficient insect predator, but one-gram scale prevents meaningful absolute damage against most of the roster.
- **Defense: 5.0** - evasion is excellent but belongs mainly under Agility; structural protection is minimal.
- **Agility: 98.0** - one of the roster's strongest candidates for an agility ceiling because of hovering, backward flight, rapid vector changes, and interception precision.
- **Stamina: 80.0** - direct migration telemetry supports very strong sustained movement, moderated by stopovers and weather dependence.
- **Intelligence: 55.0** - sophisticated predictive sensorimotor control, but no basis for crow-like domain-general cognition.
- **Special: 82.0** - exceptional visual target acquisition plus four-wing aerial control creates a rare and meaningful biological toolkit.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Predictive Intercept** - dragonfly prey capture uses interception geometry and predictive head/body control rather than simple tail-chasing, enabling precise attacks on moving aerial prey.
2. **Four-Wing Vectoring** - independently controlled fore- and hindwings permit hovering, backward flight, sharp directional changes, and unusually precise three-dimensional repositioning.

### Unique traits
1. **Darner Sky-Fovea** - *A. junius* has a specialized adult compound-eye architecture, including large dorsal ommatidia occupying roughly one third of the eye surface and viewing a narrow cross-dorsal region.
2. **Continental Relay Migrant** - the species' annual cycle uses multiple generations to span continental-scale movements, with average inferred migration over 600 km and some individuals exceeding 2,500 km.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Common green darners occur broadly across North America and south into Central America, breeding around ponds, marshes, and slow or still freshwater. Migrants can appear far from breeding water, including urban areas.

### Diet and ecology
Adults are generalist aerial predators of flying insects. Nymphs are aquatic predators and can consume aquatic insects, tadpoles, and small fish. This ontogenetic shift means the species occupies two very different predatory niches across its life cycle.

### Social structure and life history
Adults are not cooperative pack hunters. They may occur in aggregations or migratory movements, and males patrol breeding habitat. Reproduction occurs around freshwater; females insert eggs into aquatic vegetation, sometimes while paired in tandem with a male. The aquatic nymph molts repeatedly before the winged adult emerges.

### Conservation
The common green darner is widespread and is generally treated as Least Concern. Wetland quality remains ecologically important because reproduction depends on freshwater habitat.

### Adaptations
- independently controlled wings and powerful flight musculature
- large compound eyes with behaviorally specialized visual regions
- target-motion neural processing optimized for tiny moving prey
- thermoregulatory behavior supporting flight across variable conditions
- migratory behavior responsive to weather and temperature cues

### Human interaction
Harmless to people and beneficial as a predator of many flying insects. It does not possess a medically significant sting or venom system.

### Genuine fun facts
- Radio-tagged common green darners have been followed using aircraft and ground teams despite the insect's tiny body size.
- One telemetry study estimated a 122 km single-day movement for a green darner.
- Stable-isotope research reconstructed migration using chemistry preserved in dragonfly wings.
- The annual migratory cycle is multi-generational rather than one individual making the entire round trip.
- Adult dragonflies can hover and fly backward.
- The adult aerial predator and aquatic nymph are both hunters but use radically different bodies and environments.

### Concise site summary
A one-gram aerial interceptor with elite vision and near-ceiling maneuverability, the common green darner wins by seeing tiny moving targets early, predicting their path, and vectoring into them with four independently controlled wings. Its continental migration gives it exceptional insect endurance, but its absolute attack and structural defense remain tiny against most of the ABS roster.

### Rich narrative profile
The common green darner is a useful stress test for absolute ABS scaling. Relative to its size it is a spectacular predator, yet a one-gram insect cannot be allowed to inherit mammal-level Attack or Defense merely because it dominates mosquito-sized prey. Its real roster value lies elsewhere. Large compound eyes and specialized visual regions continuously extract target motion, while the flight system can hover, reverse, turn sharply, and steer toward interception points. Research on dragonfly interception shows that these maneuvers can include predictive control rather than only reflexive pursuit.

The species also has a second, very different claim to exceptional performance: migration. Radio telemetry has followed individual common green darners for days, and isotope work shows that the annual cycle spans hundreds to thousands of kilometers across generations. That supports high Stamina without pretending a migration day equals continuous combat output. In a matchup against tiny aerial prey, the darner is a precision hunter. Against larger animals, the same exquisite control mostly becomes an escape system because the animal lacks the mass, armor, and weapon scale to inflict serious damage.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** the ~1 g value is plausible and widely repeated but lacks the same quality of sex-specific primary support as the linear dimensions. Treat 0.0010 kg as provisional.
- **Speed:** the strongest direct *A. junius* tracking number is wind-assisted groundspeed. It must not become canonical intrinsic top speed. `speed_mps` stays 0.0.
- **Lifespan:** adult lifespan and total life cycle are different concepts. The canonical value here is adult lifespan because the combat specimen is the adult.
- **Cognition:** predictive-interception experiments are primarily from other dragonfly species. They support Anisoptera-level tactical mechanisms, not a direct claim that *A. junius* was the experimental species.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Crow:** Dragonfly Agility (98.0) and specialized Senses (96.0) can exceed Crow, but Intelligence (55.0), Attack (8.0), and Defense (5.0) must remain far lower. This prevents impressive insect sensorimotor control from becoming generalized cognition or physical power.
- **Versus Deathstalker Scorpion:** Dragonfly has dramatically better aerial maneuverability and migration endurance but lacks the scorpion's medically significant venom system. Therefore its Attack is lower despite superior mobility.
- **Versus Cuttlefish:** both show sophisticated sensorimotor systems, but cuttlefish has greater behavioral flexibility, camouflage machinery, and absolute mass. Dragonfly wins Agility, not overall combat capability.
- **Versus Donkey/Cougar:** there must be an enormous Attack/Defense gap. Aerial evasion does not make a one-gram insect structurally durable.
- **Inflation check:** the old live page's Protection 66.3 and Recovery 94.0 are incompatible with absolute scaling and are rejected. Near-ceiling Agility is retained only because that category explicitly measures maneuverability rather than force or size.
