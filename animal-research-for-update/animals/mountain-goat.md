# Mountain Goat (*Oreamnos americanus*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Mountain Goat / Rocky Mountain Goat
- **Scientific name:** *Oreamnos americanus* (Blainville, 1816)
- **Taxon represented:** the living North American mountain goat, family Bovidae, subfamily Caprinae. It is not a true goat of genus *Capra*.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime mature **male (billy)** from the native northern/western North American range, in normal adult condition with intact horns. Males are the appropriate combat representative because adult males are substantially heavier than females, and sexually active males fight and use aggressive displays for breeding dominance.
- **Canonical mass assumption:** **110.0 kg**. The US Forest Service synthesis gives midsummer adult male mass of 95-155 kg, while ADW gives a lower 61.4-81.8 kg range from other source material. This is meaningful geographic/source variation. 110 kg is a conservative large, representative mature-billy value inside the stronger Forest Service adult range, not a record specimen.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** adult males are larger. USFS reports 95-155 kg adult males versus 60-75 kg females in a reviewed midsummer dataset. ADW reports 61.4-81.8 kg males and 56.8-70.5 kg females, demonstrating population/source variation. Males also have thicker horn bases and greater backward horn curvature; horn-growth research found males have thicker horns at all ages and that body-mass dimorphism continues after most horn-length growth is complete.
- **Population variation:** northern populations generally show greater sexual size dimorphism than southern populations. Therefore a single canonical mass should not be read as a species-wide mean.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- US Forest Service FEIS synthesis: adult midsummer males **95-155 kg**, females **60-75 kg**.
- Animal Diversity Web: males **61.4-81.8 kg**, females **56.8-70.5 kg**.
- The discrepancy likely reflects population/source differences and illustrates why a record-like 150+ kg male should not be treated as ordinary everywhere.
- **Recommended canonical male mass: 110.0 kg**, moderate-high confidence as a representative large mature male rather than a species-wide average.

### Height and length
- ADW reports body length **124.5-178.7 cm**.
- Multiple zoological summaries place shoulder height around **90-110 cm**.
- **Recommended canonical shoulder height: 100.0 cm.**
- **Recommended canonical head-body length: 165.0 cm.** A small 10-20 cm tail exists, but the site's `length_cm` is best kept as representative body length rather than inflating it with an uncertain tail midpoint.

### Horns
- NPS: both sexes have permanent black horns about **8-12 in (20.3-30.5 cm)** long.
- ADW: approximately **200-300 mm**.
- Journal of Mammalogy study of 135 marked goats found about **93% of horn growth completed by age 3**; males had thicker horns at all ages. Adult horn size correlates with body mass and other body-size measures.
- Horns are slender, sharp, permanent weapons rather than annually shed antlers.

### Speed
- No primary or strong agency source located in this run provided a defensible species-specific physiological top running speed.
- Tertiary web values around 25 km/h exist, but they are not adequate to establish a canonical maximum.
- **Recommended `speed_mps`: 0.0 pending a defensible measured maximum.** This does not mean the animal cannot run quickly; it means the requested maximum-speed field is unresolved.

### Lifespan
- ADW reports typical wild lifespan **12-15 years**, with oldest reported male **15.5 years** and female **18 years**.
- **Recommended canonical lifespan: 14.0 years**, representing a healthy adult species expectation rather than the longevity record.

### Bite force
- The mouth is not the primary combat weapon and no reliable species-specific bite pressure measurement was located.
- **Recommended `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No force-to-pressure conversion is fabricated.

### Coat, hooves, and structural defenses
- The white double coat combines a dense undercoat with long, coarse/hollow guard hairs. NPS reports winter guard hair approaching 8 inches.
- Hooves are large and oval with a hard outer rim and pliable, rubber-like inner pad. This architecture supplies grip on steep rock. Dewclaws and widely spreading toes add purchase on irregular surfaces.
- The compact, stout bovid body and powerful limbs provide meaningful impact resistance, but the species has no armor, shell, or unusually thick hide comparable with heavy megafauna.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons
The principal weapons are the paired permanent black horns. They are relatively short compared with ibex horns but sharply pointed, structurally robust at the base, and mounted on a stout head/neck complex. Adult males use fighting and aggressive displays to establish breeding dominance. The horns are better understood as close-range stabbing, hooking, threatening, and contact weapons than as giant ramming structures.

### Secondary weapons
Body mass, shoulder drive, forelimb power, hooves, and the ability to shove or displace an opponent supplement the horns. Kicks can matter at close range, but there is not strong evidence to score the hooves as specialized offensive weapons comparable with equid kicks.

### Offensive mechanics
A mountain goat's practical attack is a close-range combination of rush, body pressure, horn presentation, hooking/stabbing contact, and positional control. ADW records present threat, horn threat, rush threat, and orientation threat. Many disputes are resolved through display, but sexually active males do fight for dominance and access to females.

### Defense and durability
Defense comes primarily from 110 kg-class body mass, compact construction, thick winter pelage, balance, and the ability to retreat into terrain that many predators cannot safely follow. The coat is excellent thermal protection but only modest mechanical armor. The species should therefore not receive a heavy-armor Defense score simply because it survives severe alpine weather.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
This is the species' signature combat-relevant strength. Large oval hooves with hard rims and flexible inner pads permit secure purchase on steep, broken rock. Mountain goats habitually use cliffs, ledges, talus, and rugged escape terrain. Their ability to choose footing, change elevation, and occupy narrow ledges gives exceptional terrain-specific maneuverability even though no reliable maximum sprint speed is available.

### Endurance and recovery
Mountain goats move seasonally between ranges, forage in rugged high-elevation terrain, and can traverse difficult slopes repeatedly. This supports strong general endurance. It does not justify elite pursuit-running stamina. Harsh winter energetics, deep snow, and poor forage can strongly constrain them, so stamina should remain below long-distance cursorial specialists.

### Senses
Vision, hearing, olfaction, and chemical communication are all behaviorally relevant. Their predator-avoidance strategy depends on detecting threats early enough to reach escape terrain. No extraordinary sensory mechanism such as echolocation or electroreception is present.

### Intelligence and tactics
Mountain goats maintain dominance hierarchies, select escape terrain strategically, alter habitat use with predation risk and snow conditions, and use graded threat displays. This indicates useful spatial and social decision-making but not primate/corvid-level flexible cognition.

### Fighting behavior
USFS reports that sexually active males fight and use aggressive displays to gain dominance and mating access; larger males are usually dominant and mate more often. ADW notes high intraspecific aggression but also emphasizes that many threats are bluffs and serious injuries are uncommon. The combat profile should therefore recognize genuine fighting capability without portraying every encounter as a lethal charge.

### Predation and predator defense
Cougars are a major predator capable of killing adults. Bears and wolves can also attack mountain goats, while eagles mainly threaten newborns. The central defense is terrain selection: Forest Service synthesis reports mountain goats commonly stay close to steep, broken escape terrain and move there when approached by large mammalian predators.

### Environmental advantages
- Steep cliffs, ledges, broken rock, talus, alpine slopes, snowfields, and narrow elevation changes strongly favor the mountain goat.
- Cold conditions favor its dense double coat.
- High ground can make frontal approaches costly and restrict an opponent's attack angle.

### Environmental disadvantages
- Flat, open ground removes much of the species' signature escape and positional advantage.
- Deep or crusted snow can limit movement and forage access.
- Heat reduces activity.
- Forest/krummholz can increase ambush risk because visibility and escape routes are reduced.

### Major weaknesses
- No heavy armor.
- Horns are dangerous but short compared with many large bovids and caprines.
- Limited ability to finish much larger opponents.
- Terrain specialization loses value on flat ground.
- Winter nutritional stress can reduce condition.
- Cougars demonstrate that a sufficiently capable ambush predator can overcome an adult despite climbing skill.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favorable:** similarly sized opponents with poorer footing on broken cliffs; small-to-medium terrestrial attackers forced to approach uphill or across narrow ledges; animals unable to match vertical repositioning.

**Unfavorable:** large cats that can ambush and climb; much larger bovids/megafauna on flat ground; opponents with superior reach and mass; sustained pursuit in terrain without escape cliffs.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings

- **Attack: 51.0** - a 110 kg adult male with sharp permanent horns, body drive, and genuine male-male fighting is dangerous at close range, but its weapons and mass remain well below large bovids and megafaunal attackers.
- **Defense: 42.0** - compact build, thick coat, sure footing, and escape-terrain access improve survival, but there is no armor and cougars can kill adults.
- **Agility: 88.0** - elite balance and control on steep broken rock. This score reflects maneuverability, not an unsupported top-speed number.
- **Stamina: 70.0** - strong sustained alpine movement and harsh-environment capacity, without evidence for elite cursorial endurance.
- **Intelligence: 62.0** - useful spatial judgment, social hierarchy, threat communication, and predator-risk decisions, below highly flexible primates and cooperative carnivores.
- **Special: 78.0** - specialized hoof architecture plus cliff escape/positioning can fundamentally change a terrain-based matchup. Special is not increased merely for cold tolerance or body size.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Cliff Lock** - hard hoof rims, flexible inner pads, spreading toes, and dewclaw support allow exceptional grip and precise repositioning on steep broken rock, giving the goat access to routes and stances many opponents cannot safely use.
2. **Escape-Terrain Mastery** - mountain goats behaviorally select cliffs and ledges as predator refuge and rapidly exploit elevation and narrow footing when threatened, converting habitat knowledge into a practical defensive advantage.

### Unique traits
1. **Permanent Black Daggers** - both sexes retain slender black horns year-round; mature males have particularly thick horn bases and use the horns in dominance contests.
2. **Alpine Double Coat** - a dense undercoat beneath long hollow/coarse guard hairs protects the animal during severe cold and wind, supporting year-round occupation of exposed mountain habitat.

## 8. Expanded profile content

### Habitat
Alpine and subalpine mountains, cliffs, talus, rocky ledges, open meadows, treeline outcrops, and seasonally lower forested or shrubby winter areas. Security terrain is a central habitat requirement.

### Geographic range
Native to mountainous northwestern North America, including Alaska and western Canada and extending south through portions of the northwestern United States and Rockies. Some populations outside the core native range were introduced.

### Diet and ecology
Mountain goats are flexible herbivorous mixed feeders. USFS documents grasses, forbs, shrubs, tree leaves/stems/bark, lichens, mosses, ferns, roots, bulbs, and other available vegetation. Winter diets often shift toward browse as snow limits herbaceous forage. Mineral licks are important seasonal resources.

### Social structure
Sociality varies seasonally. Females and young commonly associate in groups, while adult males may be solitary or in small bachelor associations outside the rut. Dominance hierarchies are well developed. During breeding, large males compete for females and dominant males tend to secure more mating opportunities.

### Reproduction and life history
The rut peaks in late autumn, chiefly November in much of the range. Males may tend individual estrous females. Kids are born in spring on secure rugged terrain. Mountain goats are relatively slow-lived alpine ungulates, and population recovery can therefore be sensitive to adult survival.

### Conservation
The species remains broadly established across western North America and is generally treated as Least Concern globally, but local populations can be vulnerable to disturbance, harvest, habitat change, disease, and climate-driven changes in alpine environments. Management must be population-specific.

### Major adaptations
- High-traction specialized hooves.
- Powerful limbs and balance for steep terrain.
- Dense insulating double coat.
- Permanent horns and social threat displays.
- Strong behavioral preference for predator escape terrain.
- Seasonal diet flexibility and mineral-seeking behavior.

### Human interaction
Mountain goats can habituate to people at heavily visited sites and may seek salt associated with human activity. They remain large horned wild bovids and should not be approached. Human recreation, aircraft, roads, and other disturbance can alter movement and increase stress in sensitive populations.

### Genuine fun facts
- Mountain goats are bovids but are not true goats of genus *Capra*.
- Both sexes grow permanent horns, and horn annuli can help estimate age.
- A marked-goat study found roughly 93% of horn length growth is already complete by age three.
- Their hooves combine a hard rim with a softer, pliable central pad, functioning like a natural high-traction climbing system.
- Forest Service habitat synthesis found predator avoidance can outweigh food acquisition in habitat choice.
- They may travel to mineral licks even when doing so requires leaving safer cliff habitat.
- Adult males can be dramatically heavier than females in northern populations.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Mountain Goat (*Oreamnos americanus*) is a powerful North American cliff specialist whose sharp permanent horns, 100-plus-kilogram male body, and extraordinary hoof traction make it dangerous on broken alpine terrain. It is not a speed specialist or heavily armored tank. Its defining advantage is positional: on cliffs and ledges, few terrestrial opponents can match its balance, grip, and escape-route control.

### Rich narrative profile
A mature mountain-goat billy is built around one problem: staying functional where the ground itself is dangerous. Its compact body, strong limbs, permanent horns, and unusual hooves allow a roughly 110 kg animal to occupy ledges and steep rock that would destabilize many larger opponents. The hard edge of each hoof finds purchase while the pliable inner pad conforms to irregular rock. This is not merely athletic spectacle; field ecology shows that mountain goats deliberately keep escape terrain nearby and use it when large predators approach.

In direct combat, however, the species should not be mythologized. Its horns are sharp and serious, and adult males genuinely fight for dominance during the rut, but the horns are shorter than the huge sweeping weapons of an ibex and the goat lacks the mass of elk, moose, musk oxen, or cattle. Many aggressive interactions are resolved by displays rather than injury. On flat ground its most distinctive advantage is reduced, leaving a strong medium-large bovid with respectable but not elite absolute offense.

Its best battle profile is therefore a terrain controller rather than a bruiser. On steep broken rock it can disengage, gain elevation, force an opponent onto poor footing, and threaten with horns from a stable stance. Against a cougar, large bear, wolf group, or much heavier ungulate on open terrain, its defenses are far less absolute. The ABS ratings preserve that distinction: exceptional Agility and strong Special, but only moderate Attack and Defense at the full-roster scale.

## 11. Confidence, conflicts, and unresolved fields

- **Mass:** moderate-high confidence in the 110 kg canonical male, but adult male ranges differ substantially among populations and summaries. Do not describe 110 kg as a universal average.
- **Height:** moderate confidence. Commonly cited 90-110 cm shoulder range is consistent across zoological summaries but was not as directly quantified in the strongest primary source retrieved here.
- **Length:** high confidence in species range; canonical 165 cm is an editorial representative large-male choice within that range.
- **Speed:** unresolved. `0.0` is intentionally used rather than promoting tertiary 25 km/h claims to measured maximum speed.
- **Bite force:** unresolved and nonessential to the species' combat system. `0.0` is correct under repository rules.
- **Horn weapon performance:** direct horn dimensions and fighting behavior are well supported, but no impact-force measurement was found. Attack scoring therefore does not invent joules or force.
- **Image:** exact male and license are strong, but strict full-body and adulthood are not established.

## 12. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Ibex:** Mountain Goat should have lower Weaponry and likely lower Attack because *Capra ibex* carries much larger specialized horns. Mountain Goat can rival or exceed it in some cliff-footing contexts but should not receive the same horn-combat score by association.
- **Versus Moose:** Moose is a 550 kg-class control with Attack 69.0. Mountain Goat at 110 kg must remain substantially lower in Raw Power, Size, Attack, and Defense. The proposed Attack 51.0 preserves that gap.
- **Versus Monitor Lizard:** Mountain Goat is far heavier and has stronger absolute body power, so it should exceed the 20 kg water monitor in Raw Power and conventional Defense, while the monitor retains distinct predatory weapon and aquatic advantages.
- **Versus Mongoose/Meerkat:** no proportional-strength leakage. A 110 kg bovid must sit vastly above sub-kilogram mammals in absolute power and conventional damage capacity.
- **Versus Megalodon:** no compression. A terrestrial 110 kg herbivore remains far from a 30-tonne macropredator ceiling in Attack, Defense, Size, and Raw Power.
- **Agility caveat:** 88.0 is intentionally high because Agility measures balance/control/maneuverability rather than absolute force. This does not imply the goat is faster than high-speed cursorial or aerial animals.
- **Special caveat:** 78.0 reflects a genuine matchup-changing terrain specialization, not a duplicate reward for Agility. Agility scores the animal's control; Special scores access to escape/attack geometry unavailable to many opponents.
