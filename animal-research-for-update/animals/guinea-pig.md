# Guinea Pig (*Cavia porcellus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Guinea pig (domestic cavy, cuy)
- **Scientific name:** *Cavia porcellus* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** the domestic guinea pig as kept for pets, food and research. It does not exist as a wild species; it was bred in the Andes from wild cavy stock, mainly the montane guinea pig (*Cavia tschudii*), with smaller genetic contributions from other *Cavia* species. Wild-cavy biology is used here only where it explains the domestic animal's behavior.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (domestic animal).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male (boar) of about **1.0 kg** of a standard pet breed, not a 3 kg Peruvian meat-breed animal and not an obese pet.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger than females (ADW) and are the sex that fights, establishing hierarchies in which one alpha male dominates others and becoming extremely aggressive when competing for territory or mates.
- **Population variation:** pet breeds weigh about 700–1,200 g; Peruvian livestock lines developed since the 1960s (cuy mejorados) reach about 3 kg. Coat type varies widely (short, rosetted, long, curly, nearly hairless) without changing combat biology.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
A guinea pig has incisors and small claws, but it is a grazer built for chewing grass, not for fighting. Its jaw muscles are organized for grinding rather than a hard incisor bite. Between boars, dominance is settled by chattering teeth, raised fur, head thrusts, leaping attacks and bites, especially to the ears. These fights can draw blood between guinea pigs, but they cannot seriously hurt a predator.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Stocky and short-legged. Wikipedia notes that guinea pigs can jump small obstacles but not high, that most are poor climbers and not particularly agile, and that they escape with rapid, darting runs. They swim well when forced to. Happy guinea pigs perform small vertical hops known as "popcorning".

### Endurance
Domestic guinea pigs have long periods of activity broken by short naps, with no regular daily rhythm. They graze almost continuously but are not built for long runs.

### Senses
Vision covers about **340°**, with two-color (dichromatic) vision that is weaker than a human's at distance. Hearing, smell and touch are well developed. A rich set of calls includes squeals of alarm, whistles to announce food and long-distance contact, chutts during chases and purrs.

### Intelligence and tactics
Guinea pigs learn complex routes to food and remember them for months. A Münster study found domestic guinea pigs, despite a relative brain size about 13% smaller than wild cavies, learned a water maze faster than wild cavies did. Their main anti-predator tactics are the individual freeze ("immobility response") and the group "scatter response", in which a startled group bolts in random directions to confuse a predator.

### Sociality
Highly social. Wild cavies live in small herds of one male, several females and young. Domestic guinea pigs prefer close contact, huddle together and groom each other, and Switzerland legally requires them to be kept in company.

### Fighting and aggression
Domestication has made guinea pigs less aggressive and more tolerant than wild cavies, with lower stress-hormone reactivity. Boars still fight over females and rank, so males are often separated when females are present.

### Predator defense
Freezing, scattering, fleeing to cover, and alarm calls. Larger animals such as dogs and cats may treat them as prey.

### Environmental strengths
- Grassy, rocky ground with dense cover and runways.
- Group settings, where scattering and alarm calls work.
- Cool, dry highland conditions like the Andean home of its wild stock.

### Weaknesses
- Weak incisor bite for a rodent and very low absolute power.
- No armor; poor climbing and jumping.
- Depends on dietary vitamin C and suffers scurvy without it.
- Freezing in place can fail against a predator that has already spotted it.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** insects and animals much smaller than itself.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized rodents and rabbits in a shoving contest.
- **Bad:** cats, dogs, foxes, mustelids, raptors, snakes and anything medium-sized or larger.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Scatter Stampede** — when a group is startled, guinea pigs bolt in haphazard directions to confuse a predator, while a lone animal freezes motionless until the threat passes. In a matchup this buys escape time but does no damage.
2. **Panoramic Watch** — laterally placed eyes give about 340° of vision, backed by alarm squeals and contact whistles, so a guinea pig rarely misses an approaching threat even while grazing.

### Unique traits
1. **Andean Livestock** — first domesticated for meat in the Andes thousands of years ago, the guinea pig is still raised and eaten as cuy in Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador and Colombia, where improved meat lines reach 3 kg.
2. **Born Ready** — unlike most rodents, guinea pig pups are born fully furred with teeth, claws and open eyes, and can run and eat solid food almost immediately.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
As a domestic animal the guinea pig lives worldwide in homes, laboratories and Andean farms. Its main wild source species, the montane guinea pig, lives in the high Andes from Peru to northern Chile and Argentina at about 2,000–3,800 m, in moist habitats with rocks and coarse vegetation where it makes runways through the plants.

### Diet and ecology
Strict herbivores: grasses, hay, leafy vegetables and fruit. Like humans, and unlike most mammals, guinea pigs cannot make their own vitamin C and need about 10 mg a day from food. Wild cavies graze open grassy ground and shelter in other animals' burrows, rock crevices and vegetation tunnels.

### Social structure
Social herd animals. Wild groups are usually one male with several females and young. Domestic groups form dominance hierarchies, with boars competing for rank and mates. Groups huddle, groom and communicate constantly by voice.

### Reproduction and life history
Gestation lasts about 59–72 days, and litters average about three precocial pups. Females can be fertile at about four weeks and can conceive again within hours of giving birth. Pets usually live five to eight years.

### Conservation
**Domesticated**; the domestic guinea pig has no wild population to assess. Its main wild source species, the montane guinea pig, is listed as Least Concern.

### Special features
- About 340° field of vision.
- Continuously growing incisors and cheek teeth.
- Precocial young born furred and mobile.
- Cannot synthesize vitamin C.
- Large vocal repertoire, including whistles and alarm squeals.

### Human interaction
Guinea pigs were domesticated for food in the Andes, where they also have roles in folk medicine and ceremonies. They became pets in Europe after the Spanish conquest, were central to early research on scurvy and infectious diseases, and gave English the phrase "guinea pig" for a test subject.

### Genuine fun facts
- Guinea pigs, like people, must get vitamin C from their food.
- A startled group scatters in random directions to confuse predators.
- Domestic guinea pigs learned a water maze faster than wild cavies, despite smaller relative brains.
- Pups are born with fur, teeth and open eyes and can eat solid food almost at once.
- Excited guinea pigs perform little vertical jumps called "popcorning".
- The oldest guinea pig recorded by Guinness World Records lived to 14 years and 10 months.

### Concise site-ready summary
The guinea pig is a 1 kg Andean grazer bred from wild cavies, with nearly all-round vision, a busy social life and a freeze-or-scatter escape plan. Its incisors are built for grass rather than fighting, so on the absolute scale it is one of the least dangerous mammals on the roster.

### Rich narrative profile
The guinea pig is a domestic animal with a long Andean history. People in the highlands of South America bred it from wild cavies thousands of years ago as a compact source of meat, and it still fills that role there, while elsewhere it is one of the most popular small pets. It lives in groups, talks constantly in squeaks, whistles and purrs, and keeps watch with eyes that see almost all the way around its head.

In a fight it has very little to offer. Its jaws are organized for grinding grass, and a comparative study of rodent jaw muscles found the guinea pig built for a relatively weak incisor bite. Boars do fight each other with chattering, head thrusts, leaping attacks and ear bites, but these contests matter only between guinea pigs. Against a predator, its options are to freeze, scatter with the herd, or dash for cover, and it is neither a good climber nor a good jumper.

On the absolute ABS scale the guinea pig therefore sits near the floor for Attack and Defense, below similarly sized predators and the spined hedgehog, with moderate scores for intelligence and senses. Its real distinctions are social and historical rather than martial: a herd animal that became Andean livestock, a laboratory model and a household pet.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Wild source species:** Wikipedia's molecular and skeletal summary points to *C. tschudii*; the Münster group uses *Cavia aperea* as the wild comparison form. Both are wild Andean or South American cavies; the report does not depend on the choice.
- **Lifespan:** sources give averages of 4–5, 5–8 and 8 years; canonical 6.0 is a midpoint for pets.
- **Bite:** only a relative, model-based comparison exists; no PSI or newton value is assigned.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Capybara (54 kg; A31 D29 Ag65 St52 I52 Sp63):** the guinea pig's much larger cavy relative outweighs it more than fifty times and sits far above in every physical category; the guinea pig is close in Intelligence (48.0 vs 52.0) because both are social grazers with rich vocal communication.
- **Versus Naked Mole Rat (0.057 kg; A14 D24 St83 I76 Sp96):** the mole-rat keeps higher Attack because its bite is measured and exceptionally strong, and far higher Stamina and Special for anoxia tolerance. The guinea pig is heavier but has a weak bite and no comparable physiology.
- **Versus Hedgehog (1.0 kg; A11 D43 Ag41 Sp69):** equal mass; the hedgehog keeps far higher Defense and Special from spines and curling, while Agility is almost level (40.0 vs 41.0).
- **Versus Syrian Hamster (0.14 kg; A9.5) and Chinchilla (0.6 kg; A10.0):** the heavier guinea pig sits marginally above both in Attack despite its weak bite and mild temper, because absolute mass still matters; it sits below the chinchilla in Agility (40.0 vs 84.0).
- **Anti-inflation check:** social complexity lifts Intelligence only to 48.0; the scatter response is not counted as Defense.
