# Maine Coon (*Felis catus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Maine Coon (Maine Coon cat)
- **Scientific name:** *Felis catus* Linnaeus, 1758 (domestic cat; Maine Coon breed)
- **Exact animal represented:** the pedigree Maine Coon breed of domestic cat, the large, long-coated breed from the US state of Maine, as recognized by the CFA and other registries. Not random-bred long-haired cats, not the Norwegian Forest Cat or Siberian, and not hybrid breeds such as the Savannah. The breed was bred from farm cats brought to New England by European settlers.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (domestic breed).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature intact male of about **7.5 kg**, fully grown (the breed can take up to three years to finish growing) and in fit, non-obese condition.
- **Sex choice:** males are much larger than females (females roughly 5–7 kg in breed summaries) and, as in all domestic cats, males fight most over territory and mates. In feral cats, males are about 36% heavier than females and have about 20% greater estimated bite force.
- **Population variation:** a measured sample of 15 Maine Coons averaged **6.3 ± 1.5 kg** (range 3.1–8.5 kg), including females and young adults. Breed summaries give males an average of 8–10 kg, and the CFA notes that it is not unusual for a mature male to reach or exceed 20 lb (9.1 kg). Record and obese animals are excluded.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Schnabl-Feichter et al. (2018): 15 healthy Maine Coons (10 males) averaged **6.3 ± 1.5 kg**, range **3.1–8.5 kg**, versus 5.5 kg for domestic shorthairs in the same study.
- Wikipedia (tertiary): males **8–10 kg**, females **5–7 kg**.
- CFA breed profile: mature males commonly reach or exceed **20 lb (9.1 kg)**.
- **Canonical:** **7.5 kg**, above the mixed-sex measured mean and below the tertiary male average.
- **Confidence:** Moderate; peer-reviewed data are few and mixed-sex.

### Height
- Wikipedia: adult height **25–41 cm**.
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 30.0 cm** for a mature male.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate; the source range is broad and its method is not stated.

### Length
- Wikipedia: up to **97 cm** including the tail, with the tail up to **36 cm**. The Guinness "Longest Cat" in 2010 was a purebred Maine Coon male, Stewie, at **123 cm** nose to tail tip, a record rather than a representative animal.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 90.0 cm**, **total length including the tail**, matching the house cat's total-length convention (76 cm). Head-body alone is roughly 55–60 cm for such a cat.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Speed
- No instrumented measurement was found for the breed or for domestic cats generally. **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0**.

### Lifespan
- Teng et al. (2024), UK VetCompass life tables: Maine Coon life expectancy at birth **9.71 years** (95% CI 8.42–11.00), against 11.74 years for all UK cats.
- Swedish pet-insurance data (2003–2006, cited by Wikipedia): median lifespan **over 12.5 years**, with 74% reaching 10 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 11.0 years**, between the two datasets.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate; the two studies measure different things in different populations.

### Bite and claws
- No Maine Coon bite-force measurement exists. In 568 stray and feral cats, body mass was the strongest influence on estimated bite force, with males averaging **206.2 ± 44.7 N** (lever model). A heavier Maine Coon male should therefore bite somewhat harder than a typical tomcat, but no figure is assigned.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.**
- Retractable claws on large, round, heavily tufted paws; some breeding lines are polydactyl, with one to three extra digits per paw.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. A heavy, shaggy, water-resistant double coat, longest on the belly, flanks and rear, with a leonine ruff in some cats, adds insulation and a little padding.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The Maine Coon has the standard domestic-cat kit on a larger frame: a stalk and pounce, forefoot claws to grab and hold, hind claws to rake, and a precise killing bite to the neck of small prey. Because bite force rises with body mass, a 7.5 kg male should bite harder than an ordinary 5 kg tomcat, and the breed is historically a working mouser on New England farms. Against anything much larger than a rabbit, however, it still lacks the mass and weaponry of wild cats such as the bobcat or lynx.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A long, muscular, rectangular body on sturdy legs with large paws. Gait studies show Maine Coons take longer steps and put a larger paw area on the ground than domestic shorthairs, with the same body-mass-normalized forces. They keep the cat's flexible spine, climbing ability and righting reflex, but greater weight makes them somewhat less nimble than small cats. The breed is over-represented in hind-limb orthopedic problems such as hip dysplasia and slipped capital femoral epiphysis.

### Endurance
Like other cats, a sprint-and-rest animal. Its heavy coat helps in cold weather but not in sustained exertion.

### Senses
Domestic-cat senses: hearing from about **48 Hz to 85 kHz**, one of the broadest ranges among mammals, low-light vision and long whiskers. Large, heavily tufted ears are a breed feature.

### Intelligence and tactics
The same cognitive profile as other domestic cats: quick reward learning, patient ambush hunting and flexibility around people. The breed is widely described as sociable and "dog-like", but no study shows greater problem-solving ability than other cats.

### Sociality
Solitary hunters with flexible social lives like other cats; the breed is noted for sociable, people-oriented behavior and tolerance of children and other pets.

### Fighting and aggression
Maine Coons are known as the "gentle giant" of the cat fancy. Intact males still fight like any tomcat, with threat displays, grappling, biting and hind-claw raking.

### Predator defense
Escape by climbing, puffing up to look bigger, and fighting back when cornered. Its size deters small predators more than a typical cat's would.

### Environmental strengths
- Cold, snowy and wet conditions, thanks to its water-resistant coat and snowshoe-like paws.
- Cluttered terrain with cover and perches.
- Low light and night conditions.

### Weaknesses
- Small absolute size and power despite being a large cat.
- No armor.
- Poor endurance in long chases.
- Breed predisposition to heart disease (hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) and hind-limb joint problems.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rodents, rabbits, birds and other animals well under its mass.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized animals such as foxes, raccoons, large ordinary cats and small mustelids.
- **Bad:** bobcats, lynxes, coyotes, dogs of medium size or larger, large raptors, venomous snakes and all bigger predators.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Snowshoe Paws** — large, round paws with long tufts of fur between the toes spread its weight on snow and soft ground, and in polydactyl lines one to three extra digits per paw add grip. In a matchup this means sure footing where other small predators flounder.
2. **Heavyweight Bite-and-Grab** — the classic cat attack of pounce, claw-grip and neck bite, delivered by one of the heaviest domestic cats; in cats, estimated bite force rises with body mass, so a big Maine Coon male hits harder than an ordinary tomcat.

### Unique traits
1. **Gentle Giant** — the largest non-hybrid domestic cat breed, slow to mature (up to three years), with mature males often reaching 20 lb; a Maine Coon named Stewie was the Guinness "Longest Cat" at 123 cm.
2. **All-Weather Coat** — a shaggy, water-resistant coat that is longest on the belly and rear, a bushy tail that wraps around the face, and heavily tufted ears suit it to Maine winters; many Maine Coons are also unusually fond of water.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
A domestic breed kept worldwide. It developed in Maine and elsewhere in New England, a region of cold, snowy forests and farmland, from cats brought by European settlers; genetic studies group the breed with Western European cats and find it most similar to random-bred cats of the northeastern United States.

### Diet and ecology
An obligate carnivore like all cats. Pet Maine Coons eat prepared food; the breed's origins are as a farm and ship mouser hunting rodents and small animals.

### Social structure
Like other domestic cats, a solitary hunter with flexible social relationships; the breed is noted for being sociable and people-oriented.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding is managed by people. Maine Coons grow slowly and may take up to three years to reach full size. UK life tables give a life expectancy at birth of about 9.7 years, while Swedish insurance data give a median over 12.5 years.

### Conservation
Not applicable as a wild species; it is a **Domesticated** breed. The Maine Coon was declared "extinct" as a show breed in the 1950s, prematurely, and has since become one of the most popular breeds, ranking first in FIFe registrations in 2024 and becoming the CFA's most registered breed in 2025.

### Special features
- Largest non-hybrid domestic cat breed.
- Heavy, water-resistant, shaggy coat with a ruff in some cats.
- Large, round, tufted "snowshoe" paws; polydactyly in some lines.
- Long, bushy, raccoon-like tail.
- Large, heavily tufted ears.

### Human interaction
Farmers in Maine held "Maine State Champion Coon Cat" contests at the Skowhegan Fair in the late 1860s, and a brown tabby female named Cosey was named Best in Show at the first North American cat show, at Madison Square Garden in New York in 1895. The breed gained CFA championship status in 1976 and became Maine's official state cat in 1985. Health screening focuses on hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, for which a *MYBPC3* mutation is common in the breed, hip dysplasia and spinal muscular atrophy.

### Genuine fun facts
- A Maine Coon named Stewie held the Guinness record as the world's longest cat, 123 cm from nose to tail tip.
- The breed can take up to three years to reach full size.
- Many Maine Coons have extra toes; in one study of 70 cats from polydactyl lines, 48 had them, with no apparent harm to welfare.
- The Maine Coon is the official state cat of Maine.
- Despite the name, the breed has no raccoon in it; the raccoon story is a myth.
- A female Maine Coon named Cosey was Best in Show at the first North American cat show, held at Madison Square Garden in 1895.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Maine Coon is the largest non-hybrid domestic cat, a 7.5 kg shaggy "gentle giant" with snowshoe paws, a weatherproof coat and the full house-cat toolkit of claws, bite, reflexes and ultrasonic hearing. It is noticeably stronger than an ordinary cat, but on the absolute scale it remains far below wild cats such as the bobcat and lynx.

### Rich narrative profile
The Maine Coon grew out of the farm cats of New England, where long winters favored big, heavily coated cats with broad, tufted feet. Breeders have since selected it for size, and a mature male commonly weighs 7–9 kg, making it the heaviest non-hybrid domestic breed. It keeps every tool of the domestic cat: retractable claws, a precise neck bite, a flexible spine and righting reflex, and hearing that reaches about 85 kHz.

Its extra size matters. In cats, bite force rises with body mass, and a heavy Maine Coon male can handle larger prey and bigger opponents than an ordinary tomcat. Its thick coat gives a little padding and superb cold-weather comfort, and its snowshoe paws keep it sure-footed in snow. On the other hand, the breed's famously gentle temperament, its slower, heavier frame and its predisposition to heart and hip problems keep it from being a fighting cat in any real sense.

On the absolute ABS scale, the Maine Coon therefore sits just above the house cat in Attack and Defense, level with a red fox, and well below the 10–24 kg wild cats such as the bobcat and lynx that it superficially resembles with its tufted ears. Its Agility, Senses and Intelligence stay close to the house cat's, because it is the same species in a bigger coat.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** the only peer-reviewed sample (mixed sex, some young cats) averages 6.3 kg, while breed summaries put males at 8–10 kg. Canonical 7.5 kg sits between them.
- **Height and length:** taken from a tertiary breed summary; length is total including the tail, following the house-cat convention.
- **Lifespan:** UK life expectancy at birth (9.71 y) and Swedish median lifespan (over 12.5 y) are different measures; 11.0 y is a compromise.
- **Bite:** no breed measurement; the feral-cat mass relationship supports a qualitative, not numeric, increase.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus House Cat (5 kg; A24 D16 Ag88 St48 I63 Sp60):** same species, 50% heavier. Attack (+4) and Defense (+4) rise with mass and coat; Agility drops (88.0 to 80.0) for the heavier frame; Intelligence is deliberately tied at 63.0 because no evidence shows breed differences in cognition; Special rises by one point for snow-suited paws and coat.
- **Versus Bobcat (10 kg; A44 D29 Ag82):** the wild cat is heavier, has larger canines and kills prey up to deer fawns, so it stays 16 points higher in Attack; its Agility (82.0) is just above the Maine Coon's.
- **Versus Lynx (24 kg; A48 D31 Ag85):** a Maine Coon can match a small lynx in length, but the lynx is about three times heavier and a specialist hunter, so it stays far above in Attack and Defense.
- **Versus Red Fox (7.2 kg; A28 D25):** similar mass; Attack is level, while the fox's hardier wild build keeps it above in Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** breed size is scored through Size, Raw Power and small Attack/Defense increases, not by treating the Maine Coon as a wild cat; its "dog-like" reputation is not scored.
