# Red-eared Slider (*Trachemys scripta elegans*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Red-eared Slider
- **Scientific name:** *Trachemys scripta elegans* (Wied-Neuwied, 1839), family Emydidae; one of three subspecies of the pond slider *Trachemys scripta*, alongside the yellow-bellied slider (*T. s. scripta*) and Cumberland slider (*T. s. troostii*).
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild red-eared slider of the Mississippi River valley and Gulf of Mexico drainages of the south-central United States. Captive colour varieties and the many feral populations worldwide come from pet-trade animals and are not treated separately.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult **female**, **1.5 kg** with a **23.0 cm straight carapace length**, representing a well-grown adult rather than a record animal.
- **Sex choice:** females are the larger sex. Animal Diversity Web gives average adult shell lengths of 25.4 cm for females and 17.8 cm for males, and females can reach 3.2 kg, while male masses are rarely reported. Males have long front claws used in courtship and fight other males by biting and chasing, but they are much smaller.
- **Population variation:** diet and growth vary by region: stomach samples from Tennessee sliders were almost all plant matter, while Florida sliders' stomachs held 89% animal material.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The slider's only weapon is its beak. Juveniles are mostly carnivorous, eating insects, crayfish, tadpoles, small fish, snails and carrion; adults become omnivores feeding mostly on aquatic plants, algae, stems, leaves and seeds, while still taking invertebrates when they can. Males bite, chase and display at each other. None of this is dangerous to anything larger than a small fish or crayfish.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Almost entirely aquatic, it swims with partly webbed feet in slow, warm, weedy water and climbs onto logs, rocks and banks to bask. It moves slowly on land, mainly when females leave the water to nest or when turtles move between ponds. In water it is reasonably quick and manoeuvrable for a turtle.

### Endurance
Its standout ability is surviving without oxygen. During winter brumation at the bottom of ponds it can survive anaerobically for weeks, cutting heart rate and cardiac output by about 80%; lactic acid built up during this time is buffered by minerals in the shell. In one experiment, the shell and skeleton of sliders held about 42% of the lactate load after seven days without oxygen at 5 °C. It is not the champion, though: painted and snapping turtles can survive up to four to five months without oxygen. Studies of anoxic slider hearts show they recover from oxygen loss and reoxygenation without the damage mammal hearts suffer. This is survival endurance, not fighting stamina.

### Senses
It has good colour vision and no external ear; a cartilage-covered eardrum sits behind the head. Senses are ordinary for a freshwater turtle.

### Intelligence and tactics
It chooses basking sites, stacks with other turtles on the best spots, and retreats into water or its shell when disturbed. No species-specific cognition studies were used.

### Sociality and hunting
It often basks in groups, sometimes piled on top of each other, but does not cooperate. Males court females by fluttering their long front claws in front of the female's face.

### Fighting and aggression
Males chase, bite and display at each other, and females may turn aggressive toward unwanted males. Against predators it withdraws into its shell or dives.

### Environmental strengths
- Warm, still or slow water with plenty of plants and basking spots.
- Oxygen-poor winter water that would kill most vertebrates.
- A wide diet that lets it thrive in new habitats.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny beak and small body; no real offense.
- Slow and exposed on land.
- Many predators, from alligators and gar to raccoons, otters and foxes.
- Depends on outside heat to be active.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small invertebrates, tadpoles and small fish.
- **Mixed:** small predators that cannot open the shell.
- **Bad:** alligators, large carnivores, and anything that can crush a 1.5 kg shell or catch it on land.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Oxygen-Free Endurance** — at the bottom of a winter pond it can survive without oxygen for weeks, dropping heart rate and cardiac output by about 80% and buffering lactic acid with minerals from its shell. Its heart recovers from this without damage.
2. **Warning-Stripe Shield** — it pulls its head and limbs into its shell, which survives bites from predators up to alligators, and its bright yellow and red markings act as a warning: largemouth bass, which hunt by sight, avoid sliders once they hatch.

### Unique traits
1. **Red Ear Stripe** — a red stripe behind each eye, where an ear would be, gives the turtle its name; brighter red stripes go with stronger immune responses.
2. **Six-Continent Invader** — released pets have set up populations on every continent except Antarctica, and it is listed among the world's worst invasive species.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The red-eared slider is native to the Mississippi River valley and Gulf of Mexico drainages of the south-central United States, living in ponds, lakes, swamps, creeks and slow rivers with warm, still water, abundant aquatic plants and places to climb out and bask. It can also live in brackish water. Through the pet trade it now has feral populations in Europe, Asia, Australia, Africa, the Caribbean, Bermuda and elsewhere.

### Diet and ecology
Juveniles are mostly carnivorous, eating insects, crayfish, tadpoles, fish, snails and carrion. Adults shift to an omnivorous diet dominated by aquatic plants and algae but still take invertebrates. In southern Illinois, adult stomachs held more than 40% plant material, with females eating more molluscs than males. Predators of adults and young include alligators, snakes, gar, catfish, wading birds, crows, opossums, raccoons, mink, otters, skunks, coyotes and foxes.

### Social structure
Sliders bask in groups and often stack on top of each other on the best spots, but they do not form social groups. Males court females underwater by vibrating their long front claws near the female's face.

### Reproduction and life history
Females lay 2–30 eggs per clutch and up to five clutches a year, digging nests on land with their hind legs. Incubation takes about 59–112 days, and some late hatchlings overwinter in the nest. Sex depends on incubation temperature: cooler nests (about 22–27 °C) produce males and warmer nests produce females. Males mature at 2–5 years and females at 5–8 years.

### Conservation
The red-eared slider is common in its native range, and its main conservation significance is as an invader. Wikipedia, citing the IUCN Invasive Species Specialist Group (Lowe et al. 2000), lists it among the world's worst invasive alien species, and it competes with native turtles for food, nesting and basking sites and spreads parasites. The European Union has banned its import, Japan banned import and release in 2023, and Queensland has spent close to AU$1 million on eradication.

### Special features
- Red stripe behind each eye and no external ear.
- Long front claws in males for courtship.
- Weeks-long survival without oxygen, with shell buffering of lactic acid.
- Bright markings that some predatory fish avoid.
- Temperature-dependent sex determination.
- Head, limbs and tail retract into the shell.

### Human interaction
It is the world's most commonly traded reptile. A 1975 U.S. Food and Drug Administration rule bans the sale of turtles with shells under 10 cm because of Salmonella infections, especially in children. Many pets are later released, which is how the species spread worldwide; in Britain, the Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles craze that followed the first live-action film led to a surge in pet sliders and later releases.

### Genuine fun facts
- It can survive for weeks without oxygen at the bottom of a frozen-over pond.
- Its shell helps neutralise the lactic acid that builds up while it goes without oxygen.
- Largemouth bass avoid eating sliders, apparently because of their bright warning colours.
- Bite marks and embedded teeth have been found in the shells of live sliders that escaped predators, including alligators.
- The Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles were revealed in the comics to be red-eared sliders.
- Cooler nests hatch males and warmer nests hatch females.

### Concise site-ready summary
The red-eared slider is a small freshwater turtle from the south-central United States, now the world's most traded reptile and a worldwide invader. A 1.5 kg adult female has only a small beak, but it can hide inside a shell that survives predator bites and can live for weeks without oxygen at the bottom of a winter pond. It is tough to kill and harmless to fight.

### Rich narrative profile
On a sunny log in a Louisiana pond, a row of red-eared sliders sits stretched out in the sun, some stacked on top of others. At the first sign of danger they slide into the water and vanish. That combination of basking and quick retreat is the slider's everyday life: warm up, feed on plants and small animals, and disappear under water when threatened.

As a fighter it has little to offer. The beak crops plants and catches crayfish and tadpoles, and the only fights are between males chasing and biting each other in the breeding season. Its defense is its shell, which it can close up completely, and which has been found on live sliders bearing bite marks and embedded teeth from predators, including alligators. Its bright yellow and red markings also seem to warn off some predators: largemouth bass, which hunt by sight, avoid sliders once they hatch.

Its most remarkable feature is invisible. In winter, a slider on the pond bottom can go for weeks without oxygen, slowing its heart by about 80% and using minerals from its shell to neutralise the acid that builds up in its body. In ABS terms that earns it a solid Stamina and a moderate Special, alongside a near-zero Attack, moderate Defense and modest Agility. The same toughness has made it one of the world's worst invasive species, found on every continent except Antarctica.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** "up to 1.5 kg" (German Wikipedia) versus "up to 3.2 kg" (ADW). The canonical 1.5 kg is a typical adult female, not a maximum.
- **Maturity:** Wikipedia gives 5–6 years for both sexes; ADW gives 2–5 years for males and 5–8 for females.
- **Carapace length:** Wikipedia's claim of carapaces over 40 cm is far above every other source and is not used.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Snapping Turtle (12 kg; 47.0 / 55.0 / 42.0 / 51.0 / 42.0 / 53.0):** the snapper has a dangerous long-neck bite and eight times the mass, so it is far above the slider in Attack and above it in Defense. Stamina is similar. The slider's Special (52.0) stays at about the snapper's 53.0 because snapping turtles tolerate even longer periods without oxygen (up to 4–5 months), so the slider's anoxia tolerance does not outrank the snapper's combined strike and physiology.
- **Versus Alligator Snapping Turtle (45 kg; 55.0 / 64.0 / 28.0 / 45.0 / 38.0 / 68.0):** thirty times the slider's mass with a massive bite and heavy ridged shell. The slider is more agile and has better Stamina, but its Special stays below the alligator snapper's unique tongue lure.
- **Versus Russian Tortoise (1.0 kg; Defense 35.0, Agility 15.0):** a similar-sized chelonian; the tortoise's thicker, more closed shell earns slightly higher Defense, while the slider is far more agile in water.
- **Anti-inflation check:** anoxia tolerance is counted in Stamina and Special, not in Defense; the shell alone sets Protection. The existing Snapping Turtle profile does not appear to count its longer anoxia tolerance, which may be worth revisiting in the roster audit.
