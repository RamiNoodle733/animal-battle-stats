# Zebra (*Equus quagga*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Zebra; specifically the plains zebra, also called the common zebra or Burchell's zebra
- **Scientific name:** *Equus quagga* Boddaert, 1785 (older literature uses *Equus burchellii*)
- **Exact animal represented:** the plains zebra, the species shown in the site's live photo (a Burchell's zebra, *E. q. burchellii*, with brownish "shadow" stripes between the black stripes). Grévy's zebra and the mountain zebras are separate species and are not represented.
- **Status:** Living. Near Threatened (IUCN, 2016).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male (harem stallion), **300.0 kg**, **135.0 cm** at the shoulder and **230.0 cm** head-body length.
- **Sex choice:** males weigh **220–322 kg** against **175–250 kg** for females (Wikipedia), and the harem stallion is the animal that fights rival males and attacks predators, so a stallion is used.
- **Population variation:** size varies across the range from East Africa to South Africa. ADW gives 250–300 kg under *Equus quagga* and 175–385 kg under its *Equus burchellii* account. Striping also varies: southern animals have fainter leg stripes and shadow stripes.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The plains zebra is one of the more combative hoofed prey animals. Stallion fights "often become very violent," with biting at the neck, head and legs, wrestling to the ground and occasional kicking; a stallion may even lie still as if surrendering and strike again when the rival relaxes (Wikipedia). Against predators the harem stallion goes on the offensive against hyenas and wild dogs, and stallions sometimes bluff-charge lions. ADW describes harems surrounding a predator that has wounded a member and biting and kicking until it flees or dies. The weapons are blunt hooves and strong incisors on a 300 kg body; there are no horns or claws.

### Defense and durability
There is no armor, and a zebra's neck, flanks and legs are exposed. Lions are most successful against lone individuals, usually old males, while spotted hyenas chase and isolate a female or foal (Wikipedia). Nile crocodiles take zebras at water. Leopards, cheetahs and wild dogs mostly hunt foals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Zebras gallop, swerve and zigzag to escape. Wilson et al. 2018 found that zebras, like lions, are less athletic than cheetahs and impalas in speed, acceleration and turning, and that hunts at lower speeds let zebras use their maximum turning capacity to escape. A 2025 dissection study found wild equid forelimb muscles broadly comparable across species when scaled, with a trend toward greater flexor force in zebras from steep or uneven habitats.

### Endurance
Plains zebras are strong walkers and migrants. Eight collared females on the Namibia–Botswana border migrated from the Chobe River to Nxai Pan and back, a straight-line round trip of **500 km**, the longest recorded large-mammal migration in Africa and longer than the straight-line Serengeti wildebeest route. Home ranges run from **30 to 600 km²**, and a family may cover over 17 km before settling for the night (ADW). Zebras must drink regularly, which limits them in drought.

### Senses
Zebras are highly vigilant. They bark or snort on sighting a predator, and a Tanzanian study found vigilance rose when zebras heard lion roars regardless of local lion activity, and fell with larger group size and for zebras in the middle of the herd. At night at least one harem member stays awake on guard (ADW).

### Intelligence and tactics
Migrating zebras appear to rely on memory as well as perception: models guided by memory brought simulated zebras two to four times, or up to 100 km, closer to the real migration destination than perception-only models. Harem members cooperate to protect threatened members, especially foals, and stand in a semicircle 50–100 m from lions to watch them. They let cheetahs, wild dogs and single hyenas come closer, showing they judge the threat each predator poses.

### Social structure and fighting
Harems of one stallion, several mares and their young stay together for months to years; bachelor groups form separately. Stallions gain mares by abducting young females from their natal harems, and fathers fight off abductors. Most fighting happens over young mares in oestrus; a healthy harem stallion is rarely challenged.

### Predator defense
Main predators are lions and spotted hyenas. Group defense, vigilance, flight and stallion counter-attacks all play a part. Stripes may help: the strongest evidence is that they stop biting flies from landing, while their effect on large predators is still debated.

### Environmental strengths
- Open grassland and savanna with room to see predators and run.
- Mixed herds with wildebeest and other grazers, sharing vigilance.
- Long migrations guided by remembered routes.

### Weaknesses
- No horns, claws or armor; kicks need precise range.
- Exposed neck and legs for lions and crocodiles.
- Dependent on water; drought and fences cut migrations.
- A lone zebra loses the group's vigilance and defense.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** hyenas, wild dogs, cheetahs and other medium predators that must enter kicking and biting range; small carnivores.
- **Mixed:** leopards and wolves in one-on-one fights, where a stallion's kicks and bites can decide the outcome.
- **Bad:** lions, large crocodiles, big bovids and rhinos, and any fight in deep water.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Harem Mob Defense** — when a family member is attacked, the harem closes around the predator, biting and kicking, and the stallion charges hyenas and wild dogs head-on.
2. **Playing-Dead Counterstrike** — a fighting stallion may lie still as if beaten and then strike again when the rival lets up, part of a violent biting, wrestling and kicking fight style.

### Unique traits
1. **Fly-Proof Stripes** — experiments show horseflies fail to make controlled landings on striped coats; far fewer land on zebras than on plain-coated horses.
2. **Longest African Migrant** — plains zebras travelling between the Chobe River and Nxai Pan make a 500 km straight-line round trip, the longest recorded large-mammal migration in Africa.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Grasslands, savannas and open woodland of eastern and southern Africa, from Uganda (its most northern and most genetically distinct population) through Kenya and Tanzania to Botswana, Namibia, Zimbabwe and South Africa. Populations may be sedentary in dense, small ranges or migratory between separate wet- and dry-season ranges.

### Diet and ecology
A grazer of grasses. Zebras and wildebeest often graze and migrate together, as in the Maasai Mara. In drought, water-dependent grazers such as zebras may decline or move away.

### Social structure
Highly social. Harems of one stallion, several mares and young; bachelor groups of young and older males. Many harems and bachelor groups gather into herds of hundreds, especially on migration. Mares have a dominance hierarchy, and the top mare leads group movement.

### Reproduction and life history
Females reach puberty before three years, males after five or six. Foals weigh 30–35 kg at birth and stay with their mothers, learning to watch for predators and to follow migration routes. Stallions that take over a harem may kill foals that are not theirs.

### Conservation
Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List (2016). Threats include competition with livestock for food and fences that block migration routes. The quagga, a southern form, was lost to hunting in South Africa.

### Special features
- Black and white stripes that deter biting flies.
- Violent stallion fighting style of biting, wrestling and kicking.
- Cooperative harem defense and graded predator responses.
- Memory-guided long-distance migration.
- Sturdy equid frame with hard single hooves.

### Human interaction
Zebras are major attractions in African parks. They compete with livestock for grazing, and fences interrupt migrations. The zebra is a totem animal for the Shona people of Zimbabwe, and zebras appear in southern African rock art.

### Genuine fun facts
- Eight collared zebras revealed a 500 km round-trip migration, longer than the Serengeti wildebeest's straight-line journey.
- Horseflies approach zebras fast and fail to slow down, so many just bump the coat instead of landing.
- A fighting stallion may pretend to give up, then attack again.
- Zebras watch lions from 50–100 m away but let a single hyena come within a few metres.
- At night at least one harem member stays awake on guard.
- Foals weigh 30–35 kg at birth.
- Zebra vigilance rises when they hear recorded lion roars, even where lions are rarely seen.

### Concise site-ready summary
The plains zebra is a 300 kg striped equid and one of the most combative prey animals on the savanna. Stallions fight with bites, wrestling and kicks, attack hyenas and wild dogs, and families mob predators together. It migrates farther than any other African large mammal, but it has no armor and lions kill it regularly.

### Rich narrative profile
The plains zebra is a horse-sized grazer of the African savannas, a stallion weighing about 300 kg and standing about 135 cm at the shoulder. Its stripes are its signature, and the best experimental evidence says they stop biting flies from landing. Whatever else they do, they have not made zebras hard for lions to catch: lions and spotted hyenas remain its main killers.

What makes the zebra formidable is its temperament. Harem stallions fight each other violently, biting at necks and legs, wrestling rivals to the ground and kicking, and sometimes faking surrender before striking again. The same stallions charge hyenas and wild dogs, and a harem will close around a predator that has wounded one of its own, biting and kicking until it leaves. Against a lion, though, the zebra's hooves and teeth are rarely enough; lions most often kill lone, older males.

Zebras are also travellers. Collared zebras in Namibia and Botswana make a 500 km round trip each year, and models suggest they steer by remembered conditions, not just what they can see. That endurance and memory lift the zebra's Stamina and Intelligence, while its lack of horns and armor keeps Attack and Defense in the middle of the roster, alongside other mid-sized hoofed animals.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Speed:** Wikipedia's 60–70 km/h and ADW's 55 mph disagree, and the instrumented Botswana values were not retrieved; canonical speed is 0.0.
- **Lifespan:** wild averages of 9 years (ADW) and 25 years (Wikipedia) conflict; 20 years is used at low-moderate confidence.
- **Mass:** ADW's two accounts give different ranges (250–300 kg and 175–385 kg); the 300 kg stallion fits Wikipedia's male range.
- **Stripes and predators:** the fly-deterrence effect is experimentally supported; effects on lions and hyenas are not, so stripes do not raise Defense.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Donkey (150 kg; Attack 48.0, Stamina 77.0):** the zebra is twice as heavy and fights more aggressively, so Attack (56.0) and Ferocity are higher; Stamina is similar, with the zebra's migration record edging it higher (79.0).
- **Versus Wild Horse (320 kg; Attack 54.0, Defense 44.0):** a close equid match of similar mass. The zebra gets two points more Attack and higher Ferocity for documented attacks on hyenas and wild dogs; the wild horse keeps one point more Defense for its heavier winter coat and slightly greater mass.
- **Versus Okapi (275 kg; Attack 53.0, Agility 61.0):** similar mass; the zebra's aggression and open-country running put it slightly above in Attack and Agility.
- **Versus Cape Buffalo (750 kg; Attack 76.0, Defense 70.0):** the buffalo is far heavier with a horn boss and stays far above in Attack and Defense.
- **Versus Wildebeest (250 kg; Stamina 87.0):** zebras travel farther on migration, but wildebeest have measured waterless endurance and muscle efficiency, so the wildebeest keeps higher Stamina.
- **Versus African Lion (Attack 70.0, Agility 72.0):** consistent with Wilson et al. 2018, where lions out-accelerated and out-braked zebras, the lion stays above the zebra in Agility and far above in Attack.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholders (Attack 1.4, Defense 69.3) are not used. A zebra stallion is a real fighter, and without armor its Defense cannot be near 70.
