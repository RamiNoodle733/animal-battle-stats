# Fin Whale (*Balaenoptera physalus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Fin whale
- **Scientific name:** *Balaenoptera physalus* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** the fin whale species as a whole, a rorqual baleen whale and the second-largest whale after the blue whale (NOAA Fisheries). Northern and southern populations differ somewhat in size; the canonical animal is a generalized large adult rather than a record Antarctic individual.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy physically mature adult **female**, **20.0 m** long and **45,000 kg**.
- **Sex choice:** in fin whales the female is the slightly larger sex. Animal Diversity Web gives average lengths of **18.9 m** for physically mature males and **20.1 m** for physically mature females, and describes size dimorphism as limited. Following the blue-whale precedent on this roster, the larger sex is used; the difference in combat terms is small.
- **Population notes:** ADW notes northern and Arctic fin whales reach up to about 25 m, and Wikipedia's summary puts typical adults at 36–45 t with the largest specimens far heavier. The canonical 45,000 kg sits at the top of the typical adult band and well below maximum records.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- NOAA Fisheries: **40 to 80 tons**.
- ADW: approximately **70,000 kg** for a whale of about 20 m (a single summary figure, not a mean from weighed animals).
- Wikipedia summary: adults usually average **36–45 tonnes**; the largest specimens reach 65.5–80 t, with older unverified claims up to 120 t.
- **Canonical:** **45,000 kg**, the upper end of the typical adult band and inside NOAA's range, deliberately below record weights.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate. Whole-whale weights are rare and historical; sources disagree by tens of tonnes.

### Length

- ADW: average physically mature length **18.9 m (male)** and **20.1 m (female)**.

- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
- Standing height does not apply to a whale. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- Gough et al. (2019) found that tagged rorquals, fin whales included, cruise at roughly **2 m/s** regardless of body length.
- ADW: cruising speed near 23 mph and "sprinting" speed near **25 mph (11.2 m/s)**.
- Baleines en direct (GREMM, St. Lawrence research group): cruising 10–15 km/h and up to **40 km/h** when feeding.
- Wikipedia summary: sustained 37–41 km/h, bursts of 46–50 km/h reported, with a maximum of **40 km/h (11.1 m/s)** measured by satellite tracking or GPS.
- Ford & Reeves (2008): *Balaenoptera* species escape killer whales by rapid directional swimming at **20–40 km/h** and can sustain the speeds needed to outrun them (above 15–20 km/h).
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 11.1**, the tracked 40 km/h maximum, consistent with ADW's sprint figure. **Confidence:** Moderate; the primary tracking paper behind the 40 km/h figure was not retrieved in this run.

### Lifespan
- NOAA Fisheries: lifespan **80 to 90 years**; physical maturity at about 25 years.
- ADW: typical lifespan roughly **75 years**, with reports above 100 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 80.0**, the lower end of NOAA's stated lifespan, treated as a long-life value rather than a proven mean.

### Bite and dentition
- Baleen whale with no teeth. **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0**, meaning not applicable.

### Feeding apparatus
- NOAA: 50 to 100 accordion-like throat pleats let the whale gulp huge volumes of water and prey; up to 2 tons of food a day.
- Shadwick et al. (2017) tested a **3.6 m** mandible from a 16 m fin whale; rorqual mandibles are the largest ossified elements in animals and are reinforced against the bending loads of lunge feeding.
- Goldbogen et al. (2010): larger fin whales have relatively larger skulls and buccal cavities, and engulfment volume rises faster than body length.

### Defensive structure
- Streamlined body with a blubber layer, no armor, no barnacle crust and no callosities. Defense rests on mass and escape speed.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The fin whale has no teeth, claws or horns. Its only damaging tools are the flukes and the momentum of a 45-tonne body. Unlike the humpback, it is not recorded swinging its flippers or flukes at predators. Ford & Reeves (2008) place the fin whale among the "flight" species: when killer whales approach, these whales swim rapidly away, and if overtaken and attacked they do little to defend themselves. Its practical offense is therefore collision and tail strikes from a very large body, not a willingness to press an attack.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
This is the fastest of the great whales. A long, slim, streamlined body and a powerful tail stock give sustained high speed; ADW reports a sprint near 25 mph and Baleines en direct describes the fin whale as the "greyhound of the sea". The same source notes its small pectoral fins and flattened head favor speed over agility. During feeding it performs rolling lunges at depth (Goldbogen et al. 2006), so it can rotate its body quickly for a whale, but it cannot turn like a humpback with its long flippers or like a toothed whale.

### Endurance
Flight species must hold escape speeds for long periods. Ford & Reeves (2008) note that they can sustain the 15–20 km/h or more needed to outrun pursuing killer whales, and the recorded 2005 kill in the Gulf of California came only after 16 killer whales chased a fin whale for about an hour (Wikipedia, citing the NMFS draft recovery plan). Lunge feeding is costly: Goldbogen et al. (2006) found it shortens dives and lengthens surface recovery, so bursts of feeding are paid for with rest.

### Senses
Fin whales hear low frequencies well and produce some of the most powerful biological sounds in the ocean (Croll et al. 2002). Most calls are 16–40 Hz down-swept pulses (Wikipedia). They do not echolocate.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior is less studied than in humpbacks. Fin whales live in groups of two to seven, feed alongside humpbacks, minke whales and dolphins in the North Atlantic (NOAA), and circle schools to condense them before lunging (ADW). Only males produce the loud patterned songs, which likely serve as breeding displays (Croll et al. 2002). There is no documented cooperative defense.

### Sociality and hunting
Fin whales feed on krill, small schooling fish and squid by lunging into prey with the mouth open (NOAA). Each lunge engulfs a volume of water comparable to a large fraction of the body, and several lunges follow in a single dive (Goldbogen et al. 2006, 2010).

### Fighting and aggression
No intraspecific fighting has been described beyond male courtship chases. Toward predators the fin whale flees rather than fights.

### Environmental strengths
- Deep open ocean, where it can run in a straight line for long periods.
- Long chases, where sustained speed and a huge body make it very expensive to catch.
- Encounters with single predators too small to stop a 45-tonne animal.

### Weaknesses
- No bite or cutting weapon and no habit of fighting back.
- Thinner body build and no barnacle or callosity armor compared with "fight" species.
- Relatively small flippers limit tight turns.
- Coordinated killer whale packs can exhaust and kill adults.
- Must surface to breathe; vulnerable to ship strikes (NOAA).

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** smaller or slower predators that cannot keep pace or stop its mass; any opponent it can simply outrun in open water.
- **Mixed:** a single large shark or orca, which can wound it but struggles to catch or hold it.
- **Bad:** coordinated killer whale packs, toothed giants such as the sperm whale that bring real weapons to comparable mass, and shallow or enclosed settings where it cannot run.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Greyhound Escape Sprint** — a long, slim body built for speed lets the fin whale reach about 40 km/h and hold escape speeds that outlast pursuing killer whales. In a battle it can refuse a fight and force a long, costly chase.
2. **Engulfment Lunge** — accordion throat pleats and reinforced 3.6 m-scale jaws let it accelerate and engulf a body-sized volume of water and prey in one lunge. It is a feeding tool, but it shows the explosive power a 45-tonne rorqual can generate.

### Unique traits
1. **Two-Tone Jaw** — the head is asymmetrically colored, dark on the left lower jaw and white on the right, with the reverse on the tongue, a striking asymmetry that makes the species easy to identify at the surface.
2. **Infrasonic Suitor** — only males make long, patterned 16–40 Hz pulses, among the lowest and most powerful sounds made by any animal, which can travel great distances through the ocean.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Fin whales live in all the world's oceans, mostly in deep offshore waters from temperate to polar latitudes. Many populations migrate between summer feeding grounds and winter areas, while some groups appear to be resident, for example in the Gulf of California, the East China Sea and the Mediterranean Sea (NOAA).

### Diet and ecology
Fin whales eat krill, small schooling fish such as herring, capelin and sand lance, and squid. They lunge into prey with the mouth open, expanding 50 to 100 throat pleats, and can eat up to 2 tons of food a day (NOAA). Tagged whales off Southern California glide during descent, make a series of lunges at depth and then swim steadily back to the surface (Goldbogen et al. 2006). Feeding dives reach about 250 m and last up to around 15 minutes (ADW).

### Social structure
Fin whales are often seen in groups of two to seven and sometimes gather in large mixed feeding groups with humpbacks, minke whales and Atlantic white-sided dolphins (NOAA). Only males produce the loud, repeated songs linked with breeding (Croll et al. 2002).

### Reproduction and life history
Males mature sexually at about 6 to 10 years and females at 7 to 12; physical maturity comes at about 25 years (NOAA). Females give birth to a single calf every 2 to 3 years; calves are about 6 m long at birth (ADW).

### Conservation
The fin whale is listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List (2018 assessment, as summarized by Wikipedia; the IUCN page itself was not readable in this run) and as endangered throughout its range under the US Endangered Species Act (NOAA). Commercial whaling greatly reduced populations; current threats include ship strikes, to which fin whales are among the most vulnerable species, fishing-gear entanglement and ocean noise (NOAA).

### Special features
- Long, streamlined body built for sustained high speed.
- 50 to 100 expandable throat pleats for engulfment feeding.
- Very large, reinforced lower jaws.
- Asymmetrical head coloration.
- Extremely loud, low-frequency male songs.

### Human interaction
Whalers did not target fin whales at first because they were fast swimmers living in open ocean (NOAA); commercial whaling later reduced their populations heavily. Today they are seen on whale-watching trips and are vulnerable to collisions with ships.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is the second-largest animal on Earth, after the blue whale.
- It is nicknamed the "greyhound of the sea" for its speed.
- The right side of its lower jaw is white and the left side dark, and the tongue is colored the opposite way.
- A fin whale can eat up to 2 tons of food in a day.
- US scientists who first recorded fin whale calls considered equipment faults and geophysical causes before realizing a whale was making them.
- Only male fin whales sing the loud songs.

### Concise site-ready summary
The fin whale is a 45-tonne, 20 m rorqual built for speed: the "greyhound of the sea" can sprint to about 40 km/h and hold escape speeds that force killer whales into long chases. It has no teeth and does not fight back, so it survives on mass, endurance and running rather than weapons.

### Rich narrative profile
The fin whale is the second-largest animal alive, but its real signature is speed. Its body is long and slender, with a sharply V-shaped head and small pectoral fins, and it can reach about 40 km/h. Early whalers did not target it because it was too fast and lived far offshore. For killer whales the problem is the same: Ford and Reeves describe the fin whale as a "flight" species that responds to attack by swimming hard and straight, and the rare successful kills recorded took packs of orcas an hour of pursuit.

That strategy defines how it would fight. A 45-tonne fin whale has enormous raw power and great tissue depth, so a single predator struggles to injure it seriously, and it can simply leave. But it carries no teeth, and it is not known to swing its tail or flippers at attackers the way humpbacks and gray whales do. If it is cornered or overtaken by a coordinated pack, it does little to defend itself.

Its feeding is some of the most explosive behavior in the ocean. A fin whale accelerates into a school, drops its huge reinforced jaw and balloons its pleated throat to engulf a volume of water and prey approaching its own body size, then filters it out through baleen. Males broadcast long infrasonic pulses that are among the most powerful sounds any animal makes. The result is a giant with high Stamina and strong Defense, respectable Agility for its size, and an Attack held down by the lack of any weapon or will to use one.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** NOAA, ADW and Wikipedia disagree widely (36–80 t for adults, far more for records). The canonical 45,000 kg is an editorial large-adult placement.
- **Speed:** 40 km/h appears in several secondary sources; the primary tracking study was not retrieved. Routine cruising is only about 2 m/s.
- **Sex:** females are slightly larger; the canonical specimen follows that, but the combat difference is negligible.
- **Conservation status:** IUCN Vulnerable is taken from Wikipedia's summary of the 2018 assessment; the IUCN page refused automated access.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Blue Whale (120 t; A78 D91 Ag44 St87 I69 Sp76):** the fin whale has well under half the mass, so Attack (68.0) and Defense (83.0) sit clearly below the blue whale. It is faster, so Agility (52.0 vs 44.0) and Stamina (90.0 vs 87.0) are higher. Special (75.0) stays just under the blue whale's because the two share engulfment feeding and infrasonic calls.
- **Versus Humpback Whale (30 t; A72 D85 Ag56 St89 I78 Sp78):** the fin whale is heavier and much faster, but the humpback actively fights killer whales with long flippers and flukes and carries more blubber and barnacles. The fin whale is therefore lower in Attack (68.0 vs 72.0), Defense (83.0 vs 85.0), Agility (52.0 vs 56.0), Intelligence and Special, and slightly higher in Stamina and Size.
- **Versus Sperm Whale (35 t; A90 D90):** similar mass, but the sperm whale has teeth, a ramming head and biosonar, so it is far higher in Attack and Special.
- **Versus Orca (5 t; A94 Ag84 I99):** the orca is the fin whale's only natural predator and is far higher in Attack, Agility and Intelligence. The fin whale's nine-fold mass gives it higher Size, Raw Power and Defense (83.0 vs 78.0), which matches the field record: single orcas do not kill adult fin whales, packs do.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Attack is held at 68.0 despite 45 t because the species has no weapon and flees rather than fights. Speed feeds Agility and Stamina, not Attack.
