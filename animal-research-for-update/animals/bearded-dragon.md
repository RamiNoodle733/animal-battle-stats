# Bearded Dragon (*Pogona vitticeps*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Bearded Dragon (central or inland bearded dragon)
- **Scientific name:** *Pogona vitticeps* (Ahl, 1926), family Agamidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the central bearded dragon of inland eastern Australia, the species kept worldwide as a pet. Other *Pogona* species (such as the eastern bearded dragon) are not included. Captive colour morphs and scale-reduced "leatherback" and "silkback" lines are bred from wild stock but are not used as the combat form.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **0.40 kg**, about **50.0 cm total length** with a snout-vent length near 25 cm, representing a well-grown wild adult.
- **Sex choice:** males have larger, more angular heads, a darker and more developed beard, and bite harder than females; a 2020 study found that adult males out-bite both normal females and sex-reversed females because of their larger heads. Males also do most of the dominance fighting.
- **Population variation:** the Tasmanian pest risk assessment gives an average adult mass of 288 g (Seebacher and Franklin 2011), while AnAge lists an adult weight of 500 g. Captive animals are often heavier than wild ones. Colour depends partly on local soil.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The weapon is a bite from a broad, triangular head, stronger in adult males. The bearded dragon is an opportunistic omnivore: stomach flushing of 14 wild adults found that 61% of dry-matter intake was animal food, dominated by termite alates, with plants making up much of the rest. It will also take small lizards and rodents when it can. Against anything beyond insect and small-vertebrate size, its offense is minimal.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It is a sturdy, semi-arboreal lizard that runs on the ground and climbs onto branches, fence posts and rocks to bask. It lifts its body high on strong legs while moving. It is agile at its scale but has no specialized climbing adhesion or gliding.

### Endurance
Like other lizards it works in short bursts and spends long periods basking. Activity depends on body temperature.

### Senses
Good colour vision and a parietal "third eye" on the top of the head that senses light; a 2020 study found light-dependent magnetic sensing via the parietal eye. Vision dominates prey detection.

### Intelligence and tactics
This is one of the best-studied lizards for cognition. Kis et al. (2015) showed that bearded dragons can learn to open a trap door by watching another dragon do it, the first evidence of imitation in a reptile. A 2025 study of inhibitory control found bearded dragons outperformed Hermann's tortoises, though both scored lower than most mammals and birds. Social signals include head bobbing, arm waving and beard displays.

### Sociality and hunting
Generally solitary, but dragons gather at good basking and feeding sites, where a hierarchy forms and dominant animals take the highest, sunniest perches. It forages actively and by sit-and-wait.

### Fighting and aggression
Dominant males bob their heads fast and inflate and darken their beards; subordinates wave an arm to submit. If neither backs down, a standoff or fight follows. Against a predator it flattens its body, inflates its beard, opens its mouth to show the yellow lining, darkens, hisses and makes small jumps toward the attacker. It is not known to attack people.

### Environmental strengths
- Hot, open arid woodland and rocky desert.
- Elevated basking perches that give a view of rivals and predators.
- Bluff displays that deter small predators without a fight.

### Weaknesses
- Very small absolute mass and a weak bite on the roster scale.
- Soft spines and no armor.
- Performance falls when cold.
- Preyed on by birds of prey, snakes, foxes and feral cats.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** insects and very small vertebrates; small predators put off by the display.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized lizards in dominance contests.
- **Bad:** almost any mammalian or avian predator, snakes, and anything that ignores the bluff.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Beard Threat Display** — when threatened it flattens and widens its body, inflates and darkens its spiny throat pouch, gapes to show the yellow mouth lining, hisses and hops toward the attacker. The display can stop a small predator without contact.
2. **Solar Colour Shift** — the skin darkens in sunlight and lightens in darkness on an internal daily rhythm, and it changes colour with mood and during displays. Darker skin absorbs heat faster, helping it warm up for action.

### Unique traits
1. **Copycat Lizard** — in a 2015 experiment, dragons that watched another dragon open a trap door opened it the same way, the first evidence of learning by imitation in a reptile.
2. **Heat-Switched Sex** — eggs incubated above about 32 °C turn genetic males into fertile females, and a 2015 study made the first report of reptile sex reversal in the wild in this species.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The central bearded dragon lives in semi-arid and arid woodland, scrub, grassland and rocky desert across inland eastern Australia, from eastern South Australia and the southeastern Northern Territory through inland Queensland, New South Wales and Victoria. It tolerates cleared farmland and perches on fence posts and even picnic tables. It retreats to shade or burrows in the hottest part of the day.

### Diet and ecology
It is an opportunistic omnivore. In a stomach-content study of 14 free-roaming adults, animal food made up 61% of dry matter, termite alates made up 95% of all prey items, and plants made up 16% of dry matter. Wikipedia's summary of the same work states plant matter was 54% of the diet by volume, so the plant share depends on how it is measured. Adults also eat beetles, grasshoppers, caterpillars, flowers and the occasional small lizard or rodent.

### Social structure
Mostly solitary, but dragons gather at prime basking and feeding spots, where a clear hierarchy appears. Communication is visual: head bobs of different speeds signal dominance, submission and courtship, and arm waving signals submission.

### Reproduction and life history
Females dig a nest and lay about 11–30 eggs per clutch, and can lay several clutches in a season. Sex is normally set by ZZ/ZW chromosomes, but hot incubation (above about 32 °C) reverses genetic males into fertile females. Sexual maturity is estimated at one to three years and depends more on body size than on age.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List (2018 assessment). It is common and widespread in its range and tolerates considerable habitat change. Australia has banned the export of native wildlife since the 1960s; most of the world's captive dragons come from stock exported illegally between 1974 and 1990, and a captive-bred pet population was established by the early 1990s.

### Special features
- Expandable spiny throat pouch (the "beard") that darkens in displays.
- Parietal "third eye" that senses light.
- Daily and mood-linked colour change.
- Head-bob and arm-wave signalling.
- Temperature-triggered sex reversal.
- Straight-legged posture that lifts the body off hot ground.

### Human interaction
A 2021 Google Trends study found the bearded dragon was by far the most popular pet reptile of the previous decade, ahead of ball pythons and leopard geckos. Captive breeding has produced many colour morphs and scale-reduced lines; scaleless "silkback" dragons lose water about twice as fast as normal dragons. Without UVB light and dietary calcium, captive dragons develop metabolic bone disease.

### Genuine fun facts
- Bearded dragons were the first reptiles shown to learn by imitation.
- Termite alates made up 95% of the prey items found in wild dragons' stomachs.
- Heat can turn a genetically male embryo into a fertile female, and such females lay more eggs than normal females.
- A dragon waves one arm in a slow circle to tell a dominant dragon it gives up.
- Its skin gets darker in sunlight and lighter at night on a built-in daily rhythm.
- It is the most popular pet reptile in the world by online search interest.

### Concise site-ready summary
The bearded dragon is a sturdy, spiny lizard from the dry woodlands and deserts of inland Australia. A 0.4 kg adult male bites harder than a female and puts on a dramatic threat display, inflating a dark spiny beard and gaping, but its spines are soft and its bite is only dangerous to insects and small animals. It stands out for its brain, not its brawn: it was the first reptile shown to learn by imitation.

### Rich narrative profile
On a fence post in inland Australia, a male bearded dragon sits high in the morning sun, darkening its back to warm up faster. When a rival approaches, it bobs its head rapidly and swells its throat into a black, spiny beard. A lower-ranking dragon answers by slowly waving an arm in a circle, and the standoff ends without a bite. Most of bearded dragon life is decided this way, by signals rather than fights.

The dragon's weapons are modest. Males have broader heads and bite harder than females, and the diet ranges from termites and beetles to flowers and the odd small lizard or rodent. When a predator appears, the dragon flattens itself, inflates its beard, opens its yellow-lined mouth, hisses and hops forward. That bluff can work on a small predator, but the spines are soft and the whole animal weighs about 400 g, so birds of prey, snakes, foxes and cats all take them.

What sets the bearded dragon apart on the roster is its mind. In a trap-door experiment, dragons that watched another dragon open a door copied the same movement, the first clear case of imitation in a reptile, and it outperformed tortoises in a test of self-control. In ABS terms it is a low-Attack, low-Defense lizard with solid Agility, a notably high Intelligence for a reptile, and a moderate Special built on its threat display and colour change.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 288 g (adult mean, Tasmania) versus 500 g (AnAge). The canonical 0.40 kg male is a judgment between them.
- **Diet:** the same stomach study is summarised as 16% plant by dry matter and as 54% plant by volume; both are reported.
- **Speed:** no sprint measurement found.
- **Lifespan:** 10–15 years is a secondary figure; AnAge flags its 12-year maximum as questionable.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Iguana (7 kg; 27.0 / 31.0 / 66.0 / 43.0 / 45.0 / 70.0):** the iguana is about 17 times heavier with a tail whip and claws, so it is well above the dragon in Attack and Defense. The dragon sits below it in Agility (no swimming or long drops) and Special (no tail autotomy), but above it in Intelligence because of the imitation evidence.
- **Versus Chameleon (45 g; 12.0 / 8.0 / 65.0 / 31.0 / 48.0 / 88.0):** similar Attack despite being much heavier, because the chameleon's ballistic tongue is a precise weapon at tiny-prey scale. The dragon has slightly better Defense and Stamina and higher Intelligence, but a much lower Special.
- **Versus Gila Monster (1.1 kg; Attack 31.0, Special 93.0):** the Gila's venom makes it far more dangerous; the dragon has no venom and a much lower Special.
- **Versus Gecko (Tokay, 0.25 kg; Intelligence 59.0):** the dragon's Intelligence stays a little below the Tokay's existing score to avoid inflation, even though its imitation evidence is stronger; both are far below mammal and bird cognition anchors.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the spiny look does not count as armor (Protection 10.0), and the display is counted once, in Special.
