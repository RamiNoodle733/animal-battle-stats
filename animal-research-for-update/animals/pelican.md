# Pelican — American White Pelican (*Pelecanus erythrorhynchos*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Roster name:** Pelican
- **Resolved taxon:** American White Pelican, *Pelecanus erythrorhynchos* (Gmelin, 1789), family Pelecanidae.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime mature **male**, 6.9 kg, approximately 155 cm total length, with a roughly 2.7 m wingspan and approximately 34 cm culmen. The species is monotypic. Males and females are similar in plumage but males average larger, making the male the appropriate canonical fighting specimen.
- **Dimorphism:** Cornell gives both-sex ranges of 4.5–9.0 kg, 127–165 cm length and 244–290 cm wingspan. Published sexed mass data summarized by taxonomic references place males around 6.3–6.9 kg and females around 5.0 kg. The NWT status assessment reports mean culmen length of 342 mm in males and 288 mm in females.
- **Population variation:** broad North American migratory distribution creates ecological and movement variation, but no recognized subspecies. Recent GPS work also shows substantial individual environmental-niche variation.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
Cornell Lab reports **4.5–9.0 kg** for the species. The canonical **6.9 kg** male is a representative adult-male value rather than the exceptional upper extreme. Some older literature reports individuals as high as 13.6 kg, but this is not used as the normal fighter.

### Length, wingspan, and height
- Total length: **127–165 cm** (Cornell); Audubon gives 1.4–1.8 m in its broader treatment.
- Wingspan: **244–290 cm** (Cornell), consistent with the NWT government status assessment.
- Canonical total length: **155.0 cm**.
- Canonical wingspan contextual value: **270.0 cm**.
- `height_cm`: **0.0** because a standardized standing-height measurement was not recovered from a strong source and total length should not be relabeled as height.

### Bill and pouch
The NWT government assessment reports males averaging **342 mm** culmen length and females **288 mm**. Assiniboine Park gives a general bill range of **26.5–36.5 cm**. The bill is long, flat and paired with an expandable gular pouch used as a scoop. During breeding, adults of both sexes develop a temporary keratinous plate or "horn" on the upper bill that is shed after breeding.

### Speed
No defensible species-specific measured maximum airspeed or swimming top speed was recovered. The old roster's generic speed value is therefore rejected. **`speed_mps = 0.0`** pending a direct measurement.

### Lifespan
Sources differ because some give typical adult life expectancy and others records. Washington Department of Fish & Wildlife gives adults **12–14 years**; Brookfield Zoo gives **12–20 years in the wild**; ADW records a maximum wild age of **26.4 years**. Canonical lifespan is **14.0 years**, representing a normal adult-life figure rather than the longevity record.

### Bite force
No defensible bite-pressure measurement was recovered. The bill is primarily a prey-scooping and manipulation structure, not a high-pressure crushing jaw. **`bite_force_psi = 0.0`** rather than fabricating PSI.

### Defensive structures
No armor. Feathers and body mass provide modest protection, while the very large wings enable escape and repositioning. Subcutaneous air sacs around the breast increase buoyancy, reported in the NWT status assessment as contributing substantially to flotation.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The long bill is the primary contact tool. It can jab, clamp, shove, scoop and strike at close range, and ADW specifically records breeding birds jabbing neighboring pelicans and other waterbirds while defending tiny nest territories. The breeding-season bill plate is real keratin but should not be treated as a horn evolved for high-energy stabbing. Webbed feet can kick or push but are principally locomotor.

### Offensive mechanics
Against small aquatic prey, the bill and pouch form an efficient capture system rather than a high-force killing bite. Against another similarly sized bird, reach is useful and repeated jabs can displace an opponent. Against a mammalian predator or armored opponent, the lack of talons, teeth, crushing jaws, venom, or a piercing raptorial bill sharply limits damage.

### Defense and durability
At roughly 7 kg this is a very large flying bird, but it remains lightly built for flight and lacks armor. Its practical defense is distance: takeoff, swimming, group vigilance and soaring escape. On land it is comparatively awkward. Eggs and chicks are vulnerable to gulls, coyotes, corvids and other predators, while adults face severe weather, disease, wires and human-related hazards.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
American White Pelicans are powerful swimmers and excellent soaring fliers but clumsy walkers. Cornell calls them superb soarers that travel long distances in large flocks. The NWT status report describes thermal soaring, low flight over water in wind, webbed-foot takeoff and water-ski-like landing. Their huge wings confer stable efficient flight, though they are not high-acceleration aerial duelists like falcons.

### Endurance
This is the strongest ordinary combat-relevant dimension. The species is migratory and uses soaring to reduce transport costs. USGS satellite telemetry found adult summer 95% home ranges spanning **177–4,710 km²** and winter ranges **185–916 km²**, demonstrating broad routine movement ecology. ADW notes daytime migration in large formations, sometimes exceeding 180 birds.

### Senses
Foraging is visually guided by day, but Audubon reports that breeding-season nocturnal feeding can locate fish by touch during repeated bill dipping. This combination supports solid multisensory foraging, but not raptor-grade visual targeting or owl-grade nocturnal specialization.

### Intelligence and tactics
The clearest tactical behavior is cooperative fish herding. Groups form lines or coordinated arrangements that push fish toward shallow water, then scoop prey from the surface. This is useful behavioral coordination but should not be overread as mammalian-style strategic cognition.

### Fighting, aggression, and predator defense
ADW records territorial nest defense and jabbing at neighboring pelicans and other waterbirds, with nests sometimes only about a meter apart. Outside dense breeding colonies, the species is highly social and generally not a specialized fighter. Flight and water access are the preferred defenses.

### Predation and feeding behavior
Surface feeder, not a plunge diver. Fish dominate the diet, supplemented by amphibians and crustaceans. The pouch is a temporary capture net rather than food storage. Nevada and California zoo sources describe cooperative corralling of fish in shallow water.

### Environmental advantages
- **Open water / shallows:** excellent swimming and cooperative feeding.
- **Thermal-rich open air:** exceptional transport efficiency and escape range.
- **Large lakes / coast:** broad operating space and social support.

### Environmental disadvantages
- **Dense terrestrial cover:** enormous wingspan becomes cumbersome.
- **Confined ground combat:** poor walking agility and limited damaging weapons.
- **No takeoff room:** removes its best defensive option.

### Weaknesses
Light flight-adapted skeleton and soft tissues, low weapon lethality for its size, awkward land movement, no armor, no strong bite-force evidence, and dependence on space for aerial disengagement.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Favored:** much smaller unarmored animals that cannot effectively reach or hold it, especially around water where the pelican can disengage.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized non-raptorial birds; reach and body size help, but the bill is not a dedicated killing weapon.
- **Unfavored:** raptors with talons, medium carnivorous mammals, constrictors, crocodilians and any opponent that can seize the bird before takeoff.

## 7. Abilities and traits

### Special abilities
1. **Cooperative Fish Corral** — groups coordinate on the water to herd fish toward shallow areas before scooping them, a grounded example of social positioning that can concentrate mobile prey.
2. **Thermal Soaring Economy** — very broad wings exploit rising air to sustain long-distance travel with reduced flapping cost, supporting exceptional movement endurance and aerial disengagement.

### Unique traits
1. **Expandable Gular Scoop** — the long bill and extensible throat pouch act as a large temporary capture net; water is drained before prey is swallowed rather than stored indefinitely.
2. **Seasonal Bill Plate** — breeding adults grow a distinctive temporary keratinous plate on the upper bill and shed it after the breeding period.

## 8. Expanded profile content

### Habitat and range
Breeds mainly on isolated islands in shallow inland lakes, rivers and marshes across western and central Canada and the United States. Winters chiefly on southern coasts, bays, estuaries and warm inland waters into Mexico and Central America. Some Gulf and Mexican populations are resident.

### Diet and ecology
Primarily fish, with crayfish, salamanders and other aquatic prey also taken. Surface feeding distinguishes it from the plunge-diving Brown Pelican. Colonies can be very large and breeding birds may commute long distances between nest and feeding water.

### Social structure
Highly gregarious: roosts, nests, migrates and often forages in groups. ADW reports colonies around 1,000 nests and large migratory formations. Nest territories themselves are small and defended aggressively enough for repeated jabbing interactions.

### Reproduction and life history
Typically lays about two eggs but commonly raises one chick successfully. Both parents care for young. Washington DFW reports females typically breed around age three, with young fledging at roughly 9–10 weeks. Dense colonies create both protection through aggregation and intense local competition.

### Conservation
IUCN status is **Least Concern**. Audubon and state agencies describe major historical recovery/increase, but nesting colonies remain sensitive to human disturbance, habitat degradation, water-level changes, disease and fishing-related hazards.

### Major adaptations
Large broad wings for soaring, fully webbed feet for propulsion, expandable gular pouch, buoyancy-enhancing air sacs, cooperative surface feeding, and seasonal breeding ornamentation.

### Human interaction
Usually not dangerous to people. Conservation conflicts can arise around fisheries, aquaculture, habitat management and disturbance of nesting colonies. Fishing line and hooks are important anthropogenic hazards.

### Genuine fun facts
- One of North America's largest flying birds, with wingspans approaching 2.9 m in Cornell measurements.
- Unlike Brown Pelicans, it normally catches fish from the surface instead of plunge-diving.
- The conspicuous breeding bill plate is temporary and is shed after breeding.
- The oldest wild individual reported by ADW reached 26.4 years.
- Satellite-tracked birds have used summer home ranges measured in the thousands of square kilometers.
- Its throat pouch is a capture tool, not a permanent food-storage bag.

### Concise site-ready summary
The American White Pelican is a roughly 7 kg soaring waterbird armed more with reach and mobility than raw violence. Its giant bill and expandable pouch excel at scooping fish, often through coordinated group corralling, while a near-3 m wingspan supports efficient migration and escape. In a direct fight it is less dangerous than a similar-sized raptor because it lacks killing talons and a crushing bite, but around open water and air its endurance, swimming and enormous flight surface make it difficult to contain.

### Narrative profile
A standing American White Pelican looks almost oversized for flight: a deep-bodied white bird, an extraordinarily long orange bill and a wing system spanning roughly the width of a small room. Yet its biology is built around efficient movement rather than brute-force combat. On water it uses webbed feet and buoyant anatomy to cruise through shallows, sometimes joining other pelicans to compress schools of fish into a catchable corridor. The bill then functions as a long-handled scoop, with the elastic pouch expanding around prey and water before drainage and swallowing.

That same body becomes far more elegant once airborne. Broad wings allow the pelican to climb on thermals and cover long distances with comparatively little flapping. Telemetry confirms that adult movement space can be enormous. In ABS terms, this makes Stamina and mobility much more important than the visual intimidation of the bill might suggest.

The bill is still relevant in conflict. Breeding birds defend crowded nest spaces by jabbing neighbors, and its reach is substantial. But the weapon should not be confused with a raptor's hooked beak and talons. The pelican lacks a high-force killing bite, heavy armor, or a grappling foot weapon. If a terrestrial carnivore gets hold of it before takeoff, the apparent size advantage collapses quickly. Its best battle strategy is therefore positional: maintain water or air access, use reach to discourage close approach, and disengage rather than trade trauma.

## 11. Confidence and source conflicts

- **Mass:** high confidence for the broad adult range. Sex-specific means vary among compilations, so 6.9 kg is deliberately representative rather than maximal.
- **Length/wingspan:** high confidence; Cornell and government assessment agree closely.
- **Height:** unresolved. Do not substitute total length.
- **Speed:** unresolved. Generic internet flight speeds and the live placeholder are rejected until species-specific measured maxima are located.
- **Lifespan:** typical-life sources range roughly 12–20+ years while the record is 26.4 years. Canonical 14 years represents normal wild adult longevity, not maximum lifespan.
- **Bite force:** unresolved and left at zero. No conversion from force to PSI is attempted.
- **Bill:** high confidence that males have longer culmens and that the breeding plate is temporary. The plate is not treated as a high-damage horn without evidence.

## 12. Cross-animal normalization notes

- At **6.9 kg**, Pelican must not approach medium mammalian predators in Raw Power, Attack or Defense simply because its 2.7 m wingspan makes it look enormous.
- **Attack 28.0** is close to Peacock 29.0 but for different reasons: Pelican has much greater reach and mass but less dedicated piercing weaponry than a spurred terrestrial male peafowl. It remains well below Golden Eagle 40.0 because the eagle's talons and predatory strike mechanics are far more lethal.
- **Defense 22.0** is only slightly above Peacock 19.0 and Golden Eagle 21.0. Large visual area is not armor.
- **Agility 72.0** stays far below Osprey 87.0 and Golden Eagle 88.0 because Pelican's soaring/swimming control is excellent but tight pursuit and aerial attack maneuverability are not its specialization.
- **Stamina 84.0** is intentionally high and slightly above Osprey 82.0 because migratory soaring and telemetry demonstrate enormous routine movement capacity. It remains below Ostrich 89.0, whose terrestrial sustained-running specialization has unusually strong biomechanical support.
- **Special 73.0** rewards the unusual pouch/buoyancy/cooperative-feeding system without treating it as direct lethality. It remains below Osprey 83.0 because the osprey's fishing adaptations are more directly weaponized.
- No proportional-strength leakage, speed/agility substitution, unsupported PSI, or duplicate counting of wingspan as raw power was used.
