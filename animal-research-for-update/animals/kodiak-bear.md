# Kodiak Bear (*Ursus arctos middendorffi*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Kodiak bear (Kodiak brown bear)
- **Scientific name:** *Ursus arctos middendorffi* Merriam, 1896
- **Exact animal represented:** the brown bear population of the Kodiak Archipelago, Alaska. It is one of the two North American brown bear subspecies recognized by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service; the other is the mainland grizzly (*U. a. horribilis*), which the roster already covers separately.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **480.0 kg**, in average active-season condition, about **140.0 cm** shoulder height on all fours. This is a representative big male, not the 680 kg pre-denning peak or a record trophy animal.
- **Sex choice:** males are much larger. ADF&G states that females are about 20% smaller and 30% lighter than males, and males are the sex that competes for mates and dominance at feeding sites.
- **Population variation:** body mass swings strongly with season. Brown bears are heaviest just before denning and lose a large share of their mass over winter, so a spring male can weigh far less than the same bear in autumn. The canonical value is an across-the-year figure.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- ADF&G Kodiak fact sheet: large males can weigh up to **1,500 lb (680 kg)**.
- Wikipedia's Kodiak bear article, compiling field data: mature males average about **477–534 kg** across the year and reach about 680 kg at peak; adult males commonly span roughly 272–544 kg in the ranges quoted by summary sources.
- **Canonical:** **480.0 kg** mature male.
- **Confidence:** Moderate. Seasonal swings and hunter-kill measurements (often estimated, not weighed) make a single figure uncertain.

### Height
- ADF&G: a large male stands about **5 ft (152 cm)** at the shoulder on all fours and over **10 ft (3 m)** when standing on its hind legs.
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 140.0 cm** shoulder height for a representative rather than maximum male.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Length
- No direct head-body length series for Kodiak males was returned in this run. A standing bear's nose-to-heel height is a rough proxy for its nose-to-rump length; ADF&G's 10 ft standing height is for large males.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 250.0 cm** head-body length, a **low-confidence derived estimate** that discounts the large-male standing height to a representative male. Replace it when a measured series is available.

### Speed
- ADF&G's brown bear species profile describes short bursts of up to about 40 mph (64 km/h) for brown bears in general; this is not an instrumented measurement of a Kodiak male.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 15.6 m/s (35 mph)**, the same conservative value the roster uses for the lighter grizzly, kept below the 40 mph agency maximum because of the Kodiak's greater mass. **Low-moderate confidence.**

### Lifespan
- ADF&G: the oldest known wild Kodiak female was 34 years old and the oldest male 27. Summary sources give a typical wild lifespan of roughly 20–25 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 20.0 years**, because males live shorter lives than females and are heavily hunted.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-low.

### Bite and dentition
- Christiansen (2007) modelled bite mechanics across bear species; no Kodiak-specific pressure value with contact area exists.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0** (unresolved; not an absence of bite).

### Natural weapons
- **Claws:** ADF&G describes brown bear claws as longer and straighter than black bear claws, used for digging roots and excavating small mammals. No Kodiak-specific representative claw length was returned in this run.
- **Canines and jaws:** large canines and heavy jaws; used for seizing salmon, carcasses and rivals.
- **Forelimbs:** the shoulder hump is a large mass of digging muscle that powers forelimb strikes and pinning.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Thick fur, a thick layer of autumn fat and very large body mass give substantial protection against smaller attackers. Head and neck are the target areas in male-male fights.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons and offensive mechanics
The Kodiak bear fights with mass, forelimb strikes, long digging claws and a heavy bite. A male of nearly half a tonne can knock down, pin and bite an opponent, and the shoulder hump gives the forelimbs great power in short strikes and pulls. It is not a specialised killer like a big cat: its weapons are built as much for digging, fishing and tearing open carcasses as for subduing prey. Its offense comes from overwhelming size rather than a refined killing technique.

### Hunting and foraging behavior
Kodiak bears are omnivores. In spring they graze sedges in salt marshes and alpine meadows and scavenge winter-killed animals; in summer and autumn they gather at salmon streams and berry patches. Salmon and red elderberry are the two most important summer foods, and a recent study showed that Kodiak bears keep eating salmon even when berries ripen at the same time. They take deer and occasionally young elk and livestock, but hunting large mammals is not their main way of life.

### Intraspecific fighting
At crowded salmon streams, Kodiak bears manage conflict through a rich language of body posture, threat displays and displacement, so serious fights are uncommon. During the mating season males roam widely and fight rivals, and adult males sometimes kill cubs. Females with cubs avoid concentrations of males and will fight much larger males to protect their young.

### Defense and durability
Mass, fat and a deep frame make the Kodiak one of the hardest land predators to injure seriously. The main weaknesses are the face, neck and eyes, and the absence of armor against penetrating tusks, horns or large bites.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Surprisingly fast in a short burst for its size, and competent on steep slopes, in rivers and through dense alder. Inertia limits quick turns, fine evasion and sustained sprinting. Adults rarely climb trees.

### Endurance
Bears travel long distances between seasonal food sources and can stand in cold rivers fishing for hours. Burst exertion, especially in warm weather, is costly for an animal this heavy.

### Senses
Excellent sense of smell, which guides them to carcasses, salmon and other bears. Hearing is good and vision is adequate.

### Intelligence and tactics
Kodiak bears learn fishing techniques, remember seasonal food locations across the archipelago, and handle crowded feeding sites through social signalling rather than constant fighting. That is strong behavioral flexibility for a large carnivore.

### Aggression and ferocity
Generally conflict-avoidant toward people and each other when food is plentiful, but dangerous when surprised at close range, defending cubs or defending a carcass.

### Environmental strengths
- Coastal forest, salmon streams, estuaries and alpine meadows of the Kodiak Archipelago.
- Cold and wet conditions.
- Shallow rivers where its mass and fishing skill dominate.

### Environmental limitations
- Heat, which limits sustained exertion.
- Open ground against faster, more agile opponents that can keep their distance.
- Trees and cliffs that lighter climbers can use to escape.

### Weaknesses
- Low maneuverability and slow turns relative to cats and canids.
- No armor over the face, neck and eyes.
- Weapons built as much for digging and fishing as for killing.
- High energy cost of bursts at nearly half a tonne.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** most medium and large predators, smaller bears, deer-sized ungulates, anything that commits to close contact without comparable mass.
- **Mixed:** polar bears and large bovids, where mass and weapons are closer to even.
- **Bad:** megafauna (elephants, rhinos, hippos), large crocodilians in water, and fast opponents that avoid the grapple.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Hump-Powered Swipe** — the large shoulder hump of digging muscle drives short, powerful forelimb strikes and pins, so a Kodiak can knock an opponent down or hold it before biting.
2. **Salmon-Stream Diplomacy** — a detailed language of postures, threat displays and displacement lets many large bears share one stream with few serious fights, and lets a Kodiak judge a rival before committing.

### Unique traits
1. **Island Colossus** — the Kodiak Archipelago's brown bears are among the largest bears in the world, with big males reaching about 680 kg before denning.
2. **Isolated Island Population** — around 3,500 Kodiak bears live only on the Kodiak Archipelago, genetically isolated from mainland brown bears and with very little genetic diversity.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Kodiak bears live only on the islands of the Kodiak Archipelago in southwestern Alaska. They use almost every habitat on the islands: low-elevation salt marshes in spring, salmon streams and berry patches in summer, and alpine slopes, where they dig dens, in winter.

### Diet and ecology
Omnivores that follow seasonal food. Sedges and other plants in spring, several salmon species during summer and autumn runs, and berries such as salmonberry, red elderberry, blueberry and devil's club fruit. Carrion, deer and occasional larger mammals add protein. Bears move salmon nutrients from streams into the surrounding forest.

### Social structure
Mostly solitary but tolerant at rich food sources, where many bears gather and a dominance order forms. Males roam widely in the breeding season. Females with cubs keep away from adult males.

### Reproduction and life history
Mating takes place in late spring and early summer. Cubs are born in the winter den and stay with their mother for about two to three years. The main threat to cubs is adult male bears. Females are known to live longer in the wild than males.

### Conservation
The brown bear species (*Ursus arctos*) is assessed as **Least Concern** globally; the Kodiak subspecies is not separately listed. The Kodiak population, about 3,500 bears (3,526 estimated in 2005), is healthy and closely managed by ADF&G and the Kodiak National Wildlife Refuge. Its low genetic diversity may make it more vulnerable to new diseases or parasites.

### Special features
- Very large body mass that swings strongly by season.
- Shoulder hump of digging muscle.
- Long, fairly straight front claws for digging.
- Keen sense of smell.
- Fasting and denning physiology for surviving winter without feeding.

### Human interaction
Kodiak bears are a major draw for bear viewing and regulated hunting. Attacks on people are rare; most follow surprise encounters at close range or defense of cubs or food. Securing food and garbage reduces conflict.

### Genuine fun facts
- A large male can stand over 3 m (10 ft) tall on its hind legs.
- Big males can weigh about 680 kg (1,500 lb) before denning.
- The oldest known wild Kodiak female lived to 34; the oldest male to 27.
- Females are about 20% smaller and 30% lighter than males.
- Kodiak bears kept eating salmon even when red elderberries ripened at the same time, according to a 2023 diet study.
- The whole population, around 3,500 bears, lives on one island group.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Kodiak bear is a giant island brown bear from Alaska's Kodiak Archipelago, with big males weighing close to half a tonne. It wins through sheer mass, a powerful shoulder-driven swipe, long claws and a heavy bite, backed by thick fat and fur. Its weaknesses are slow turning, the lack of armor and weapons built as much for fishing and digging as for killing.

### Rich narrative profile
The Kodiak bear is what a brown bear becomes when food is abundant and there is no competition from other large carnivores. On the islands of the Kodiak Archipelago, rich salmon runs and berry crops support bears that routinely reach sizes the interior grizzly never approaches. A representative adult male of around 480 kg is more than twice the roster's grizzly, and in autumn a big male can carry nearly 700 kg into the den.

That size is the core of its fighting ability. A forelimb strike powered by the shoulder hump can knock down a large animal, and once on top the bear's weight and bite do the rest. Thick fat and fur and a very deep body make it hard for smaller predators to reach anything vital. But the Kodiak is a generalist rather than a killer by design. Its claws are for digging, its day is spent grazing, fishing and eating berries, and it avoids fights it does not need. Against fast, agile opponents that refuse to close, its heavy frame becomes a handicap.

Kodiak bears are also socially skilled. At salmon streams dozens of bears can feed close together, reading one another's postures to avoid costly fights. In a matchup that translates into good judgement: a Kodiak tends to test an opponent before committing, then uses its mass decisively. Against almost any land predator it holds the size advantage; only megafauna, big crocodilians in water and the polar bear meet it on equal or better terms.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Grizzly Bear (220 kg; A66.0 D59.0 Ag59.0 St75.0 I75.0 Sp63.0):** same species, more than twice the canonical mass. Attack (+11.0) and Defense (+12.0) rise strongly; Agility falls 4.0 for inertia; Stamina is 3.0 lower because burst work is costlier and food is concentrated; Intelligence is 1.0 lower (similar cognition, less evidence of the interior grizzly's problem-solving range); Special is 2.0 lower.
- **Versus Polar Bear (500 kg; A78.0 D72.0 Ag62.0 St88.0 Sp77.0):** nearly the same mass. The polar bear keeps a small Attack lead because it is a true predator of large marine mammals, a small Defense lead from blubber, and a large Stamina and Special lead from its long-distance swimming and ice-hunting systems. The Kodiak's forelimb digging power gives it a marginally higher Raw Power substat.
- **Versus Bengal and Siberian Tigers (A85.0 and A84.0):** the Kodiak is more than twice as heavy and far more durable (Defense 71.0 against about 61), but the tigers keep higher Attack and much higher Agility because their weapons are built for killing and their movement for precise ambush.
- **Versus Cape Buffalo (750 kg; A76.0 D70.0):** close overall; the bear's claws and bite put Attack slightly above, while the buffalo's heavier body and boss keep Defense close.
- **Anti-inflation check:** size is not counted three times. Mass feeds Size, Raw Power and Toughness, but Weaponry, Agility and Special are held down.
