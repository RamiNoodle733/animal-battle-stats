# Moose

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Roster name:** Moose
- **Scientific name:** *Alces alces* (with some modern authorities splitting North American moose as *Alces americanus*; the roster-level profile retains the broadly used *A. alces* treatment and documents the conflict rather than silently changing taxa)
- **Living/extinct:** Living
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime mature bull in hard-antler rut condition, approximately **550.0 kg**, **200.0 cm shoulder height**, and **300.0 cm total length**. This represents a large adult male without using the 771 kg Alaska record cited by ADW or the 1,697 lb Kenai record cited by NPS.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** strong. ADW reports males 360-600 kg versus females 270-400 kg and males more than 40% heavier. Only bulls normally carry antlers.
- **Population variation:** Alaska moose (*A. a. gigas* in the broad species treatment) are the largest population form. Eurasian and other North American populations can be substantially smaller. The canonical 550 kg bull is intentionally near the upper end of ordinary mature-male scale because males are the fighting sex, but below documented records.

### Defensive structures and weapons
A bull's main weapon is its broad palmate antler rack with projecting tines, driven by a massive neck and shoulder complex. NPS field research reports that rutting neck musculature can expand to about twice normal size, while forehead skin is thick and protective against punctures. Hooves and long legs provide a powerful secondary kicking/stomping system. Moose lack true body armor, so flank, abdomen and legs remain vulnerable to penetrating trauma.

## 4. Combat biology

### Offensive mechanics
Prime bulls are specialized seasonal wrestlers. NPS Denali observations distinguish harmless sparring from genuine fights. In serious contests bulls clash antlers, push, twist an opponent's head, try to force rivals backward or down, seek uphill leverage, and may gore with antler points. Serious injuries and occasional deaths occur. The antler palm also works as a shield while tines can puncture body tissue or injure eyes. Long forelegs and hard hooves add dangerous kicks and stomps when an opponent gets past the rack.

### Defense and toughness
At roughly 550 kg, simple body mass makes the canonical bull difficult for small and medium predators to stop. The heavy shoulder girdle and rut-swollen neck support violent collisions. Thick forehead skin protects the area most exposed in antler contests. This is still not passive armor: large carnivores can exploit hindquarters, abdomen and legs, especially in deep or crusted snow.

### Locomotion and agility
Moose combine very long legs with large splayed hooves adapted to snow, mud and wetlands. They can move rapidly on land and are powerful swimmers. Their large size and wide antler rack reduce fine turning precision compared with smaller cervids, but field fighting shows meaningful balance and positional skill, including deliberate use of slope advantage.

### Endurance
Moose are capable swimmers, extensive browsers and in some populations long-distance seasonal migrants. However, rutting bulls can cease or sharply reduce feeding while investing heavily in reproductive competition, creating energetic costs. Stamina is therefore strong but not near the roster ceiling.

### Senses
Moose rely strongly on smell and hearing, with broad mobile ears and strong predator/rival detection. Vision is useful but is not treated as an exceptional specialist system. Rut behavior includes chemical communication through urine wallows and flehmen investigation.

### Intelligence and tactics
This is not a high-cognition specialist, but combat behavior is not purely mechanical. Bulls assess rivals, display before escalation, learn through sparring when young, recognize dominance, seek uphill advantage in fights and disengage after defeat. Those are grounded tactical behaviors without implying primate/corvid cognition.

### Fighting and aggression
NPS Denali field work found large, high-ranking males performed 88% of observed mating. Serious fights were usually between mature bulls of similar size. Unlike practice sparring, true fights can produce severe wounds or death. Rutting bulls are consequently a much more aggressive canonical combat state than ordinary solitary browsing moose.

### Predator defense
Healthy adults can deter wolves, bears and other predators through size, kicks, charges and antlers when present. Deep crusted snow is a major vulnerability because it can restrict movement while wolves travel more efficiently. Calves are far more vulnerable and are not relevant to the canonical specimen.

### Environmental strengths and limitations
**Best:** cool boreal forest edges, wetlands, shallow water, snow where broad hooves remain effective, and open rutting terrain with room for the rack.

**Weak:** heat. Moose are strongly cold-adapted and seek shade/water in warm conditions. Dense woody tangles constrain the antler rack. Deep crusted snow can reverse the normal long-leg advantage. Aquatic mobility is excellent for a terrestrial ungulate but does not make the animal an aquatic fighter.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favorable:** smaller terrestrial mammals forced into frontal range; similarly sized lightly armored opponents vulnerable to goring, trampling or displacement; opponents that cannot absorb a 550 kg collision.

**Unfavorable:** much heavier megafauna, heavily armored animals, large predators able to repeatedly attack the rear/flanks, and small highly evasive opponents that can avoid the frontal weapon zone.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings
- **Attack: 69.0** - A 550 kg prime bull combines a huge palmate rack, sharp tines, neck-driven collision force and hoof strikes. It should sit above Elk (62.0) and Kudu (58.0), but below the most destructive megafauna and giant macropredators.
- **Defense: 50.0** - Size, robust shoulders and forehead protection are substantial, but the torso and limbs are not armored.
- **Agility: 58.0** - Remarkably mobile for its mass and competent in snow/water, while broad antlers and bulk constrain fine maneuvering.
- **Stamina: 70.0** - Strong swimming, migration/ranging and sustained browsing movement, moderated by rut costs and heat sensitivity.
- **Intelligence: 55.0** - Direct evidence supports rival assessment, learning through sparring and positional tactics, not broad high-order cognition.
- **Special: 60.0** - Annual giant antlers, snow-adapted hooves and wetland competence meaningfully change matchups, but none is a rare bypass system comparable with venom or electricity.

## 7. Abilities and traits

### Special abilities
1. **Palisade Rack** - the broad palmate antler shield carries projecting tines and is used to absorb clashes, twist rivals, shove, gore and control frontal space during the rut.
2. **Bog-and-Snow Stride** - extremely long legs and broad hooves let a massive animal cross wetlands, soft snow and shallow water while retaining unusual mobility for its size.

### Unique traits
1. **Rut-Neck Surge** - NPS field research reports bull neck musculature expanding dramatically during the rut, reinforcing the body region that powers and absorbs antler combat.
2. **Slope Fighter** - observed bulls actively seek uphill position during serious fights to improve battering leverage, a rare directly documented positional tactic in a giant ungulate.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Moose occupy circumpolar northern forests, shrublands, wetlands, lake margins and boreal landscapes across North America and Eurasia under the broad *Alces alces* treatment. They are closely tied to cool climates and abundant woody browse.

### Diet and ecology
They are generalist browsers. USDA Forest Service synthesis reports heavy use of willow, aspen and birch, with leaves, stems, buds and bark forming most of the annual diet; aquatic vegetation is important seasonally. Their browsing can materially alter plant-community structure.

### Social structure
Moose are among the least social cervids and are usually solitary outside breeding. During the rut, temporary groups form around receptive cows and dominant bulls. Sociality therefore does not inflate individual power.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding occurs in autumn. Prime fighting/breeding performance in Denali bulls develops around seven years, with bulls remaining prime to roughly eleven. Cows generally bear one or two calves after an approximately eight-month gestation. Antlers are grown annually and shed after breeding.

### Conservation
At the global species level moose are generally treated as Least Concern, though regional populations can decline from habitat change, parasites, predation, hunting pressure and warming-related stress.

### Adaptations
- long legs for snow, marshes and browsing
- broad hooves that spread load on soft substrate
- hollow insulating guard hairs and dense underfur
- huge seasonal palmate antlers in males
- flexible muzzle/upper lip for browsing
- strong swimming ability

### Human interaction
Moose are hunted across much of their range and are a major vehicle-collision hazard because of their height and mass. Rutting bulls and cows defending calves can be dangerous at close range. Reputation alone is not used as a score input.

### Fun facts
- NPS reports mature Interior Alaska bull antler pairs averaging roughly 45-50 lb, with exceptional pairs up to 75 lb.
- Prime bulls can grow antler tissue at roughly an inch per day during the fastest growth period.
- Serious bull fights can be fatal, while ordinary sparring is largely practice and assessment.
- Bulls have been observed seeking uphill position to gain leverage in fights.
- Moose are powerful enough swimmers to travel several miles and feed in aquatic habitats.
- Their long hollow hairs are a major cold-weather insulation system.

### Concise site summary
The moose is the world's largest living deer. A prime bull is a half-tonne seasonal combat specialist carrying a broad multi-tined rack, powerful hooves and enormous rut-conditioned neck and shoulders. It combines severe frontal damage potential with surprising snow, wetland and swimming mobility, but lacks true body armor and remains vulnerable to flanking predators and heat.

### Narrative profile
A prime bull moose is built around frontal control. The rack is not decoration: field observations show mature bulls crashing antlers, twisting heads, driving rivals backward, seeking slope advantage and occasionally inflicting fatal wounds. Behind that rack is a massive shoulder complex and a neck that becomes substantially more muscular during the rut. If an opponent bypasses the antlers, the long legs still carry hard hooves capable of dangerous kicks and stomps.

Its size does not make it clumsy in the simplistic sense. Long legs and broad hooves carry it through marshes and snow, and moose are excellent swimmers. Yet this mobility is terrain-dependent. Crusted deep snow can expose it to wolf attacks, dense cover can interfere with a broad rack, and heat stresses a body designed for northern climates. On the ABS scale the moose should therefore be a high-attack, high-power ungulate with moderate defense and agility rather than an armored tank or an all-purpose megafaunal ceiling.

### Future field proposals
- Instrument prime bulls with high-rate GPS/IMU tags to measure true sprint acceleration and turning performance.
- Quantify antler impact impulse and tine penetration loads during naturalistic collision tests using shed racks.
- Publish representative bull hoof-strike force and antler-rack mass distributions by subspecies/population.
- Compare heat-limited endurance with cool-condition endurance under standardized protocols.
- Resolve North American *Alces americanus* versus broad *A. alces* taxonomy consistently before production migration.

### Confidence and conflicts
**High confidence:** adult male is the correct fighting sex; broad mass/length/height ranges; antler combat; rut aggression; sexual dimorphism. **Moderate confidence:** canonical 550 kg as a representative large prime bull across geographically variable populations; 15.6 m/s top speed because sources are reputable but not primary sprint measurements. **Low/not defensible:** bite pressure, so 0.0 PSI. Taxonomy remains a real conflict: some authorities recognize North American moose as *A. americanus*, while many references and the roster use *A. alces* broadly.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes
- **Elk:** Moose is ~1.7x the canonical Elk mass (550 vs 320 kg) and has a heavier, broader palmate rack. Attack **69.0** versus Elk **62.0** and Size **66.0** versus Elk **56.0** preserve that absolute gap without making the moose megafaunal-ceiling level.
- **Greater Kudu:** Moose substantially exceeds the 230 kg Kudu in mass and collision leverage, justifying Attack **69.0** versus **58.0** despite both having dangerous head weapons.
- **Llama:** 550 kg versus 150 kg plus specialized rut weaponry creates a large power/weapon gap.
- **Jaguar/Leopard:** Moose exceeds them massively in size and frontal collision power, but cats retain superior maneuverability and killing specialization. Attack similarity must not imply identical combat style.
- **Megalodon:** Moose remains far below the 100.0 Attack ceiling and other giant megafaunal anchors. No proportional-strength leakage is used.
- **Agility check:** 58.0 is intentionally far below Mongoose/Leopard because speed is not agility; the moose's bulk and rack constrain fine turning despite respectable running speed.
- **Double-counting check:** antlers primarily drive Weaponry/Attack; body mass and forehead protection drive Defense; snow/wetland locomotion supports Agility/Special. The same adaptation is not used to inflate every category.
