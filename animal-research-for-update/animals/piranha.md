# Piranha

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Roster name:** Piranha
- **Resolved taxon:** Red-bellied piranha, *Pygocentrus nattereri* Kner, 1858, family Serrasalmidae.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime **sexually mature adult**, **1.2 kg**, **30.0 cm total length**. Sex is left unspecified because reliable external sexual dimorphism is weak and a 2026 field study found overlapping male/female size distributions rather than a consistent larger fighting sex.
- **Population variation:** the species is widespread in tropical South America. Color and body size vary among populations. A Central Amazon floodplain study estimated asymptotic standard length at 29.4 cm, while zoo/reference sources commonly give 28-35 cm adults and exceptional values can be larger.

The live roster already resolves Piranha to *P. nattereri*. This report therefore researches that species rather than treating “piranha” as the whole Serrasalmidae assemblage.

## 2. Physical dimensions and ranges

### Mass
A representative canonical mass of **1.2 kg** is used. Museum/zoo summaries report adults up to several kilograms, including MNHN at up to 3.5 kg and Oregon Zoo at up to 4 lb (~1.8 kg). A 2026 wild biometric sample was much lighter: males ranged 113.2-524.2 g and females 145.2-593.5 g in the sampled seasons. The ABS value therefore represents a robust mature fish rather than a maximum specimen, while acknowledging that wild populations and husbandry conditions vary substantially.

### Length
**30.0 cm total length** is the canonical field. A Central Amazon growth study estimated asymptotic standard length at **29.4 cm**; Seriously Fish lists 350 mm standard length as an upper aquarium reference, while MNHN gives 28-33 cm. The exact distinction between standard length and total length matters, so the 30 cm canonical field should not be interpreted as a species maximum.

### Height
`height_cm = 0.0`. A standardized standing height is not meaningful for this fish and should not be invented from body depth.

### Speed
`speed_mps = 0.0`. No defensible instrumented species-specific maximum swimming speed was recovered. The legacy 32 km/h value is therefore rejected rather than recycled. Red-bellied piranhas can accelerate rapidly during attacks and evasive swimming, but acceleration is not evidence for a verified top-speed field.

### Lifespan
`lifespan_years = 10.0`. MNHN reports up to 10 years in captivity and Oregon Zoo says about 10 years. Other husbandry sources report 10-20 years, so 10.0 is a conservative canonical value rather than a maximum-longevity claim.

### Bite force and tooth mechanics
`bite_force_psi = 0.0`. This does **not** mean a weak bite. It means force in newtons cannot honestly be converted to pressure in PSI without a defensible contact area and standardized protocol.

Published bite evidence is strong. A 2023 study measured juvenile voluntary bites averaging about 1.19 N with a maximum of 9.71 N. Its literature synthesis reports restrained adult *P. nattereri* bites averaging **84 ± 68 N** for specimens averaging 182 ± 55 mm standard length, while noting restrained measurements can overstate natural feeding output. A 2026 biomechanical model for a 0.728 kg, 167 mm standard-length *P. nattereri* estimated **18.8 N anterior** and **42.0 N posterior** bite force, with posterior bite stress about 3308.9 kPa. These are force/stress results, not a warrant to publish a single PSI number.

The teeth are triangular, serrated and interlocking. They act as cutting blades rather than mammalian crushing molars. This geometry is the key weapon advantage.

### Defenses
There is no armor comparable with a cory catfish's scutes. Defense is mainly a deep compact body, rapid aquatic maneuvering, shoaling, vigilance and avoidance. The species is itself prey for larger fishes, caimans, birds and other predators.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offensive mechanics
The primary weapon is a short, deep jaw armed with a single row of interlocking triangular cutting teeth. Rather than needing to swallow prey whole, the fish can seize and remove tissue. Experimental predator-prey work shows red-bellied piranhas preferentially succeed when bites reach vulnerable unarmored regions such as the opercular opening and caudal peduncle. Armored cory catfish survived 37% of confirmed bites without damage, demonstrating that famous teeth are not armor-negating magic.

A piranha's useful attack sequence is approach, rapid burst, targeted bite, withdrawal or repeated bites. The fish can exploit fins, tails, eyes and exposed tissue. Against a much larger opponent, however, one 1.2 kg individual cannot be scored as though an entire shoal is present.

### Locomotion and agility
A laterally compressed teleost body, strong caudal propulsion and paired fins permit fast short-range corrections in water. This supports high maneuverability relative to many terrestrial animals, but the fish is completely environment-bound. Out of water its combat mobility collapses.

### Endurance and recovery
The species is an active freshwater forager rather than an extreme migratory endurance specialist. It can sustain normal swimming and repeated short attacks, but there is no basis for ranking it with long-distance pelagic fishes or elite endurance birds and mammals. Recovery is ordinary vertebrate tissue recovery, with no exceptional regeneration mechanism.

### Senses
Vision, olfaction and the fish lateral-line system provide useful aquatic detection. The species also produces sounds. Peer-reviewed work links three sound types with three behavioral contexts and shows swimbladder-associated sonic muscles operating around 100 Hz for one sound mechanism. This is communication and threat signaling, not a damaging sonic attack.

### Intelligence and tactics
The red-bellied piranha is behaviorally more nuanced than its movie reputation. A field-derived shoaling experiment found non-random partner choice: shoals were size structured, with large sexually mature fish tending toward central positions. The same work found no evidence of routine intraspecific aggression in its tests. Predatory work documents attacks aimed at vulnerable body regions. These support moderate tactics and social decision-making, but not high mammalian/bird cognition.

### Shoaling, fighting and predation
Shoaling should be treated primarily as social/antipredator behavior rather than an automatic “pack hunting multiplier.” Mature fish can occupy central shoal positions, and schooling reduces individual predation risk. Feeding aggregations can exploit concentrated resources, but the canonical ABS fighter remains one animal.

Intraspecific aggression and biting can occur, particularly under constrained or feeding conditions, yet the stereotype of constant cannibalistic frenzy is not supported as a normal baseline. Adults are opportunistic generalists consuming fish, aquatic invertebrates, insects and also plant material such as seeds and fruits. Scavenging is ecologically important.

### Environmental strengths
- Warm freshwater with room for maneuver.
- Vegetated rivers, lakes, floodplains and flooded forest providing cover.
- Close aquatic encounters where cutting bites can reach fins or soft tissue.
- Situations where repeated hit-and-withdraw attacks are possible.

### Environmental weaknesses
- Any terrestrial arena or removal from water.
- Armored, thick-shelled or strongly scuted opponents.
- Much larger aquatic predators able to swallow or crush the fish.
- Opponents too fast or too well protected for repeated bites to accumulate damage.
- Reliance on close contact to inflict damage.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Favorable:** smaller soft-bodied aquatic animals and similarly sized fish lacking strong armor or dangerous spines.
- **Competitive:** similar-sized predatory fish where first bite, maneuverability and vulnerable-target access matter.
- **Unfavorable:** armored fish, toxic/spined prey, much larger piscivores and aquatic reptiles.
- **Nonviable:** terrestrial opponents in a dry arena, because normal locomotion and respiration constraints dominate the matchup.

## 7. Abilities and traits

### Special abilities
1. **Interlocking Shear Bite** — serrated triangular teeth meet in a blade-like arrangement that punctures and slices tissue efficiently, allowing a small fish to remove chunks rather than overpower prey by mass.
2. **Vulnerability Targeting** — observed predatory attacks exploit less-protected regions such as fins, caudal peduncle and opercular openings, increasing the practical value of repeated bites against imperfect armor.

### Unique traits
1. **Size-Structured Shoal** — mature large individuals show non-random social positioning within shoals, supporting predator avoidance and context-dependent group behavior without treating the group as one fighter.
2. **Swimbladder Signal System** — specialized sonic muscles and the swimbladder produce behavior-linked acoustic signals, including a mechanism driven around 100 Hz.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Red-bellied piranhas occupy freshwater systems across tropical South America, including the Amazon, Paraguay-Paraná and Essequibo drainages and northeastern Brazilian coastal systems. Typical habitats include rivers, streams, lakes, floodplains and seasonally flooded forest. They commonly use vegetated or structurally complex water.

### Diet and ecology
They are opportunistic generalists, not obligate flesh-only predators. Natural food includes fishes, aquatic invertebrates, insects, worms, crustaceans, carrion, seeds, nuts, fruits and other plant material. Their role therefore spans predation, scavenging and omnivorous foraging.

### Social structure
Shoaling is important and has a substantial antipredator component. Research on Amazon-caught fish found size-assorted social choices and mature individuals disproportionately occupying central shoal positions. Group living should not be confused with permanent coordinated pack hunting.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding is associated with seasonal hydrology and rainfall. Zoo accounts describe circular courtship behavior, nest preparation/defense and parental guarding by males. Females lay large clutches of eggs that males fertilize. Exact reproductive timing varies geographically with flood cycles.

### Conservation and human interaction
The species is widespread and locally common. Conservation listings vary among databases, with some institutional sources treating it as not evaluated and general references listing Least Concern. It is harvested as food, caught by anglers and traded internationally as an aquarium fish. Human danger is heavily sensationalized: bites can be serious, but routine attacks on healthy humans are not the normal ecology of the species.

### Major adaptations
- Deep laterally compressed body for maneuvering.
- Strong jaw-adductor system.
- Interlocking triangular serrated teeth.
- Burst swimming and rapid close-range repositioning.
- Lateral-line mechanosensation and chemical/visual sensing.
- Behavior-linked acoustic signaling.
- Flexible omnivorous/scavenging diet.
- Shoaling that reduces predation risk.

### Genuine fun facts
- A 2026 biomechanical analysis found a modeled 0.728 kg *P. nattereri* posterior bite force of 42.0 N, illustrating how effective the jaw is without resorting to fake PSI.
- Juvenile voluntary bites in a 2023 experiment were far lower than stressed/restraint-derived measurements, showing how testing method changes the apparent “bite force.”
- Armored cory catfish survived 37% of confirmed juvenile piranha bites without damage in experimental encounters.
- Mature red-bellied piranhas can preferentially occupy the center of a shoal.
- The species produces multiple behavior-linked sounds using both swimbladder-associated and jaw mechanisms.
- Its natural diet includes plant foods, so the classic image of a strict meat-only predator is wrong.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Red-bellied Piranha is a small freshwater cutter whose reputation exceeds its absolute size. A mature fish combines interlocking serrated teeth, quick aquatic maneuvering, vulnerable-target biting and sophisticated shoaling behavior. Its bite is formidable for a roughly kilogram-scale animal, but it has little armor and should never receive the combat power of an entire school in a one-animal matchup.

### Rich narrative profile
The piranha's danger is mechanical precision rather than brute size. At only around 30 cm, it cannot wrestle with large predators, but it does not need mammalian mass to make its mouth effective. The jaw closes a row of sharp triangular teeth into a compact shearing system. A bite can puncture and slice exposed tissue, and repeated attacks can exploit fins, eyes, the tail base or other weak points.

That specialization also defines the limits. Experimental armored prey can survive bites by presenting protected surfaces, and a piranha remains a small fish vulnerable to larger aquatic predators. Shoaling helps solve that ecological problem, but ABS must not turn a defensive social strategy into multiplied individual attack power. The canonical animal is one fish.

Behaviorally, *P. nattereri* is also less cartoonish than its reputation. It chooses shoaling partners non-randomly, uses group position, produces context-linked sounds and can direct attacks toward vulnerable anatomy. It is simultaneously predator, scavenger, omnivore and prey. In a water-based matchup it is an agile specialist with a disproportionately effective cutting weapon. Outside water, its specialization becomes an overwhelming liability.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Body size:** field samples and asymptotic length estimates are materially smaller than some zoo/reference maxima. The canonical 1.2 kg / 30 cm specimen avoids both tiny sample means and exceptional 3.5 kg claims.
- **Sex:** no consistent larger/primary fighting sex was established, so sex is not guessed from appearance.
- **Bite:** voluntary, restrained and modeled bite values differ strongly. ABS therefore records no fabricated PSI.
- **Lifespan:** institutional values range from roughly 10 years to substantially longer husbandry claims. 10.0 years is conservative.
- **Speed:** no defensible species-specific maximum was found. Legacy 32 km/h is rejected.
- **Conservation:** institutional databases disagree between Not Evaluated and Least Concern presentations; the report does not overstate status certainty.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- At **1.2 kg**, Piranha must remain far below Otter (12 kg), Ostrich (130 kg), Oryx (230 kg), Orca (5000 kg) and megafauna in absolute Raw Power, Attack and Defense.
- Its **Attack 37.0** can slightly exceed similarly small animals with weak natural weapons because the cutting dentition is unusually effective, but it remains below the 5 kg Golden Eagle's 40.0 Attack and vastly below heavyweight predators.
- **Agility 82.0** is high because aquatic turning/control can genuinely favor a small fish. It is still below the Peregrine Falcon's 96.0 and does not depend on the unsupported legacy speed value.
- **Special 67.0** rewards unusual shearing dentition and aquatic sensory/acoustic systems without treating ordinary schooling as a supernatural swarm ability.
- No proportional-strength leakage is allowed: impressive bite performance per unit mass does not make a 1.2 kg fish physically comparable with large carnivores.
- No double-counting: tooth mechanics drive Weaponry/Attack; shoaling mainly informs Tactics/social ecology; acoustic signaling is a Special/communication feature, not damage.
