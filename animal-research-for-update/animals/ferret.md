# Ferret

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Domestic ferret / Ferret
- **Preferred current taxon:** *Mustela furo* Linnaeus, 1758. ITIS and the ASM Mammal Diversity Database currently treat the domestic ferret as a species; *Mustela putorius furo* remains a widely used synonym/subspecies treatment and is the name currently used by the live ABS schema, USFWS, ADW and NCBI.
- **Living/extinct:** Living, domesticated.
- **Exact animal represented:** Domestic ferret, not the black-footed ferret (*Mustela nigripes*) and not a wild European polecat (*M. putorius*).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** Healthy, intact, mature male (hob), approximately **1.5 kg**. Males are materially larger and more muscular than females, making the intact adult male the appropriate representative fighting sex.
- **Dimorphism/variation:** Shump & Shump reported adult males at 1.4–1.5 kg versus females at 0.8–0.9 kg. Veterinary references give normal intact hobs around 1–2 kg and intact jills around 0.6–1.0 kg. Seasonal body mass can vary substantially, reportedly approaching 40% in some animals. Neutering also changes typical mass and muscularity, so the canonical specimen is explicitly an intact adult hob rather than an average pet of unknown reproductive status.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Peer-reviewed developmental data: adult males **1.4–1.5 kg**, adult females **0.8–0.9 kg**.
- ADW broader husbandry range: males **0.9–2.7 kg**, females **0.3–1.1 kg**.
- Veterinary text: intact hobs normally **1–2 kg**; neutered males average about **1.2 kg**.
- **Canonical:** **1.5 kg**, representative of a robust adult intact male without selecting a record-sized individual.

### Length and height
- ADW reports male head-body length **38.0–40.6 cm**, female 33.0–35.5 cm, and tail length **7.6–10.0 cm**.
- A representative adult male therefore totals roughly 45.6–50.6 cm including tail.
- **Canonical total length:** **50.0 cm**.
- **Shoulder height:** no sufficiently strong standardized adult-male shoulder-height measurement was verified in this run. Do not preserve a guessed value.

### Speed and locomotion
- No defensible species-specific maximum sprint measurement was verified. The live 36 km/h value is therefore not retained as fact.
- A peer-reviewed locomotion experiment measured domestic ferrets moving at about **0.8 m/s average trial velocity** in both unconstrained overground and simulated subterranean conditions. This is experimental preferred/trial movement, not top speed.
- Ferrets retained broadly similar gait and force mechanics despite tunnel constraints that reduced back height by 40% and hip height by 25%, strong evidence for tunnel-locomotion specialization.

### Lifespan
- ADW: **6–10 years** in captivity.
- MSD Veterinary Manual: typical **6–10 years**.
- Veterinary reference tables also report 5–9 years, average 5–7, depending on population/husbandry.
- **Canonical:** **8.0 years**, the midpoint of the repeatedly reported 6–10-year typical range.

### Dentition and natural weapons
- Ferrets have **34 permanent teeth**, including four prominent canines, and five non-retractile claws per paw.
- Primary weapon is a close-range carnivoran bite, with claws useful mainly for traction, digging, grappling and manipulation rather than as large slashing weapons.
- Controlled prey-capture research found ferrets innately direct bites toward the anterior portion of moving prey. Fur-like targets elicited more biting than hard-surfaced targets.
- **Bite pressure:** no defensible ferret-specific PSI measurement was verified. Do not convert generic force estimates or skull morphology into PSI.

### Defensive structures
- No armor, shell, thick hide or other major passive protection.
- The elongated flexible trunk, low profile and ability to reverse/reposition in confined spaces help avoidance but do not justify high absolute Defense.
- Anal scent glands can release a pungent secretion when frightened or traumatized in intact animals, but many domestic ferrets are descented and this is a deterrent rather than armor.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The bite is the principal damaging tool. Ferrets are small obligate carnivores with enlarged canines and carnassial dentition. Their predatory motor pattern is not random snapping: controlled experiments found an innate tendency to target the anterior end of prey-like moving objects. The claws are non-retractile and useful for purchase, burrowing and holding position but are not comparable to felid talons or large mustelid digging claws as standalone weapons.

### Offensive mechanics and predation
Domestic ferrets retain strong mustelid predatory behavior. Historically they have been used to pursue rabbits and other small quarry through burrows. Experimental work shows prey-catching is strongly released by movement and tactile/fur cues. Prey size matters: objects more than about twice ferret size elicited avoidance in controlled tests, a useful constraint against exaggerating ferocity. Another study described ferret prey capture as largely instinctive behavioral sequences whose time to overpower defensive prey depends on prey size relative to the predator.

### Defense and durability
At only about 1.5 kg, the ferret has little absolute damage tolerance against medium or large roster animals. It lacks armor and has relatively delicate small-mammal dimensions. Its best defense is positional: a narrow flexible body, rapid turning, low center of mass and ability to use confined passages where larger opponents cannot maneuver effectively.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Biomechanical work directly supports subterranean adaptation. Ferrets maintained similar average velocity, gait and many force variables in an experimentally restricted tunnel despite major postural compression. This makes the animal exceptionally effective in narrow spaces. High Agility is therefore based on body control and tunnel maneuverability, not on an unsupported sprint-speed claim.

### Endurance and recovery
Ferrets are active in intense bouts but sleep extensively. Laboratory work found roughly 39% wake time and over 60% sleep, while a 2026 international husbandry survey found most owners reporting 12–20 hours of sleep daily. This argues against treating the species as an elite continuous-endurance runner. Its high metabolism and short digestive transit also favor frequent feeding. Stamina should be moderate rather than wolf/dhole-like.

### Senses
Ferrets have well-studied hearing. Adult-male audiograms have been measured from very low frequencies through at least 48 kHz, and behavioral/neurophysiological work shows useful sound-localization capability. Chemical communication and scent marking are also important. These senses support prey detection and close-range orientation in dim or confined spaces.

### Intelligence, learning and tactics
Domestic ferrets are highly inquisitive, trainable animals that can learn routines and litter use, and auditory research commonly exploits their trainability. Their tactical strength is flexible exploration and close-quarters prey pursuit rather than sophisticated cooperative planning. They can alter activity around human schedules. Intelligence is therefore above many small prey animals but far below corvid/primate roster ceilings.

### Fighting and social behavior
Ferrets wrestle, bite and kick during rough social play, which can superficially resemble aggression. They may fight when unfamiliar individuals are introduced, but they do not justify a high absolute Ferocity score merely because mustelids have a reputation for aggression. Canonical combat behavior is a close-range bite-and-grapple approach with strong exploratory drive.

### Environmental advantages
- Burrows, tubes, dense clutter and other narrow terrain strongly favor the ferret's body plan.
- Low light does not eliminate its useful hearing, tactile and chemical cues.
- Small size makes concealment and entry into refuges easy.

### Environmental disadvantages
- Open terrain exposes its low absolute speed, reach and durability against larger opponents.
- Heat is a husbandry vulnerability; ferrets can suffer heat stress.
- Deep water, large vertical gaps and environments without cover remove much of the tunnel specialist advantage.

### Major weaknesses
- Very low absolute mass and striking force.
- No armor and limited reach.
- Bite requires close contact.
- Long sleep requirement and bursty activity profile do not support elite sustained exertion.
- Many larger predators can incapacitate it with one successful bite, stomp or strike.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Best:** similarly sized soft-bodied prey, small rodents, opponents that must enter narrow passages, slow targets vulnerable to repeated close bites.
- **Mixed:** other small agile carnivores where bite placement and maneuverability matter.
- **Worst:** fox-sized and larger predators, armored animals, large birds of prey in open space, heavy hoofed animals, and any opponent whose mass/reach prevents safe entry to biting range.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Tunnel Predator** — the elongated flexible mustelid body retains effective gait mechanics under severe tunnel restriction, allowing pursuit, turning and attack in spaces that strongly constrain larger animals.
2. **Scent Burst** — intact anal glands can release a pungent secretion under fear or trauma, creating a close-range deterrent/distraction. This is situational and many domestic ferrets are descented, so it is not treated as universal high-grade chemical weaponry.

### Unique traits
1. **Flexible Musteline Frame** — long torso, short limbs and low profile provide exceptional confined-space body control without implying high absolute strength.
2. **Anterior-Bite Instinct** — controlled prey-capture experiments found an innate tendency to aim bites at the front of moving prey-like targets, giving the animal a grounded close-quarters targeting behavior.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The domestic ferret is a domesticated animal with worldwide captive distribution rather than a naturally bounded wild range. It lives principally in human-associated environments. Feral populations exist in some regions, most famously New Zealand, but the canonical ABS animal is the domestic form rather than a feral hybrid population.

### Diet and ecology
Ferrets are obligate carnivores with rapid gastrointestinal transit and high feeding frequency. Historically, people used them for rabbit hunting and rodent control. Their body form and predatory behavior reflect a lineage specialized for pursuing vertebrate prey in burrows and cluttered cover.

### Social structure and behavior
Domestic ferrets can be social with conspecifics and commonly engage in wrestling, chasing and play. They are inquisitive and exploratory. Activity is naturally crepuscular but readily shifts with husbandry and human schedules.

### Life history
Sexual maturity can occur around six months, while full adult status is generally placed around one year. Gestation is about 42 days. Veterinary references report litter sizes varying widely, with averages near eight in some datasets. Kits are altricial, born blind and deaf, and require substantial maternal care.

### Conservation
As a domesticated taxon, the ferret is not meaningfully evaluated as a threatened wild species. This must not be confused with the endangered black-footed ferret, *Mustela nigripes*.

### Adaptations
- Long, narrow torso and short limbs for confined locomotion.
- Non-retractile claws useful for traction and digging.
- Carnassial dentition and prominent canines for a meat diet and prey capture.
- Strong auditory system and chemical/tactile communication.
- Seasonal coat and body-mass changes in intact animals.

### Human interaction
Ferrets have been kept for more than two millennia, historically for hunting and today as pets and laboratory animals. Their respiratory biology and trainability have made them important biomedical and auditory research models.

### Fun facts
- Ferrets can maintain surprisingly similar locomotor mechanics even when a tunnel forces a major reduction in back and hip height.
- Adult males are substantially larger than females; this is real sexual dimorphism, not merely a pet-breed difference.
- Controlled experiments show moving, fur-textured targets are especially effective at eliciting prey-catching bites.
- Ferrets are unusually sleepy mammals: controlled laboratory work found more than 60% of the day spent asleep.
- Their hearing has been studied extensively enough that complete experimental audiograms extend from very low frequencies into ultrasound-range frequencies.
- The familiar name *Mustela putorius furo* remains common in biomedical and agency databases even though ITIS and the Mammal Diversity Database currently recognize *Mustela furo* as the valid species name.

### Concise site-ready summary
A 1.5 kg tunnel specialist, the domestic ferret trades armor and raw power for extreme close-quarters maneuverability, sharp carnivoran teeth, strong prey instincts and useful hearing. It is dangerous to similarly sized prey in confined spaces but badly outscaled by medium and large roster animals.

### Rich narrative profile
The ferret is a good test of ABS's absolute-scale rule. Pound for pound, its elongated mustelid body is an effective predatory machine: it can thread through burrows, maintain functional gait mechanics while crouched, orient to prey with strong sensory systems, and commit to an instinctive anterior bite. None of that makes a 1.5 kg animal physically comparable to a fox, wolf or big cat. Its battle identity should therefore be built around maneuverability and situational terrain control rather than inflated Attack or Defense.

In a tunnel, the matchup changes sharply. A larger opponent may lose turning room while the ferret keeps useful movement mechanics and can exploit small openings. In open ground, that advantage collapses. The ferret has no armor, no long-range weapon, no defensible extreme sprint figure and no evidence for elite endurance. Its teeth remain dangerous to prey near its own scale, but every damaging action requires entering close range.

This produces a deliberately polarized ABS profile: very high Agility, respectable senses/tactics, moderate Special, and low absolute physical combat scores. That shape is more biologically faithful than the old placeholder profile and avoids converting mustelid reputation into fictitious force.

## 11. Confidence and conflicts

- **Taxonomy:** High confidence that the domestic ferret is the intended animal; nomenclature is genuinely split across authorities. Use *Mustela furo* as current preferred species treatment while preserving *M. putorius furo* as the live-schema/agency synonym.
- **Mass:** High confidence. Multiple sources agree males are much larger; 1.5 kg is directly supported by peer-reviewed adult data.
- **Length:** Medium-high. Head-body and tail ranges are credible, but the 50.0 cm total is a representative synthesis rather than one measured canonical specimen.
- **Height:** Low/unresolved, therefore 0.0.
- **Top speed:** Low/unresolved, therefore 0.0. Do not mislabel 0.8 m/s experimental travel velocity as maximum sprint.
- **Bite PSI:** Unresolved, therefore 0.0. No force-to-pressure conversion is fabricated.
- **Lifespan:** High for captive domestic ferrets; husbandry strongly affects realized longevity.
- **Combat ratings:** Editorial but evidence-grounded. Highest confidence is in the profile shape: very high maneuverability, low absolute power/protection, moderate endurance, useful senses/tactics.

## 12. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Fennec Fox (1.5 kg):** Similar canonical mass, but the ferret receives slightly higher Attack (17.0 vs 15.0) for close-quarters mustelid predation and slightly higher maneuverability in confined terrain. Fennec remains stronger in open-desert mobility and environmental Special. Neither should approach medium-canid physical scores.
- **Versus Dingo/Dhole (~18 kg):** Ferret Attack and Defense must remain dramatically lower because the canids have roughly twelve times the mass plus longer reach and substantially greater absolute bite/impact capability. Ferret Agility can exceed them because Agility is maneuverability, not force.
- **Versus Emperor Scorpion (0.03 kg):** Ferret has far more absolute physical power and durability, while the scorpion can retain a higher Special score due to venom. This preserves absolute scaling without making body size automatically control every category.
- **Versus Dragonfly (~0.001 kg):** Dragonfly can remain higher in aerial Agility, but Ferret should dominate Raw Power, Attack, Defense and mammalian tactical flexibility in absolute terms.
- **Inflation check:** No 90+ headline score is assigned. The only near-elite value is Agility 89.0, directly tied to flexible confined-space locomotion rather than an unsupported speed figure.
- **Double-counting check:** Tunnel specialization drives Maneuverability/Agility and one named ability, but does not inflate Raw Power, Defense or Stamina. Hearing supports Senses and contributes modestly to tactics without becoming a separate magical ability.
