# Swordfish (*Xiphias gladius*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Swordfish (broadbill)
- **Scientific name:** *Xiphias gladius* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the broadbill swordfish, the only member of the family Xiphiidae, found worldwide in tropical and temperate oceans. It is not a marlin or sailfish, which belong to a separate billfish family.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy large adult female, **200.0 kg**, about **300 cm** total length including the sword.
- **Sex choice:** females grow larger than males (Wikipedia; NOAA), so the larger sex is used, as in the roster's marlin report.
- **Population variation:** Pacific swordfish reach larger sizes than north-west Atlantic and Mediterranean fish. The fishery average in the North Atlantic is only 50–200 lb (23–91 kg; NOAA), while high-latitude fish tagged in the Tasman Sea weighed 50–350 kg and were "disproportionately large adults" (Tracey et al. 2023). Maximum published weights of 650 kg (FishBase) and an IGFA angling record of 536 kg are exceptional fish, not the baseline.

- No instrumented maximum speed for the species was found.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- NOAA: swordfish **live about 9 years**; females mature at 4–5 years.
- Wikipedia: oldest fish in a recent study were a 16-year-old female and a 12-year-old male; ageing uses fin-ray rings because the otoliths are tiny.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 9.0**, following NOAA; large females can be older.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Sword, teeth and weapons
- A long, flat, sword-like bill (rostrum), broader and flatter than a marlin's round spear. FishBase: the sword is already well developed at 10 mm body length, and adults "use their sword to kill their prey".
- Adults lose all teeth and scales (Wikipedia).
- A 2016 MRI and electron-microscope study found an oil-producing gland near the base of the rostrum connected by capillaries to pores on the head skin; the oil probably forms a water-repellent layer with small denticles that reduces drag (Videler et al. 2016).
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Adults are toothless; no bite measurement exists and the bite is not a weapon.

### Defensive structure
- Scaleless adult skin; protection comes from size, speed and depth rather than armor.

### Other measurements
- Eye and brain heating: a specialised heater tissue warms the eyes and brain **10–15 °C above ambient water** (Fritsches et al. 2005).
- Depth: usually from the surface to about 550 m; recorded down to **2,878 m** (Wikipedia; FishBase).
- Temperature tolerance: about 5–27 °C, the widest among billfish (Wikipedia).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The sword is the weapon. Swordfish slash large prey with it and swallow small prey whole (Wikipedia), and FishBase states that they use the sword to kill prey. The popular image of skewering prey is based on little evidence; slashing to injure is more likely. The sword is also used against sharks. Campos-Sosa et al. (2025) examined ten blue sharks stranded in the western Mediterranean after fatal encounters with swordfish: every shark carried rostral fragments, the brain was the most damaged organ, and some fragments were already surrounded by fibrous tissue, showing earlier non-fatal clashes. Dead or dying shortfin makos have also been found with broken-off swords in their heads (Wikipedia). This is one of the best-documented lethal weapon uses of any fish on the roster.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A streamlined, round-bodied, scaleless fish with a lunate tail built for fast cruising and bursts. The oil-lubricated head and denticle layer probably cut drag. Swordfish also breach powerfully. Evidence for tight turning is limited, and the rigid sword adds length, so maneuverability is rated below the sailfish.

### Endurance
Swordfish are highly migratory. Tagged fish in the Tasman Sea travelled up to **3,275 km** to spawning areas in the Coral Sea and some returned close to their release point (Tracey et al. 2023). They also make daily vertical migrations, descending at dawn to a median daytime depth of about **495 m** and rising near the surface at night. NOAA notes North Atlantic swordfish migrate thousands of miles each year.

### Senses
Vision is the key sense. Warming the retina more than ten times improves temporal resolution, the ability to track fast motion, when the fish hunts in deep, cold water (Fritsches et al. 2005). The heater organ gives the swordfish an edge over cold-blooded prey in dim water.

### Intelligence and tactics
Swordfish are solitary hunters, usually alone or in loose groups spaced up to 10 m apart. They time their vertical migrations to light and moon phase, rising less in bright moonlight and in clear water (Tracey et al. 2023). Tactical behaviour is limited to visual stalking, slashing attacks and depth selection.

### Fighting and aggression
The shark evidence shows the swordfish will drive its sword into large predators, sometimes repeatedly over its life, since some sharks carried healed-over fragments from earlier clashes.

### Predator defense
Fully grown swordfish have few natural predators: killer whales sometimes take them, sperm whales may do so rarely, and shortfin makos attack them at considerable risk. Juveniles are eaten by many predatory fish (Wikipedia; NOAA).

### Environmental strengths and limitations
- Open ocean and deep water: it can dive to depths most opponents cannot follow and still see well thanks to heated eyes.
- Wide temperature tolerance lets it feed in cold waters.
- It cannot leave the water and has no value in shallow, cluttered habitat.

### Weaknesses
- No armor or scales in adults.
- The sword is a single frontal weapon and can snap off inside a target.
- No bite weapon; nothing to fight with at close quarters once the sword is used.
- Loses to large toothed predators such as orcas and big sharks that can close in.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** medium sharks, large fish and squid; any opponent that approaches head-on in open water.
- **Mixed:** shortfin makos and other large sharks, where either animal can deliver a fatal wound.
- **Bad:** killer whales, the largest sharks and any opponent outside open water.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Broadsword Slash** — a long, flat rostrum used to slash and wound prey and to stab large sharks; stranded blue sharks have been found with sword fragments lodged in their brains and eyes.
2. **Heated Deep Vision** — a heater tissue warms the eyes and brain 10–15 °C above the water, giving more than ten times better tracking of fast movement while hunting hundreds of metres down.

### Unique traits
1. **Oil-Slick Head** — a gland at the base of the sword pumps oil to pores on the head, which with tiny denticles probably forms a water-repellent layer that cuts drag.
2. **Sole Broadbill** — the only living member of its family, with no teeth or scales as an adult and a flat sword instead of a marlin's rounded spear.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Worldwide in tropical and temperate parts of the Atlantic, Pacific and Indian Oceans and the Mediterranean, from roughly 45°N to 45°S. Usually from the surface to 550 m, exceptionally to 2,878 m. Preferred temperatures are 18–22 °C, but it tolerates 5–27 °C and moves toward colder regions to feed in summer.

### Diet and ecology
An opportunistic predator feeding from the surface to the bottom. In the north-western Atlantic, 82% of 168 stomachs held squid and 53% held fish such as gadids, mackerels, butterfish, bluefish and sand lance (Wikipedia). Other prey include barracudinas, hake, rockfish, herring, lanternfish and crustaceans. It feeds mostly at night near the surface. Almost 50 parasite species are recorded, including remoras, lampreys and cookiecutter sharks.

### Social structure
Solitary or in very loose aggregations, with individuals up to 10 m apart. Males and females pair only to spawn.

### Reproduction and life history
Spawning is external, in warm surface waters (about 24 °C or more) down to 75 m. Females mature at 4–5 years and produce 1–29 million eggs depending on size (NOAA). Young fish are voracious predators from an early age.

### Conservation
FishBase lists the global IUCN status as Near Threatened (assessed 2021). The North Atlantic stock is not overfished and not subject to overfishing under the 2022 ICCAT assessment (NOAA). Longline fisheries target it across all oceans.

### Special features
- Long, flat, broad sword-like bill.
- Heater tissue warming the eyes and brain.
- Oil gland and pores on the head.
- No teeth or scales in adults; no pelvic fins.

### Human interaction
A major commercial and sport fish, taken by longlines, harpoons and rod and reel. As a top predator it accumulates mercury: in the western equatorial Atlantic, swordfish had the second-highest mercury levels among the sharks and pelagic fish sampled, after the shortfin mako, and the authors set limits on safe monthly meals (de Alencar Goyanna et al. 2025). Sword strikes on sharks may be increasing as fishing reduces shared prey (Wikipedia).

### Genuine fun facts
- Swordfish heat their eyes and brain up to **15 °C** above the surrounding water.
- They have been recorded as deep as **2,878 m**.
- A gland at the base of the sword oils the front of the head.
- Adults have no teeth and no scales.
- One tagged swordfish swam **3,275 km** from Tasmania toward the Coral Sea.
- Stranded blue sharks in the Mediterranean have been found with pieces of swordfish sword in their brains.
- The famous 100 km/h swordfish speed has no reliable measurement behind it.

### Concise site-ready summary
The swordfish is a 200-kilogram ocean hunter armed with a long, flat sword that has killed blue sharks and makos. It heats its eyes and brain to see fast prey in cold water hundreds of metres down and migrates thousands of kilometres, but it has no scales or armor and loses to orcas and the biggest sharks.

### Rich narrative profile
The swordfish is a deep-diving, heat-assisted hunter built around a single weapon. Its flat, broad sword makes up a large share of its three-metre length, and it uses it to slash through squid and fish before swallowing them. The sword is not only for feeding: blue sharks stranded in the Mediterranean have been found with sword fragments in their brains and eyes, some of them healed over from earlier encounters, and dead shortfin makos have turned up with broken swords in their heads.

What sets the swordfish apart is its physiology. A heater tissue keeps its eyes and brain 10 to 15 °C warmer than the surrounding water, which makes its vision many times better at following fast movement in the cold, dark depths where it spends the day. It commonly hunts at around 500 m and has been recorded below 2,800 m, then rises toward the surface at night. A gland at the base of the sword oils the skin of the head, probably helping it slip through the water.

In a battle, the swordfish is dangerous head-on and very hard to follow into the deep, and it has the stamina to migrate thousands of kilometres. Its limits are just as clear: adults have no scales or armor, the sword can snap off, and it has no bite to fall back on. Killer whales and big toothed sharks that manage to close the distance can overpower it, and outside open water it has nothing to offer.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** fishery averages (23–91 kg) and tagged high-latitude adults (50–350 kg) differ widely; 200 kg is a large but not record female.
- **Speed:** no measurement exists; the 100 km/h figure is rejected.
- **Lifespan:** NOAA's 9 years is lower than the 16-year female found in ageing work; fin-ray ageing is difficult.
- **Sword use:** slashing is better supported than spearing for feeding; shark impalements show stabbing does occur in fights.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Marlin (250 kg; A58 D41 Ag78 St90 Sp70):** similar billfish niche. The swordfish's sword has direct necropsy evidence of killing sharks, so Attack (61.0) sits a few points above the marlin, and its heated eyes and extreme depth range lift Special (79.0) above the marlin's 70. The marlin keeps slightly higher Agility and Stamina for its better-documented maneuvering and longer tracked migrations.
- **Versus Sailfish (70 kg; A52 D38 Ag89 Sp82):** the sailfish is far lighter but the most maneuverable billfish; the swordfish is heavier and more lethal but less agile (74.0).
- **Versus Mako Shark (275 kg; A75 D53 Ag85 St88 Sp86):** makos hunt swordfish and sometimes die doing it. The mako's teeth, mass and speed keep it well ahead in Attack and Agility; the swordfish's sword gives it a real chance, not parity.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholders (Speed 98.5, Attack 21.8, 650 kg) are not used. The unsupported 100 km/h speed does not earn near-ceiling Speed, and a sword with documented shark kills clearly deserves far more than 21.8 Attack.
