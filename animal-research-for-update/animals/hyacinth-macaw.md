# Hyacinth Macaw (*Anodorhynchus hyacinthinus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Hyacinth Macaw (also hyacinthine macaw or blue macaw)
- **Scientific name:** *Anodorhynchus hyacinthinus* (Latham, 1790)
- **Exact animal represented:** the living South American macaw *Anodorhynchus hyacinthinus*, the longest parrot in the world, as a wild bird of the Pantanal wetlands and neighbouring palm savannas. Captive and pet birds are the same species and are covered where relevant.
- **Status:** Living; Vulnerable (IUCN); CITES Appendix I.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult, **1.3 kg**, **100.0 cm** total length. Sex is left unspecified because the sexes look alike and no source read in this run gives a consistent size gap between them.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** plumage is identical in both sexes (World Parrot Trust). No reliable sex-specific mass data were located.
- **Population notes:** there are three separate wild populations: the Pantanal of Brazil, eastern Bolivia and north-eastern Paraguay; the cerrado of interior eastern Brazil; and open areas along rivers of the eastern Amazon basin. A 30-year Pantanal nest study found rare dwarf birds whose body mass and total length were about 20% and 22% below normal, and these stay small as adults. They are excluded from the canonical baseline.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Animal Diversity Web (ADW) and Wikipedia: **1.2–1.7 kg**.
- World Parrot Trust (WPT): **1,200–1,450 g**.
- Harrison et al. (2025) bite-force sample: mean body mass **1,253.7 ± 34.5 g** for 7 live birds.
- AnAge: adult weight **1,330 g**.
- **Canonical:** **1.3 kg**, the centre of the converging values; the 1.7 kg upper figure is not used as the representative bird.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Length and wingspan
- ADW: **95–100 cm**, about half of it tail; wingspan **117–127 cm**.
- WPT: **90–100 cm**. Wikipedia: about **1 m** from the top of the head to the tip of the tail; each wing **38.8–42.5 cm**.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 100.0 cm** total length including the tail.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Height
- No standardized standing-height measurement exists for this bird. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- No measured flight speed was located. **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0.** This is an evidence decision, not a claim that the bird is slow.

### Lifespan
- WPT gives longevity of **30–50 years or more**. AnAge records one bird that lived **38.8 years** in captivity; a 54.3-year claim is listed there as unverified. ADW mentions an estimate of around 50 years. No wild survival study was located.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 30.0**, the low end of the WPT range, because the higher figures come from captivity.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Bill and bite force
- The bill is massive, black and hooked (ADW). It cracks palm nuts that Henry Walter Bates described as "difficult to break with a heavy hammer" (quoted on Wikipedia) and opens coconuts and macadamia nuts.
- **Measured in-vivo bite force: 538.86 N** (mean of 7 live birds, force transducer), with **466.81 N** predicted from the jaw-muscle scar on the skull (Harrison et al. 2025, *Journal of Anatomy*). The authors state that macaw bite forces are the highest yet recorded for any bird, and that the hyacinth's almost 540 N is the highest in their study.
- For comparison in the same study, the measured bite of the Scarlet Macaw was **224.8 N** and that of the Green-winged Macaw **350.2 N**.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** A force in newtons cannot be turned into PSI without a defensible contact area. The "300 psi to crack a macadamia" figure repeated on Wikipedia has no traceable measurement and is not used.
- **Additional proposed field:** `bite_force_n = 538.9` (measured, in vivo).

### Feet and defensive structure
- Zygodactyl feet (two toes forward, two back) grip branches and hold nuts; the bill is used as a third foot when climbing (ADW).
- No armor. Protection is feathers, flight and alertness.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The bill is the one real weapon, and it is an exceptional one. Seven live hyacinth macaws produced a mean measured bite of about 539 N, the highest measured bite force of any bird in the published record read for this report, more than twice the measured bite of a Scarlet Macaw in the same study. The bill is built to crush and chisel palm nuts: the bird cracks the shell in one area and then breaks it cleanly in half (ADW). The same tool can badly injure a hand or a small animal. The feet grip and hold, but they are not killing talons, and the hyacinth is not a predator. Its food is almost entirely palm nuts, with some other fruit and apple snails (*Pomacea*) (WPT).

### Offensive mechanics
A bite is delivered at close range from a perch or while clinging. The hyacinth can hold an object with one foot and the tongue while the bill works on it, and captive birds use chewed leaves or wood pieces as wedges so that a hard nut does not slip or rotate while they gnaw into it (Borsari and Ottoni 2005). In a fight this means precise, very strong bites, not repeated slashing or rapid strikes.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It is a strong flier with long wings and a very long tail, usually seen in pairs or small groups flying between palm stands, nest trees and roosts. It climbs with feet and bill. Its size and long tail make it less nimble in tight clutter than smaller parrots, so Agility is scored slightly below the Scarlet Macaw.

### Endurance
Hyacinths move daily between feeding areas and nest trees across open wetland and savanna, but no tracking study of daily distance was located. Stamina is scored as solid, not elite.

### Senses
Good vision and hearing typical of a large parrot. No special sense is documented.

### Intelligence and tactics
Tool use during nut opening has been recorded in both wild and captive birds, and a 2005 study found that juveniles open nuts less well than adults, suggesting practice matters. The authors concluded the birds have an innate tendency to use objects as aids. Hyacinths are described as curious, noisy and fearless (Wikipedia), traits that made them easy to trap.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Pairs defend nest cavities, which are scarce: in the Pantanal about 90% of nests are in manduvi trees (*Sterculia apetala*), only about 5% of those trees have suitable hollows, and only trees older than about 60 years produce cavities large enough (WPT; Guedes et al. 2022). In captivity hyacinths are often called gentle for their size, but a bite from this bill is a serious injury.

### Predator defense
Adults rely on size, vigilance, loud calls and flight. The main documented losses are at the nest: in the Pantanal the toco toucan takes about 53% of the eggs lost to predators, even though it also spreads 83.3% of manduvi seeds, the nest tree the macaw depends on (Pizo et al. 2008). Of 837 Pantanal nestlings measured over 30 years, 395 died, mainly from predators or other natural causes such as parasites (Guedes et al. 2022).

### Environmental strengths
- Open palm savanna, gallery forest and wetland edges with tall nest trees.
- Air space above open ground, where it can see threats early.

### Weaknesses
- About 1.3 kg in absolute mass.
- No armor; hollow-boned flying body.
- Only one real weapon, delivered at close range.
- Depends on one or two palm species and scarce old nest trees.
- Noisy and predictable, which made it vulnerable to trapping and shooting.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small animals that can be bitten once and cannot follow it into the air.
- **Mixed:** other large parrots and corvids, where bite strength and flight skill trade off.
- **Bad:** raptors, cats and any carnivore large enough to grab it; venomous animals that punish a bite.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Record Crusher Bill** — seven live hyacinths produced a mean measured bite of about 539 N, the highest measured bite force recorded for any bird, strong enough to crack palm nuts that are hard to break with a hammer.
2. **Wedge Tool Nutcracker** — the bird holds hard nuts with foot and tongue and may wedge a chewed leaf or wood chip against them so they do not slip, then chisels the shell open along one line.

### Unique traits
1. **Longest Parrot** — about 1 m from head to tail tip, half of it tail, making it the longest parrot species in the world.
2. **Cattle-Dung Forager** — in the Pantanal it picks acuri palm nuts out of cattle dung: the cattle eat the fruit and pass the nuts undigested, and the macaw cracks them. It also nests almost only in old manduvi trees spread by toucans.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The hyacinth macaw lives in central South America in three main areas: the Pantanal of Brazil with adjacent eastern Bolivia and north-eastern Paraguay; the cerrado of interior eastern Brazil; and open areas along the Tocantins, Xingu and Tapajós rivers and Marajó Island in the eastern Amazon basin. It uses palm savanna, forest edge, dry woodland with gallery forest, and swampy stands of palms such as the moriche palm. The key requirement is the presence of its food palms.

### Diet and ecology
It feeds mainly on the nuts of a few palm species in each region. In the Pantanal the main palms are acuri (*Attalea phalerata*) and bocaiuva (*Acrocomia aculeata*); in the Amazon it uses *Maximiliana*, *Orbignya* and *Astrocaryum*; in north-eastern Brazil it uses *Attalea funifera* and *Syagrus coronata* (WPT). It also takes other fruit and apple snails. Its link with the toco toucan is unusual: the toucan spreads most seeds of the manduvi tree the macaw nests in, but is also its main egg predator.

### Social structure
Hyacinths live in pairs, family groups or small flocks of up to about 10 birds, and are noisy and conspicuous. Peak feeding activity is in the morning and late afternoon (WPT).

### Reproduction and life history
Nesting runs from July to December, in tree cavities in the Pantanal and Amazon and in cliff crevices in north-eastern Brazil. About 75% of females lay two eggs, but usually only one chick survives; for every 100 eggs laid, about 25 produce a chick that leaves the nest (Guedes et al. 2022). Chicks fledge at around 110 days and depend on their parents until about six months old.

### Conservation
The hyacinth macaw is listed as **Vulnerable** by the IUCN and is on CITES Appendix I. WPT gives a world population of about 4,700–11,000 mature birds. The first estimate, in 1987, was about 3,000 birds, after heavy trapping: an estimated 10,000 were taken from the wild in the 1980s. Threats are trapping for the cage-bird trade, burning and clearing of habitat, and the scarcity of old nest trees. It remains locally common in the Brazilian Pantanal, where many ranchers now protect it, and the Hyacinth Macaw Project has added artificial nests to make up for lost cavities.

### Special features
- Strongest measured bite force of any bird.
- Longest parrot, with a tail making up half its length.
- Bright yellow bare skin around the eye and at the base of the lower bill against cobalt-blue plumage.
- Dry, smooth tongue with a bone inside that helps hold and work food.
- Tool use while opening nuts.

### Human interaction
Hyacinths are prized in aviculture, and captive numbers were once thought to rival the wild population. Trapping drove the wild decline, and export is now banned under CITES Appendix I. As pets they need very large spaces and extremely tough chew items; WPT recommends access to an enclosure at least 15 m long for part of the year. In the Pantanal, ecotourism and rancher protection now support the species.

### Genuine fun facts
- A hyacinth macaw's measured bite of about 539 N is the strongest yet recorded for any bird.
- It is the longest parrot in the world, and half of its 1 m length is tail.
- In the Pantanal it picks acuri palm nuts out of cattle dung after the cattle have eaten the fruit.
- Captive hyacinths wedge bits of leaf or wood against nuts to stop them slipping while they crack them.
- The toco toucan both plants the macaw's favourite nest tree and eats many of its eggs.
- Only trees older than about 60 years have hollows big enough for hyacinth macaw nests.
- In the 1980s an estimated 10,000 wild birds were taken for the pet trade.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Hyacinth Macaw is the longest parrot in the world, about 1 m long and 1.3 kg, and it has the strongest measured bite of any bird, about 539 N. That bill cracks palm nuts that are hard to open with a hammer. In a real fight, though, it is a light, unarmored bird with one close-range weapon and no talons.

### Rich narrative profile
The hyacinth macaw is a specialist built around one extraordinary tool. Its huge black bill, powered by jaw muscles that produced a mean measured bite of about 539 N in live birds, lets it live almost entirely on palm nuts that few other animals can open. It holds the nut with its foot and tongue, sometimes wedges a chewed leaf against it, and chisels a line across the shell until it splits cleanly. In the Pantanal it even collects acuri nuts from cattle dung, after the cattle have eaten the fruit and passed the nuts.

Its life is tied to a few plants. It eats one or two palm species in each part of its range and nests almost only in old manduvi trees whose hollows take about 60 years to form. The toco toucan spreads most manduvi seeds and also takes about half of the macaw eggs lost to predators. Trapping for the pet trade in the 1970s and 1980s cut the wild population to a few thousand; protection by ranchers, artificial nests and the CITES Appendix I listing have helped it recover in the Pantanal.

In an absolute matchup the hyacinth has the best bite of any bird on the roster, but it sits on a 1.3 kg body with feathers for armor and feet made for holding nuts, not prey. It can deliver a crippling bite to anything small enough to be caught in it and can fly away from ground threats. Against a raptor or a carnivore that seizes it, that single weapon is not enough.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** sources range from 1.2 to 1.7 kg; the canonical 1.3 kg matches the measured sample and AnAge.
- **Bite force:** the 538.9 N figure is a measured in-vivo mean from seven birds; the PSI field stays 0.0 because no contact area exists.
- **Lifespan:** all longevity figures are from captivity; the canonical 30 years is deliberately conservative.
- **Speed:** no measured flight speed; field left at 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Macaw (Scarlet, 1.2 kg; A24.0 D17.0 Ag86.0 St65.0 I86.0 Sp64.0):** similar mass, but the hyacinth's measured bite is more than twice the Scarlet Macaw's measured value in the same study, so Attack (27.0) and Special (68.0) sit above. Its bulk and long tail put Agility just below (83.0).
- **Versus Cockatoo (0.8 kg; A21.0 I94.0 Sp76.0):** stronger bite and more mass give a higher Attack; the cockatoo's field-tested innovation and social learning keep it well above on Intelligence and Special.
- **Versus Kea (0.96 kg; A22.0 I92.0):** the kea uses its bill on live sheep, but the hyacinth's bite is far stronger; the kea's controlled cognition evidence keeps it above on Intelligence.
- **Versus Golden Eagle (5.0 kg; A40.0) and Red-tailed Hawk (1.2 kg; A35.0):** raptors keep clearly higher Attack because they have killing talons and hunt, even though the macaw's bite is stronger.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the record bite raises Attack only modestly because it sits on a 1.3 kg body; Defense is not raised by flight.
