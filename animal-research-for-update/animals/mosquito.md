# Mosquito (*Anopheles gambiae*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Mosquito (African malaria mosquito)
- **Scientific name:** *Anopheles gambiae* Giles, 1902, family Culicidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the African malaria mosquito *Anopheles gambiae*, chosen because it is the best-studied mosquito and, according to UF/IFAS, the most efficient vector of human malaria in the Afrotropical region. Research on other mosquitoes (notably *Aedes aegypti*) is used only where noted.
- **Living/extinct:** Living
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult female, about **2 mg** and **3 mm** body length. Only females bite and take blood, so the female is the combat-relevant form; males feed on plant sugars and never bite.
- **Population notes:** body size varies with larval nutrition; mean wing length of about 2.9 mm has been recorded in wild *An. gambiae*, with laboratory groups ranging roughly 2.4–3.4 mm. Larger females carry more reserves and are more likely to develop eggs from a first blood meal.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Dickerson et al. (PNAS 2012): the *Anopheles* mosquitoes in their raindrop study had a mass of about **2 mg**, while raindrops can weigh up to 50 times as much.
- **Canonical `weight_kg`: 0.000002** (2 mg, unfed female).
- A blood meal roughly matches or exceeds body weight: in seven laboratory *Anopheles* colonies, the ratio of unfed female weight to blood-meal volume was 0.76–0.88, so a meal is about 1.1–1.3 times the unfed weight.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0** (not a meaningful measurement for a mosquito).

### Length
- PNAS 2012: body length about **3 mm** for the *Anopheles* studied. Wing length is the standard size measure: mean about 2.94 mm in a Kenyan coastal sample; a wing length of 3 mm was the size above which females could lay eggs from their first blood meal in a Tanzanian study.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 0.3**.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Speed
- No reliable measured flight speed for *An. gambiae* was located. A tertiary summary gives upwind host-seeking flight of about 0.5–1.5 m/s, without a clear primary source.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- UF/IFAS and CDC material: adults can live up to about a month in captivity but usually survive around **one to two weeks** in the wild. Mark-recapture studies of wild cohorts give estimates from about 3 to 20 days.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 0.03** (about 11 days, adult female in the wild).
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Mouthparts
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** A mosquito does not bite with force; it pierces.
- The proboscis holds a bundle (fascicle) of six stylets: the labrum, hypopharynx, two mandibles and two maxillae, sheathed by the gutter-shaped labium. The sharp labrum tip and saw-toothed maxillae work together so the fascicle slides into skin with a tiny force, averaging about **16.5 µN** in Kong and Wu's measurements, around a thousand times less than an artificial microneedle needs.
- Saliva injected during feeding contains anticoagulants that stop the host's blood from clotting.

### Defensive structure
- A thin but strong exoskeleton. Its main protection is its very low mass: in the PNAS study, falling raindrops lose little momentum when they hit a flying mosquito, so the forces on it are small and it survives the impact.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The only tool is the piercing fascicle, which draws blood rather than causing injury. Its real-world danger comes from what the saliva carries: malaria parasites (*Plasmodium*) that it can transmit to people. That effect takes days to weeks to appear and depends on the mosquito being infected and the host being susceptible, so it has almost no bearing on a fight in progress.

### Offensive mechanics
Host finding is a chain of cues. Studies on the yellow fever mosquito (*Aedes aegypti*) summarised by Caltech show that a mosquito first detects a host's carbon dioxide plume from about 10–50 m away, begins to see the host at about 5–15 m, and senses body heat within about a metre. It then lands, probes and feeds, often unnoticed. *An. gambiae* prefers people and often feeds indoors at night.

### Defensive features and durability
Almost none in absolute terms. Any swat or snap that connects kills it. Its low mass lets it survive being hit by raindrops, and its small size and night-time activity make it hard to catch.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A flying insect with very low inertia. It can hover, dodge and land lightly on skin. It is a weak, slow flier compared with dragonflies, hornets or bees, and it is easily carried by wind.

### Endurance
Females make repeated host-seeking flights and can take a blood meal as heavy as themselves, then fly off. Endurance at the absolute scale is modest.

### Senses
A strong specialist sensory package: carbon dioxide detection, odour detection, vision for approach, heat sensing at close range, and hearing. Males and females hear each other's wingbeat tones, and during swarm mating they shift their flight tones so that the male's second harmonic matches the female's third ("harmonic convergence").

### Intelligence and tactics
Individual behaviour is largely hard-wired, but it integrates several cues in sequence to find and approach a host. Night-time and indoor feeding are consistent behavioural patterns that help it avoid being swatted.

### Sociality and hunting
Not social. Males gather in mating swarms at dusk, and females fly into these swarms to mate. Females seek blood alone.

### Fighting and aggression
None. It avoids conflict and only persists in approaching a host to feed.

### Environmental strengths
- Warm, humid places with sunlit pools of standing water for larvae.
- Night-time and indoor settings where hosts are still.
- Any matchup where the opponent cannot see or react to something 3 mm long.

### Weaknesses
- About 2 mg, with no physical way to harm a large animal.
- Killed by any direct hit.
- Short adult life.
- Disease transmission is delayed and host-specific, not a battle weapon.
- Weak flier in wind.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** large, slow or sleeping animals that cannot detect or reach it; it can feed and leave unharmed.
- **Mixed:** animals with tails, fur or quick reflexes that sometimes swat it.
- **Bad:** insect-eating predators such as dragonflies, bats, birds and fish (for larvae), and any direct fight at all.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Breath-Tracker Homing** — follows a host's carbon dioxide plume from about 10–50 m, switches to vision at about 5–15 m and to body heat within about a metre, landing on a target that has not noticed it.
2. **Six-Needle Siphon** — a fascicle of six stylets slides into skin with a force of about 16.5 µN while saliva with anticoagulants keeps the blood flowing, letting it drink a meal as heavy as its own body.

### Unique traits
1. **Malaria Carrier** — *An. gambiae* is the most efficient malaria vector in Africa; WHO estimated 263 million malaria cases and 597,000 deaths worldwide in 2023, about 95% of the deaths in the WHO African Region.
2. **Raindrop Survivor** — at about 2 mg it is so light that a falling raindrop up to 50 times heavier barely slows on impact, so the mosquito is knocked aside and flies on.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
*An. gambiae* lives across sub-Saharan Africa, especially in humid savanna and farmland near people. Larvae grow in small, sunlit pools of fresh water such as puddles, hoof prints and rice fields. Adults rest and feed in and around houses.

### Diet and ecology
Adults of both sexes drink plant sugars. Females also take blood, preferring humans, to develop their eggs. Larvae filter food from the water surface. Mosquitoes are prey for many animals: dragonflies, bats, birds, spiders and, as larvae, fish and other aquatic predators.

### Social structure
Not social. Males form mating swarms at dusk, and females enter the swarms to mate; males and females tune their wingbeat tones to find each other.

### Reproduction and life history
Eggs are laid on the water surface and hatch into aquatic larvae, then pupae, then flying adults, following the standard *Anopheles* life cycle described by the CDC. A female takes a blood meal, digests it and lays a batch of eggs, then repeats the cycle. Larger females carry more reserves at emergence and develop eggs more readily.

### Conservation
Not Evaluated: no IUCN Red List assessment exists for this species in the sources located. It is abundant and the target of major control efforts because of malaria.

### Special features
- Six-stylet piercing and sucking mouthparts.
- Anticoagulant saliva.
- Carbon dioxide, odour, visual and heat sensing used in sequence to find hosts.
- Hearing tuned to wingbeat tones, used in swarm mating.
- Very low mass that lets it survive raindrop impacts.

### Human interaction
It is among the most important animals in human health because it transmits malaria. WHO reports that malaria control has averted billions of cases since 2000, yet 263 million cases and 597,000 deaths were estimated for 2023. Bed nets, indoor spraying and new genetic approaches all target this species. Its painless piercing mouthparts have also inspired designs for finer medical needles.

### Genuine fun facts
- A raindrop can weigh up to 50 times as much as a mosquito, yet the mosquito survives the hit.
- Only female mosquitoes bite.
- A blood meal can weigh more than the mosquito itself.
- The mosquito's mouthparts are a bundle of six needle-like stylets.
- It inserts them into skin with a force of only about 16.5 micronewtons.
- Males and females "sing" to each other by matching wingbeat harmonics.
- It can detect a person's breath from tens of metres away.

### Concise site-ready summary
The African malaria mosquito weighs about 2 mg and tracks people by their breath, body heat and appearance before slipping six needle-like stylets into the skin. It cannot hurt anything directly, and any swat kills it, but the malaria parasites it carries make it one of the most dangerous animals to humans outside the battle arena.

### Rich narrative profile
*Anopheles gambiae* is a 3 mm insect weighing about 2 mg, and on the absolute battle scale it sits at the very bottom for Size, Attack and Defense. It has no jaws to bite with and no sting. Any direct hit kills it. What it has instead is a precision system for getting blood without being noticed.

A female looking for a meal follows carbon dioxide from a host's breath from tens of metres away, then uses sight and, at close range, body heat to land. Her proboscis holds six stylets; the sharp labrum and saw-edged maxillae slide into skin with micronewton forces, and saliva with anticoagulants keeps blood flowing. She can drink a meal as heavy as herself and fly off, usually at night and often indoors, when people are asleep.

The mosquito's reputation as the deadliest animal comes from what it carries, not what it does in a fight. Malaria parasites transmitted in its saliva caused an estimated 597,000 deaths in 2023, almost all in Africa. That effect appears days later and only in susceptible hosts, so the ratings credit it partly under Special while keeping Attack at the floor. In a direct contest it relies on low inertia, small size and good senses to avoid being hit.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Speed:** no reliable measured flight speed; the tertiary 0.5–1.5 m/s is not used.
- **Host-seeking ranges:** measured on *Aedes aegypti*; *An. gambiae* uses the same kinds of cues, but exact ranges may differ.
- **Insertion force:** Kong and Wu's figure is a general mosquito value, not specific to *An. gambiae*.
- **Mass and length:** from a physics study of *Anopheles* mosquitoes; field size varies with larval nutrition.
- **Disease impact:** malaria deaths are caused by *Plasmodium* parasites carried by several *Anopheles* species, not by *An. gambiae* alone.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Army Ant (A 1.8, D 0.9, Ag 66.0, St 44.0, I 11.0, Sp 24.0):** both sit at the floor for Size and Raw Power. The ant soldier has jaws and a sting that can hurt small animals, so it keeps higher Attack and Defense; the mosquito flies, so Agility is slightly higher (68.0). The mosquito's host-tracking senses and blood-feeding system put its Special (48.0) and Intelligence (15.0) above an individual army ant.
- **Versus Dragonfly (A 8.0, D 5.0, Ag 98.0, St 80.0, I 55.0, Sp 82.0):** dragonflies eat mosquitoes. The dragonfly must stay far above the mosquito in Attack, Agility, Stamina, Intelligence and Special; the mosquito's flight is slower and far less precise.
- **Versus Hornet (A 10.0, Ag 79.0) and Bullet Ant (A 18.0):** stinging insects deliver venom that causes injury or pain; the mosquito causes neither, so its Attack stays far below them.
- **Versus Vampire Bat:** both feed on blood with anticoagulant saliva. The bat is thousands of times heavier with real teeth and must stay far above the mosquito in every physical rating.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "deadliest animal to humans" is a public-health fact about disease transmission over weeks. It is not converted into Attack; it is credited only partly in Special and Abilities, and noted in the traits.
