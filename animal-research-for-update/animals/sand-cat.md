# Sand Cat (*Felis margarita*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Sand cat (also sand dune cat)
- **Scientific name:** *Felis margarita* Loche, 1858
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild sand cat of the sandy and stony deserts of North Africa, the Arabian Peninsula and Central Asia, treating the African/Arabian (*F. m. margarita*) and Asian (*F. m. thinobia*) forms together.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **2.8 kg**, matching the mean of four Pakistani males (2.78 kg) and sitting inside the 2.1–3.4 kg range of Turkmen males (Cole and Wilson 2015).
- **Sex choice:** males are heavier on average. ADW gives males 2.1–3.4 kg and females 1.4–3.1 kg. Central Asian adult males can reach 3.35–3.5 kg (Cole and Wilson, citing Heptner and Sludskii).
- **Population notes:** Central Asian animals grow a very long, thick winter coat with hairs up to 51 mm. Coat colour is pale sandy to light grey. There is no sexual dimorphism in pelage colour.

### Bite and claws
- Kills snakes by stunning them with rapid paw blows to the head, then biting the neck (Cole and Wilson 2015). Kills rodents with a bite and digs rapidly to reach burrowing prey.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-force measurement exists for this species.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. The soles are covered with dense black fur 15–30 mm long that insulates against hot sand and helps it move on loose sand (Cole and Wilson 2015). Otherwise ordinary fur and skin.

### Hearing apparatus
- Ear canal about **twice** the diameter of a domestic cat's and a middle-ear air space about twice the volume; the acoustic input at the eardrum is about five times larger than a domestic cat's (Huang et al. 2002). Wikipedia summarizes its hearing as about 8 decibels more sensitive than a domestic cat's.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The sand cat hunts gerbils, jirds, jerboas, spiny mice, larks, lizards and young hares. It hunts mainly by walking, listening and pausing to look around, rarely by sitting and waiting (Cole and Wilson). It locates prey moving underground with its enlarged ears and digs rapidly to reach it. Saharan nomads describe it as a skilled hunter of horned and sand vipers: it stuns a snake with rapid paw strikes to the head before killing it with a neck bite. When it catches more than it can eat, it buries the rest for later. These are precise, well-timed small-predator weapons carried on a 2.8 kg body.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Short legs placed wide apart give a low, belly-to-the-ground gait at a fast pace with occasional leaps, and it can sprint at 30–40 km/h for short distances. Cole and Wilson describe it as a poor climber and jumper, although sand cats in the Moroccan Sahara have been found resting in brown-necked raven nests about 3.5 m up acacia trees (Bompar et al. 2019). Its reflexes are good enough to strike vipers safely.

### Endurance
Radio-collared sand cats in Israel moved 5–10 km in a single night, averaging 5.4 km; males walked about 5.5 km a night and females 3.2 km (Wikipedia; Cole and Wilson). A Moroccan male travelled 14.1 km in 30 hours, and a Moroccan tracking study (data from 2015–2019, published 2024) measured mean home ranges of about 275–320 km². It can meet its water needs from prey. This is strong sustained travel and heat tolerance.

### Senses
Hearing is its standout sense: enlarged ear canals, middle-ear spaces and auditory bullae raise sensitivity, especially at low frequencies below about 2 kHz (Huang et al. 2002). Long white hairs protect the inner ear from sand. Night vision is typical of cats.

### Intelligence and tactics
It combines walking-and-listening searches, rapid digging and a safe snake-killing technique, and caches surplus food. When a light is shone on it, it crouches low and closes its eyes so that no eyeshine shows, and it often stays motionless until people are within a few metres (Cole and Wilson, citing Sliwa 2013). This is effective solitary behavior, not social coordination.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Home ranges overlap between sexes; an Israeli male used about 16 km² and Moroccan cats used far larger areas.

### Fighting and aggression
ADW calls it a "fearless snake hunter" that attacks venomous snakes. Toward larger animals it relies on stillness, camouflage and burrows.

### Environmental strengths
- Flat or rolling sandy and stony desert with sparse grass and shrubs.
- Extreme heat by day (spent in burrows) and cold desert nights.
- Can live far from water.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny absolute mass and power.
- No armor.
- Weak climber and jumper by felid standards.
- Predated by golden eagles, caracals, wolves, golden jackals, dogs and possibly large snakes; juveniles by eagle-owls and red foxes.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** gerbils, jerboas, small birds, lizards and small venomous snakes.
- **Mixed:** animals of similar mass that bite back or have defensive spines.
- **Bad:** any medium or large carnivore, raptors and armored animals.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Underground Ear** — ear canals about twice as wide as a domestic cat's and a greatly enlarged middle ear let it hear prey moving below the sand and then dig it out. This detection edge matters most against burrowing or hidden opponents.
2. **Viper Stun** — it stuns horned and sand vipers with rapid blows of its paws to the head, then kills with a neck bite. The technique lets a 3 kg cat deal with venomous snakes that would injure a careless predator.

### Unique traits
1. **Fur-Soled Feet** — dense fur 15–30 mm long covers the soles of its feet, insulating the pads against burning sand and spreading its weight on loose sand.
2. **Waterless Desert Cat** — it gets the moisture it needs from its prey and can live in desert far from any water source, drinking only when water happens to be available.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Sandy and stony deserts with sparse grasses and shrubs across the Sahara, the Arabian Peninsula, and parts of Iran, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Afghanistan and Pakistan. It prefers flat or undulating ground with some vegetation and avoids bare, shifting dunes where there is little prey. It shelters in burrows about 1.5 m deep, often ones dug by foxes, porcupines or gerbils. The rare Arabian subspecies has recently been recorded in Iraq (Al Sheikhly and Haba 2017) and in the central Syrian desert near Palmyra (Serra et al. 2007).

### Diet and ecology
Small rodents (gerbils, jirds, jerboas, spiny mice), young hares, small birds such as larks, lizards such as sandfish and spiny-tailed lizards, insects and venomous snakes (Wikipedia; Cole and Wilson). It satisfies its moisture needs from prey.

### Social structure
Solitary; males and females come together only to breed. Home ranges overlap. In Morocco, tracked adults used very large ranges of about 275–320 km² on average.

### Reproduction and life history
Two to three kittens are usually born in spring in the wild; in captivity, births can happen year-round and more than one litter a year is possible. Kittens weigh about 39–80 g at birth, reach three-quarters of adult size in five months and become independent by the end of their first year. In the studbook, 30% of young died before day 30 (Cole and Wilson).

### Conservation
Listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List (2016 assessment) because of its wide range, although it is rarely seen and poorly studied. Threats include habitat degradation, loss of vegetated sand habitat, uncontrolled hunting, poisoned baits set for other predators, domestic and feral dogs, and prey declines during droughts.

### Special features
- Enlarged ear canals, middle-ear spaces and auditory bullae.
- Low-set ears on a broad, flat head.
- Thick fur on the soles of the feet.
- Very long winter coat in Central Asia.
- Ability to go without drinking.

### Human interaction
Harmless to people. It is threatened by habitat change, persecution and poisoned baits; in the early 1970s sand cats caught in Pakistan were exported to zoos worldwide. The Toubou people of the Sahara report sand cats coming into camps at night to drink camel milk (Wikipedia).

### Genuine fun facts
- Its ear canals are about twice as wide as a domestic cat's.
- The soles of its feet are covered with fur up to 3 cm long.
- It can sprint at about 30–40 km/h over short distances.
- It kills venomous vipers by stunning them with rapid paw blows.
- Shine a light on it and it crouches and closes its eyes so no eyeshine gives it away.
- Sand cats in the Moroccan Sahara have been found sleeping in ravens' nests 3.5 m up in acacia trees.
- It can live without drinking, getting its water from prey.

### Concise site-ready summary
A small, broad-headed desert cat with fur-soled feet and huge ear canals, the sand cat hears prey under the sand, kills venomous vipers and goes without drinking. Superbly specialized for deserts, it is still only a 3 kg animal with little absolute power or protection.

### Rich narrative profile
The sand cat is built for places most predators avoid. It spends the hottest hours in a burrow and comes out at night to cover five to ten kilometres across sand and gravel, walking, listening and pausing. Its ear canals and middle ear are roughly twice the size of a domestic cat's, which lets it pick up faint sounds of rodents moving underground; it then digs them out at speed.

Its most striking hunting skill is snake killing. Saharan nomads describe it as a formidable hunter of horned vipers, and field accounts describe it stunning the snake with a flurry of paw blows before a fatal neck bite. It also has the stillness of a true desert specialist: when caught in a light beam it crouches, closes its eyes and waits, relying on its sandy coat to vanish.

In absolute combat terms, none of this changes its size. At about 2.8 kg it is lighter than a house cat and far lighter than the caracals, jackals and wolves that prey on it. It rates low in Attack and Defense, higher in Stamina for its long desert travel, and high in Special and Senses for its specialized hearing.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Climbing:** described as a poor climber and jumper, yet found resting in tree nests 3.5 m up; it can clearly climb when needed, but agility is still scored below arboreal cats.
- **Speed:** the 30–40 km/h sprint comes from field observation, not instrumented measurement; 9.7 m/s is kept at moderate-low confidence.
- **Hearing:** the "8 dB more sensitive" figure is a model-based prediction from ear measurements, not a behavioral hearing test.
- **Home range:** Israeli figures (about 16 km²) and Moroccan figures (about 300 km²) differ enormously, likely reflecting prey density.
- **Bite:** no measurement; 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus House Cat (5 kg; A24 D16 Ag88 St48 Sp60):** the sand cat is lighter, so it sits below in Attack (18.0) and Defense (10.0) and well below in Agility (76.0) because it is a weak climber and jumper. Its long desert travel and hearing lift Stamina (70.0) and Special (75.0) above the house cat.
- **Versus Fennec Fox (1.5 kg; A15 D10 Ag89 St67 Sp79):** another desert hearing specialist. The sand cat is almost twice as heavy with better killing weapons (Attack 18.0 vs 15.0) and similar stamina, while the fennec keeps higher Agility and slightly higher Special.

- **Versus Black-footed Cat (1.9 kg; A17 D9 Ag85):** a similar-sized desert cat; the sand cat is heavier and sits one point higher in Attack and Defense, but lower in Agility.
