# Leopard Gecko (*Eublepharis macularius*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Leopard Gecko (common leopard gecko)
- **Scientific name:** *Eublepharis macularius* (Blyth, 1854), family Eublepharidae (the eyelid geckos)
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild common leopard gecko of the dry rocky grasslands and deserts of Afghanistan, Pakistan, northwest India and western Nepal (records from Iran are disputed). Five subspecies are recognised. Captive colour morphs are bred from wild stock and are not used as the combat form. This is not the Tokay gecko, which the roster already covers as Gecko.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **0.05 kg** (50 g) and **22.0 cm total length**, representing a well-grown wild adult.
- **Sex choice:** males are the larger sex and the fighting sex. Male leopard geckos attack other males with short dashes and vigorous bites that can lacerate skin, while they court females.
- **Population variation:** captive animals are often much heavier than wild ones. A laboratory sample of commercially supplied adults averaged 36.3 g, while pet-care sources quote 60–80 g for adult males.

- Wikipedia: adult males 60–80 g and females 50–70 g, citing a pet-care website (weak source that likely reflects well-fed captive animals).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The only weapon is a small bite. It eats mainly insects and other invertebrates, and will take small vertebrates such as newborn mice, small lizards and even hatchling leopard geckos. Male-male fights involve rapid dashes and bites that can tear skin and sometimes badly injure a rival. None of this matters much against larger animals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Unlike most geckos, it has no adhesive toe pads; its toes end in small claws, so it cannot climb smooth vertical surfaces. It walks with its body raised and swings its heavy tail from side to side; experiments that restricted or removed the tail changed its limb movements and posture. It moves well over rocks and through crevices at its scale.

### Endurance
The thick tail stores fat that the gecko can live on when food is scarce, and it spends cold winters in brumation underground. Activity is otherwise short bursts at dusk and night.

### Senses
Large eyes with movable eyelids suit low-light activity. Males identify the sex of other geckos by chemicals in their skin: they attack males and court females, and even attack females while the females are shedding. Its ear structure is so open that light shines through the head from one ear to the other.

### Intelligence and tactics
It stalks prey slowly, twitching or waving its tail as it closes in, then lunges. It hides by day and uses crevices and burrows well. No cognition experiments were found for this species.

### Sociality and hunting
Field sources describe loose colonies in the wild, while pet-care guides describe it as solitary. Males are territorial toward other males.

### Fighting and aggression
Males threaten by raising the body on straight legs, arching the back and swelling the tongue, then attack with short dashes and vigorous bites. When a predator confronts it, it may squeak or chirp.

### Environmental strengths
- Rocky dry grassland and semi-desert with crevices and burrows.
- Night-time, when it is hidden from daytime predators.
- Long periods without food, thanks to tail fat.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny absolute size; almost any predator can kill it.
- No toe pads for escaping up walls.
- Losing the tail costs much of its stored energy.
- Slows down in cold weather.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** insects, other invertebrates and very small vertebrates.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized lizards and invertebrates with stings or pincers.
- **Bad:** snakes, owls, foxes and essentially every medium or large roster animal.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Decoy Tail Drop** — the tail breaks off at built-in fracture planes with little blood loss, and the dropped tail keeps swinging and even flipping up to 3 cm into the air for up to 30 minutes. A predator that grabs or watches the tail gives the gecko time to escape, and the tail then regrows.
2. **Twilight Ambush** — it hunts at dusk and night, stalking slowly with a twitching tail before lunging at prey, while staying hidden from daytime predators.

### Unique traits
1. **Fat-Storing Tail** — the thick tail holds a fat reserve, about a quarter of body mass in one study, that carries the gecko through lean periods and winter.
2. **Gecko With Eyelids** — unlike most geckos it has movable eyelids and clawed toes instead of sticky pads, the features that give the eyelid geckos their name.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The leopard gecko lives in rocky, dry grassland and semi-desert with sparse vegetation and clay or gravel soils in Afghanistan, Pakistan, northwest India, western Nepal and possibly eastern Iran, where records are disputed. It reportedly avoids pure sand and shelters in crevices, burrows and under loose bark in dry forests. It was first recorded in Nepal only in 2016. Winter temperatures below about 10 °C drive it underground to live on its fat reserves.

### Diet and ecology
It is an opportunistic predator of insects and other invertebrates, and takes small vertebrates when it can, including newborn mice, small lizards and hatchling geckos. It forages at dusk and night, when rocks still hold the day's warmth.

### Social structure
Wild animals are described as living in loose colonies. Males are aggressive toward other males and court females, recognising sex by skin chemicals.

### Reproduction and life history
Females lay clutches of almost always two eggs, several times a season, and can store sperm. Sex depends on incubation temperature: cool (about 26–29 °C) and very warm (34–35 °C) eggs give mostly females, while intermediate temperatures (about 31–33 °C) give males. Maturity is reached at around 18 months in captivity. Hatchlings are banded rather than spotted.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List. Most pet leopard geckos are captive-bred, so collection pressure on wild populations is lower than for many traded reptiles.

### Special features
- Tail autotomy with a thrashing, flipping dropped tail and later regrowth.
- Fat-storing tail.
- Movable eyelids.
- Clawed toes without adhesive pads.
- About 100 teeth, each replaced every 3–4 months.
- Temperature-dependent sex determination.

### Human interaction
The leopard gecko is one of the most popular pet lizards, ranking behind the bearded dragon in a 2021 study of pet-trade search trends, and has been called possibly the first domesticated lizard. Decades of captive breeding have produced many colour and pattern morphs. It is also a laboratory model for studying tail regeneration.

### Genuine fun facts
- A dropped leopard gecko tail can keep moving for up to 30 minutes, with flips up to 3 cm high.
- Its tail can hold about a quarter of its body mass as stored fat.
- It replaces each of its roughly 100 teeth every three to four months.
- Light can shine straight through its head from one ear opening to the other.
- The temperature of the egg decides whether a leopard gecko hatches male or female.
- Males tell males from females by the chemicals in their skin.

### Concise site-ready summary
The leopard gecko is a small, spotted ground gecko from the rocky dry grasslands of South and Central Asia. A 50 g adult male hunts insects at night and fights rival males with fast bites, but it is far too small to hurt most animals. Its real trick is its fat-filled tail: it can drop it as a thrashing decoy that keeps moving for up to 30 minutes, then grow a new one.

### Rich narrative profile
At dusk on a rocky hillside in Pakistan, a leopard gecko slips out of a crevice where it spent the hot day. It moves slowly across the still-warm stones, then spots a cricket. Its thick tail begins to twitch, it creeps closer, and it lunges. This is the whole of its hunting life: small prey, taken at night, close to shelter.

The gecko is built for hiding and escaping rather than fighting. It has no armor, and unlike most geckos it has no sticky toe pads, so it cannot run up a smooth wall to safety. What it has is its tail. The tail stores fat for lean times, and when a predator grabs it, the gecko can shed it. The dropped tail thrashes, swings and even flips into the air for up to half an hour, a distraction that lets the gecko get away. A new tail grows back, shorter and smoother, but losing the old one costs much of its stored energy.

In ABS terms the leopard gecko sits near the floor for Attack and Defense, with moderate Agility and Stamina. Males do fight hard with one another, and they recognise rivals by skin chemicals, but those fights are gecko-scale. Its Special score is high because the decoy tail, backed by regrowth and fat storage, is a genuinely unusual survival mechanism.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** measured laboratory adults (36 g) are much lighter than pet-care figures (60–80 g); the canonical 50 g is a judgment between them.
- **Lifespan:** the 4.9-year average has unclear wild or captive context; left unresolved.
- **Activity:** field sources call it nocturnal, while some husbandry sources call it crepuscular.
- **Speed:** only sub-maximal laboratory speeds were found.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Gecko (Tokay, 0.25 kg; 12.0 / 11.0 / 96.0 / 44.0 / 59.0 / 94.0):** the Tokay is five times heavier with a famously hard bite, so the leopard gecko sits below it in Attack and Defense. The biggest gap is Agility (52.0 versus 96.0) because the leopard gecko lacks adhesive toe pads, and Special (72.0 versus 94.0) because it has tail autotomy but not dry adhesion.
- **Versus Chameleon (45 g; 12.0 / 8.0 / 65.0 / 31.0 / 48.0 / 88.0):** similar mass. The chameleon's ballistic tongue gives it higher Attack and Special, and its branch control gives higher Agility; the leopard gecko has better Stamina (fat reserves) and similar Defense.
- **Versus Bearded Dragon (0.4 kg; Attack 11.0, Intelligence 54.0):** the dragon is eight times heavier and has experimental evidence of imitation, so it scores higher in Attack and Intelligence; the gecko's decoy tail earns it the higher Special.
- **Anti-inflation check:** fierce male fights do not lift Attack above single digits, and tail regrowth is counted in Recovery and Special only, not as in-fight healing.
