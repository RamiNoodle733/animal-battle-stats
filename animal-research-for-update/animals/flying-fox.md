# Flying Fox (*Pteropus vampyrus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Flying Fox (large flying fox; also called greater flying fox, Malayan flying fox or kalong)
- **Scientific name:** *Pteropus vampyrus* (Linnaeus, 1758), family Pteropodidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the large flying fox of Southeast Asia, one of the largest bats in the world. The generic roster label "Flying Fox" is resolved to this species; other *Pteropus* species are not pooled with it.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male, **1.0 kg**, **30.0 cm** head-body length, wingspan about **1.5 m**, forearm about **20 cm**.
- **Sex choice:** males defend small harems and roosting positions in a male hierarchy, and males carry thicker, stiffer fur and glandular neck tufts. Adult females in Peninsular Malaysia averaged about **864 g**, inside the species range of **0.6–1.1 kg**. A sex-specific male mean was not located in this run, so the 1.0 kg male is an upper-middle value below the 1.1 kg top of the range rather than a measured male average.
- **Population notes:** body size varies across the range (mainland Southeast Asia, Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java, Borneo, the Philippines and Lesser Sundas). The canonical value describes a Malaysian-Indonesian adult.

- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 15.0.**

### Bite and teeth
- Sharp canines and cheek teeth used to slice fruit rinds and crush pulp; the long tongue laps nectar.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No species-specific bite measurement was located.

### Other weapons and structures
- Clawed thumbs on the leading edge of each wing and strongly clawed feet for hanging and clambering. These grip branches and can scratch, but are not predatory weapons.
- Wing membranes are thin and easily torn; there is no armor.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The large flying fox is a fruit and nectar eater, not a predator. Its canines are sharp enough to slice through fruit rinds and to give a painful defensive bite to a handler or rival, and its thumb and foot claws can scratch. None of this adds up to meaningful offensive power against animals of real size; it has no hunting behavior to draw on.

### Defense and durability
A 1 kg body with fur and very thin wing membranes. Wing damage can ground it. Its protection comes from height, flight and life in large colonies where many eyes watch for danger.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A strong, direct flier with long, broad wings. It is not an aerobatic hunter: large flying foxes fly steady commuting routes, land by grabbing branches and climb through the canopy using thumb claws and feet. Maneuverability is good in open air and competent in the canopy, but it cannot match the tight turning of small bats or hunting birds.

### Endurance
This is its best physical attribute. Satellite tracking in Peninsular Malaysia recorded foraging trips of up to about **87.5 km in one night**, a 130 km flight in two hours, and adult males moving hundreds of kilometres between roosts in a single year, including across national borders.

### Senses
Flying foxes do not echolocate. They navigate and forage by vision and smell. Their retinas are rod-dominated (about 350,000–800,000 rods per mm²) for sensitivity at night, but also contain two cone types, giving them potential for dichromatic color vision in brighter light. Smell guides them to ripe fruit and flowering trees.

### Intelligence and tactics
Social behavior is its main cognitive feature. Colonies can hold up to about **15,000** bats, males hold roosting positions and small harems in a social hierarchy, and returning bats face hostile displays from roost-mates at dawn. Route knowledge over large foraging areas suggests good spatial memory, but no species-specific cognition experiments were located.

### Hunting behavior
Not a hunter. It forages at night on nectar, pollen, flowers and fruit, including coconut, durian and fig, slicing fruit with its teeth and pressing out the juice and pulp.

### Intraspecific fighting
Males squabble over roosting positions and harems with wing displays, vocal threats and occasional bites and scratches. These disputes are short and rarely injurious.

### Predator defense
Flight, roosting high in tall trees or mangroves, and colony vigilance. Humans are its main threat through hunting.

### Aggression and ferocity
Low toward other species. It bites defensively when handled.

### Environmental strengths
- Open air and forest canopy where it can fly away from ground threats.
- Large landscapes where long commuting distances matter.
- Night-time foraging with good low-light vision.

### Weaknesses
- Almost no damaging weapons.
- Fragile wings; a grounded flying fox is clumsy and exposed.
- Low mass and no armor.
- Heavily dependent on flowering and fruiting trees.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** ground-bound opponents it can simply fly away from; slow animals that cannot reach the canopy.
- **Mixed:** other bats and small arboreal mammals in roost disputes.
- **Bad:** raptors, pythons and arboreal cats that catch it at the roost, and any opponent that can pin it on the ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Night Commuter** — flies up to about 87.5 km in a night and has been tracked covering 130 km in two hours, letting it disengage from almost any ground-bound opponent and reach distant food.
2. **Echo-Free Night Vision** — navigates without echolocation, using rod-rich eyes with two cone types and a keen nose to find fruit and flowers in the dark.

### Unique traits
1. **Giant Fruit Bat** — one of the largest bats in the world, with a wingspan of about 1.5 m and a forearm up to 22 cm.
2. **Rainforest Pollinator** — feeds on nectar and pollen of trees such as durian and carries seeds and pollen across huge distances, supporting forest regeneration and valuable fruit crops.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Found across Southeast Asia, from southern Myanmar, Thailand and Indochina through the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java, Borneo, the Philippines and the Lesser Sunda Islands. It roosts in tall trees in lowland forest, coastal forest and mangroves, and forages over forests, orchards and plantations.

### Diet and ecology
Feeds on nectar, pollen, flowers and fruit, including coconut, durian and fig. It slices fruit rinds with its teeth and uses its long tongue to lap nectar. As a long-distance forager it disperses seeds and pollinates forest trees, including durian, whose fruit is highly valued across the region.

### Social structure
Highly colonial, with roosts of up to about 15,000 individuals. Males hold roosting positions and small harems within a dominance hierarchy, and a male may mate with several females.

### Reproduction and life history
Females typically give birth to a single pup per year. Newborns weigh about **133 g**, roughly 20–30% of the mother's mass, and cling to her for early care. Lifespan averages about 15 years in the wild by summary estimates, with captive records around 21 years.

### Conservation
Assessed as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List (2022). Chronic over-harvesting and loss of primary forest drive declines. A 2009 study estimated about **22,000** bats were hunted legally each year in Malaysia and warned the species could disappear from Peninsular Malaysia within about 6 years if hunting continued at that rate.

### Special features
- Wingspan about 1.5 m and forearm 180–220 mm.
- No echolocation; rod-dominated retinas with two cone types.
- Clawed thumbs and feet for hanging and climbing.
- Long tongue for nectar feeding.
- Glandular neck tufts in males.

### Human interaction
Hunted for food and sport across much of its range and sometimes treated as an orchard pest. It is also a natural reservoir of henipaviruses, including Nipah virus, and is suspected as the reservoir behind the 1998–1999 Nipah outbreak in pigs and people in Malaysia. Its long-distance movements across national borders make regional management important for both conservation and disease monitoring.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its wings span about 1.5 m, wider than many eagles'.
- It does not echolocate and finds food by sight and smell instead.
- One satellite-tagged bat flew 130 km in just two hours.
- It can forage up to about 87.5 km from its roost in a single night.
- A newborn pup weighs about 133 g, up to a third of its mother's weight.
- Colonies can hold up to about 15,000 bats.
- It helps pollinate durian trees.

### Concise site-ready summary
The large flying fox is a 1 kg Southeast Asian fruit bat with a 1.5 m wingspan that can fly more than 80 km in a night. It finds food by sight and smell rather than echolocation, but with only a defensive bite and fragile wings, it survives by flying away, not by fighting.

### Rich narrative profile
The large flying fox is one of the biggest bats alive. A healthy adult weighs about a kilogram, and its wings stretch to about 1.5 m, carried on forearms longer than a human hand. Its fox-like face, large eyes and dark mantle of fur give it its name. Unlike the small insect-eating bats, it does not echolocate. It relies on rod-rich eyes that work well at night and a strong nose to find ripe fruit and flowering trees.

Its real strength is distance. Satellite tracking in Malaysia showed bats foraging up to about 87.5 km from the roost in one night, and one bat covered 130 km in two hours. Over a year, adult males move between roosts hundreds of kilometres apart and across national borders. That range makes it an important pollinator and seed disperser for forest trees such as durian.

In a fight, though, the flying fox has very little. Its sharp canines and clawed thumbs can give a painful defensive bite or scratch, and males squabble over roosting spots and harems, but it has no predatory weapons, no armor and thin wings that are easily damaged. Its winning move against almost any opponent is to take off. Humans are its biggest threat: hunting and forest loss have pushed the species to Endangered, and its role as a natural host of Nipah virus makes it a species of public-health interest too.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Vampire Bat (35 g; A5.5 D6 Ag84 St46 I64 Sp86):** the flying fox is about 30 times heavier, so it edges above in Attack and Defense, but both remain near the floor. The vampire bat keeps higher Agility (ground running and jump takeoff) and much higher Special (infrared sensing and anticoagulant saliva). The flying fox is far ahead in Stamina because of its documented long-distance flights.
- **Versus Flying Squirrel (0.16 kg; A9 D8 Ag96 St61 Sp87):** the squirrel's controlled gliding earns elite Agility and a higher Special; the flying fox's powered long-range flight earns higher Stamina, and its sixfold mass keeps Attack slightly higher.
- **Versus Raven (1.3 kg; A17 D18 St87) and Crow (0.5 kg; A14):** similar mass class; corvid beaks are more versatile weapons and corvid cognition is far better documented, so the flying fox sits just below them in Attack and well below in Intelligence.
- **Versus Condor (10 kg; Ag70 St82 Sp74):** both are long-range fliers, but the condor's thermal soaring and far larger mass keep it higher in Special and all physical categories.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Nipah virus carriage is noted but not scored as Attack, and the large wingspan is not counted as Size or Defense.
