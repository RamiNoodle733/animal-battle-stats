# Ocean Sunfish (*Mola mola*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Ocean sunfish (common mola)
- **Scientific name:** *Mola mola* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** the common ocean sunfish, one of the heaviest bony fishes. The related giant sunfish (*Mola alexandrini*), which holds the bony-fish weight record, and the hoodwinker sunfish (*Mola tecta*) are separate species and are not represented.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult, **200 cm** total length (snout to the rudder-like clavus) and **400 kg**, a large adult within the 247–1,000 kg range reported for mature fish, rather than the 3.3 m, 2,300 kg record.
- **Sex choice:** sex is left unspecified; no reliable size difference between the sexes was found.
- **Population notes:** sunfish grow extremely fast from tiny larvae; an aquarium juvenile at Monterey Bay increased its weight more than 14 times in about a year. Size varies widely among adults, and the mass distribution in stranding and catch records is strongly right-skewed (McClain et al. 2015).

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Mature ocean sunfish weigh about **247–1,000 kg** (Wikipedia, tertiary). FishBase maximum published weight **1.3 t**; ADW gives up to 2,235 kg.
- McClain et al. (2015) record the largest *M. mola* as a **3.3 m, 2,300 kg** fish that washed ashore at Whangarei Heads, New Zealand, in 2006.
- FishBase's Bayesian length-weight relationship (W = 0.03548 × TL^3.06) gives about **390 kg** at 200 cm.
- **Canonical:** **400.0 kg** for a 200 cm adult.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate; mature weights vary fourfold.

### Length
- Mature sunfish average about **1.8 m** long and **2.5 m** from fin tip to fin tip (Wikipedia). FishBase maximum 333 cm TL.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 200.0** total length for a large adult.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
- A sunfish is as tall as it is long when the fins are included (up to 3.2 m tall in the record fish), but standing height does not apply to a fish. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0**, with fin span recorded here as about 250 cm for an average adult.

### Speed
- Watanabe and Sato (2008, PLOS One) tagged three sunfish of 48–153 kg: they swam continuously at **0.4–0.7 m/s**, similar to salmon, marlins and pelagic sharks. One 59 kg fish accelerated for 15 seconds to **2.4 m/s**.
- Other tracking recorded sunfish covering 26 km in a day at a cruising speed of about 3.2 km/h (Wikipedia).
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 2.4**, the highest measured burst, from a young fish.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate for a full-sized adult.

### Lifespan
- Wild lifespan has not been determined. Sunfish may live up to 10 years in captivity (National Geographic); the captive record is 2,993 days, about 8 years, at Kamogawa Sea World. Wild estimates range from 2–23 years for females and 1–16 years for males (Wikipedia).
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 10.0.**
- **Confidence:** Low.

### Bite and dentition
- The teeth are fused into a parrot-like beak that cannot fully close, and there are pharyngeal teeth in the throat. A 2026 study describes the pharyngeal teeth as a retention cage for mechanically challenging prey.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No measurement exists.

### Defensive structure
- Skin rich in reticulated collagen, up to **7.3 cm** thick on the underside, covered with denticles and mucus instead of scales (Wikipedia).
- Beneath the skin is a stiff gelatinous layer, about 90% water held in a collagen and elastin mesh, that works like an exoskeleton and makes the fish neutrally buoyant without a swim bladder (Wikipedia; Davenport et al. 2018; Watanabe and Sato 2008).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The ocean sunfish has almost no offensive capability. Its beak of fused teeth and throat teeth are made for soft or slippery prey such as siphonophores, jellyfish, squid, small fish and crustaceans, and its small mouth cannot fully close. It is not known to attack anything larger than its prey. Its only incidental force is its bulk, which can injure: in 2005 a 1 m sunfish leapt onto a family boat off Wales and slightly injured a child.

### Defense and durability
Defense is its best combat quality. The skin is tough, collagen-rich and up to 7.3 cm thick on the belly, and the gelatinous layer under it acts as a stiff capsule. Wikipedia and ADW note that the sunfish can shift its skin colour from light to dark, especially when attacked. The limits are clear from its predators: sea lions, orcas and sharks all eat adults, and young sunfish are taken by bluefin tuna and mahi-mahi. It cannot outrun or outfight a determined attacker.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The sunfish has no tail fin and no body-bending swimming muscles. It flaps its tall dorsal and anal fins together, side to side, to generate lift like a pair of wings, and steers with the clavus, a rudder-like fringe at the back (Davenport et al. 2018; Watanabe and Sato 2008). The two fins have the same shape, area and muscle mass. This gives steady cruising at 0.4–0.7 m/s and short bursts; sunfish also breach up to about 3 m clear of the water. Turning is modest for such a deep, flat body.

### Endurance
Sunfish swim continuously rather than drifting, cover tens of kilometres a day and make repeated deep dives. Off Japan, tagged sunfish foraged mainly at 100–200 m during the day in water below 12 °C, then returned to the surface to warm up (Nakamura et al. 2015). Long cold stays can disorient and kill them, which limits how long they can work at depth.

### Senses
Specific sensory data were not found. Their feeding on siphonophores in dim water at 100–200 m and their use of cleaning stations show good visual detection of prey and cleaners.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior is simple but purposeful: dive to feed, bask on their side at the surface to rewarm and to let seabirds and cleaner fish remove parasites, and visit kelp or reef cleaning stations. Aquarium sunfish show curiosity toward visitors; one in Japan stopped eating while its aquarium was closed for renovation and recovered after staff put up cardboard cutouts of people (Wikipedia). There is no evidence of combat tactics.

### Hunting behavior
A generalist predator of soft-bodied animals. DNA work shows that sunfish eat small fish, fish larvae, squid, crustaceans and other invertebrates as well as jellyfish and salps, which make up only about 15% of the diet (Sousa et al. 2016; Wikipedia). Off Japan, siphonophores were the prey caught most often (Nakamura et al. 2015). Where jellyfish are abundant, a bioenergetic model estimated each sunfish needs about 71 kg of jellyfish a day (Curr. Biol. 2017).

### Intraspecific fighting and predator defense
No fighting between sunfish is documented. Defense against predators is passive: thick skin, the gelatinous capsule, colour change and diving away.

### Aggression
Docile; poses no threat to divers.

### Environmental strengths
- Temperate and tropical open ocean, from the surface to several hundred metres.
- Situations where an attacker must get through thick skin.
- Areas rich in jellyfish and other soft prey.

### Weaknesses
- No weapon and no attacking behavior.
- Slow, with no tail fin; cannot escape fast predators.
- Fins can be bitten off; sea lions, orcas and sharks prey on adults.
- Struggles in cold water below about 12 °C for long.
- Heavy parasite loads.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** very small predators that cannot bite through its skin.
- **Mixed:** mid-sized predators that can wound fins but struggle with the thick body.
- **Bad:** sea lions, sharks, orcas and any opponent that can simply keep biting.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Gelatinous Armor Capsule** — a stiff layer of watery collagen and elastin under the skin, topped by skin up to 7.3 cm thick, cushions bites and keeps the fish neutrally buoyant at any depth without a swim bladder.
2. **Twin-Fin Flight** — with no tail fin, it beats its tall dorsal and anal fins together to generate lift like wings, cruising steadily and bursting to about 2.4 m/s, and can breach up to about 3 m out of the water.

### Unique traits
1. **Solar Recharger** — after feeding in cold water 100–200 m down, it basks on its side at the surface to warm up, often while seabirds and cleaner fish pick parasites from its skin.
2. **Egg Champion** — a female can produce up to 300 million eggs at a time, more than any other known vertebrate.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Found in temperate and tropical oceans worldwide, including the Atlantic, Pacific, Indian Ocean and Mediterranean. Sunfish are often seen at the surface but spend much of their time hunting at depths below 200 m, mainly in water warmer than 10 °C. Aerial surveys in the north-east Atlantic estimated at least 12,700 sunfish in summer across about 300,000 km² (Breen et al. 2017).

### Diet and ecology
A generalist predator of soft-bodied animals: siphonophores, jellyfish, salps and ctenophores, plus small fish, fish larvae, squid, crustaceans and other invertebrates, and sometimes eelgrass. It feeds from the surface down to deep water and sometimes near the seafloor. More than 40 species of parasites live on and in sunfish.

### Social structure
Solitary, though several may gather at cleaning stations or in food-rich areas.

### Reproduction and life history
Spawning areas are thought to exist in all major oceans, but mating has rarely been observed. Eggs are released into the water and fertilised externally, and a female can release up to 300 million at once. The tiny, spiky larvae look more like pufferfish than adult sunfish and grow very quickly. Wild lifespan is unknown.

### Conservation
Listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List, with a decreasing population. The main threat is bycatch in drift gillnets; plastic bags mistaken for jellyfish are another hazard. Sale of sunfish products is banned in the European Union, but sunfish are eaten as a delicacy in parts of East Asia.

### Special features
- No tail fin; propulsion by tall dorsal and anal fins with identical muscle mass.
- Clavus, a rudder-like fringe in place of a tail.
- Stiff gelatinous subcutaneous layer and no swim bladder.
- Skin up to 7.3 cm thick with denticles and mucus.
- Beak of fused teeth plus pharyngeal teeth.

### Human interaction
Harmless to divers and a prized sight on dive trips. Few aquariums keep them because of their demanding care; Monterey Bay Aquarium and several Japanese aquariums have displayed them. Sunfish occasionally leap onto boats.

### Genuine fun facts
- A female sunfish can release up to 300 million eggs at once, more than any other known vertebrate.
- Sunfish have no tail fin; they swim by flapping two tall fins like wings.
- A sunfish's belly skin can be up to 7.3 cm thick.
- After deep dives into cold water, sunfish lie on their side at the surface to warm up in the sun.
- Seabirds pick parasites off sunfish basking at the surface.
- The heaviest bony fish ever weighed was a 2,744 kg giant sunfish, a different sunfish species, found in the North Atlantic.
- A young sunfish at Monterey Bay Aquarium increased its weight more than 14 times before release.

### Concise site-ready summary
The ocean sunfish is a 400 kg, tail-less giant that swims by flapping two tall fins like wings. Very thick skin over a stiff gelatinous capsule makes it hard to wound, but it has no weapons, swims slowly and never fights, so sea lions, sharks and orcas can prey on it.

### Rich narrative profile
The ocean sunfish looks like a fish that forgot to finish growing a back half. A large adult is about 2 metres long, taller than that from fin tip to fin tip, and weighs around 400 kg, yet it has no tail fin at all. Instead it beats its tall dorsal and anal fins together, side to side, generating lift like a pair of wings and steering with a frilly rudder called the clavus. Tagging shows that it swims constantly at around half a metre per second and can put on short bursts.

Much of its life happens out of sight. Sunfish dive 100–200 metres into cold water to hunt siphonophores, jellyfish, squid and small fish, then return to the surface and lie on their side to warm up, often while seabirds peck parasites from their skin. A stiff gelatinous layer under the skin keeps them neutrally buoyant without a swim bladder, and the skin itself can be over 7 centimetres thick on the belly.

In a battle the sunfish is almost entirely passive. Its small beak is made for soft prey, it has no muscular body to fight with and it cannot flee fast predators. Its thick skin and capsule make it hard for small predators to hurt, but sea lions tear at its fins and sharks and orcas eat adults. It wins by being difficult to damage, not by doing damage.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** mature sunfish range from about 247 to 1,000 kg; 400 kg follows the FishBase length-weight relationship at 2 m.
- **Record weight:** older popular sources call *M. mola* the heaviest bony fish; the record is now held by the giant sunfish *M. alexandrini* (2,744 kg, per the Wikipedia citation of Gomes-Pereira et al. 2022).
- **Speed:** measured on young fish of 48–153 kg; full-sized adults may differ.
- **Lifespan:** wild lifespan is unknown; 10 years is a captive maximum.
- **Tetrodotoxin:** some sources claim sunfish organs contain the toxin, others dispute it; it is not scored.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Manta Ray (1.4 t; A42 D57 Ag72 St89 I76 Sp70):** both are large, docile open-ocean fish that "fly" with fins. The manta is heavier, much more agile and far more intelligent, so it is above in every rating; the gap in Agility (72.0 vs 38.0) reflects the manta's fast banking turns against the sunfish's steady cruising.
- **Versus Whale Shark (15 t; A48 D82 Ag32 St88 I45 Sp72):** both rely on thick skin rather than weapons. The whale shark is nearly 40 times heavier with thicker skin, so Attack, Defense and Stamina are higher; the sunfish's lighter, lift-based swimming gives it slightly higher Agility (38.0 vs 32.0).
- **Versus Leatherback Sea Turtle (400 kg; A35 D60 Ag59 St95):** same mass, but the leatherback has a strong beak, a leathery carapace and extreme migratory endurance, so it stays above in every rating.
- **Versus Pufferfish (1.5 kg; A23 D56 Sp97):** a much smaller fish of the same order (Tetraodontiformes). The sunfish's mass gives it slightly higher Attack (26.0 vs 23.0), but the pufferfish's toxin and inflation keep its Special far higher and its Defense slightly higher.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "heaviest bony fish" fame does not raise Attack; 400 kg of mass without muscles for striking stays at 26.0. Defense stays at 50.0 because thick skin does not stop sea lions, sharks and orcas from preying on adults.
