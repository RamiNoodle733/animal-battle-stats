# Fighting Bull (*Bos taurus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Fighting bull (Spanish fighting bull, toro bravo, toro de lidia)
- **Scientific name:** *Bos taurus* Linnaeus, 1758, Lidia breed (raza de lidia)
- **Exact animal represented:** the Lidia breed of domestic cattle, bred on the Iberian Peninsula and in southern France and Latin America for aggressive, persistent charging behavior. Other cattle breeds are not represented.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (domestic breed).
- **Status:** Living; a recognized native Spanish cattle breed with an official breed standard (Real Decreto 60/2001).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy bull of four to five years at about **520 kg**, the age and weight class fought in first-rank Spanish rings, which require bulls to be at least four years old and at least 460 kg.
- **Sex choice:** bulls are larger than cows in almost every body measurement (a male-to-female ratio of about 1.1 across measurements), with thicker horns. Cows also carry long horns and are tested for aggression before breeding, but the bull is the fighting animal.
- **Population variation:** the breed is split into many small, closely managed bloodlines called encastes, with limited gene flow between them. Size, horn shape and temperament vary between ranches. Mature bulls range from about 400 to 700 kg.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Wikipedia (breed article): mature bulls **400–700 kg**.
- Spanish breed summaries: adult males average around **500 kg**, commonly **450–600 kg**.
- Spanish regulation: minimum **460 kg** for first-rank rings, **435 kg** for second-rank and **410 kg** for third-rank rings.
- **Canonical:** **520.0 kg**, a typical first-rank ring bull above the legal minimum.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Height
- Spanish breed summaries: average withers height about **120–130 cm**, varying between ranches; some individuals to about 140–150 cm.
- A morphometric study of 184 bulls places the Lidia as mid-sized among cattle, with a slightly "downhill" profile (rump about 3 cm lower than withers).
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 125.0 cm** at the withers.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Length
- Spanish tertiary summaries give about **250 cm** body length excluding the tail.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 250.0 cm**, body length without tail.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate; the peer-reviewed morphometric values could not be read in this pass.

### Speed
- Pamplona encierro guides say bulls can reach about **25 km/h** during the street run, a group run on cobblestones over about 850 m that usually lasts two to three minutes. This is not a maximum sprint on open ground.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable maximum measurement).

### Lifespan
- Ring bulls are killed at about four to six years. Bulls pardoned in the ring (indultados) become breeding sires and can live about **20–25 years**; breeding cows are kept until they stop calving.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 20.0 years**, the potential lifespan of a breeding bull, because the four-to-five-year figure reflects slaughter age rather than biology.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Bite and horns
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Cattle do not fight by biting.
- **Horns:** present in both sexes, longer than in most cattle breeds, and required by regulation to be intact and even for a bull to be fought. Horn length and diameter were measured in the morphometric study; specific values were not recovered in this pass.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Protection comes from mass, thick hide and a heavy, muscular neck hump (the morrillo) over the shoulders.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The fighting bull's weapons are its horns, driven by a fast charge and a powerful neck. Medical studies of bullfighting injuries describe the mechanism: the bull lowers its head as it charges, hooks the target with one or both horns, then extends its neck with great force, lifts the victim off the ground and swings its head in a circle so the victim spins on the horn, causing extensive tissue damage. Across Spain, Portugal and southern France, 1,239 people were treated for bull-horn injuries between 2012 and 2019; goring was the most common mechanism and the thigh and groin the most frequent sites, with vascular damage in about a fifth of thigh and groin gorings.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Breeders score mobility along with aggression, and the bull is lighter and more agile than tonne-class wild cattle. It accelerates quickly, turns sharply to follow a moving target and repeats charges. It is less sure-footed in steep terrain than wild mountain bovids.

### Endurance
The fighting bull is built for short, explosive effort. Blood studies of Lidia bulls after 45 minutes of continuous running in roping-bull festivals show sharp rises in glucose, muscle enzymes such as creatine kinase and lactate dehydrogenase, and stress hormones. During intense effort the bull shifts to anaerobic metabolism, accumulating lactic acid and depleting muscle glycogen. Stamina is well below wild bovids that range long distances.

### Senses
The bull reacts strongly to movement, which is what the cape exploits; regulations require fought bulls to have fully functional vision. Smell and hearing are typical of cattle. No breed-specific sensory measurements were found.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior is that of domestic cattle with a much stronger drive to attack. The bull's tactics are simple: face the threat, charge, hook and repeat. It has no herd-coordinated defense of the kind seen in some wild bovids.

### Sociality and hunting
A herbivore bred exclusively free-range in herds on extensive estates. It does not hunt; its aggression is defensive and bred, not predatory.

### Fighting and aggression
Aggression is the breed's defining trait. The breed has been kept reproductively isolated from other cattle and bred for aggressive behavior, and studies of behavior scores show moderate heritability, about **0.20–0.36** for aggressiveness, ferocity and mobility and up to **0.33** for ferocity. Cows are tested for courage (the tienta) before being kept for breeding.

### Environmental strengths
- Open, flat ground where it can build a charge.
- Confined arenas where a target cannot escape.
- Short, intense encounters decided in the first minutes.

### Weaknesses
- Tires quickly in prolonged fights.
- No armor; flanks and hindquarters are exposed.
- Charges moving targets, which a clever opponent can exploit.
- Lighter than wild buffalo, bison and gaur.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** medium predators forced into frontal contact, people and dogs, lighter herbivores with weaker horns.
- **Mixed:** big cats that avoid the horns and attack from the side or behind; other bovids of similar mass.
- **Bad:** heavier buffalo, bison, gaur, rhinoceroses and elephants; opponents that can outlast its short burst.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Hook-and-Lift Goring** — the bull lowers its head to hook the target, then drives its neck upward to lift it off the ground and swings its head in a circle, spinning the victim on the horn and causing deep wounds, most often to the thigh and groin.
2. **Relentless Charge** — the breed is bred to charge moving targets again and again without backing off, with measurable heritability for aggressiveness and ferocity.

### Unique traits
1. **Bred for Bravery** — kept reproductively isolated from other cattle for generations and divided into bloodlines (encastes) chosen for aggressive behavior, with cows tested for courage before breeding.
2. **Ring-Standard Bull** — Spanish rules fix the fighting bull's minimum age (four years), minimum weight by ring rank (460 kg for first-rank rings), intact and even horns and fully functional vision, making it one of the most tightly specified animals on the roster.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Raised on extensive free-range estates mainly in the west and southwest of the Iberian Peninsula, and in southern France and numerous Central and South American countries where bullfighting is organized, including Mexico and Colombia.

### Diet and ecology
A grazing herbivore that feeds on grasses and other pasture plants on its home estate, like other domestic cattle. Its ecology is managed by ranchers rather than shaped by predators.

### Social structure
Kept in free-range herds managed by ranchers. Breeding is controlled: a pardoned seed bull may be mated with some 30 cows, and bloodlines (encastes) are kept separate with little exchange of breeding animals.

### Reproduction and life history
Cows are tested in the tienta and, if they pass, are kept for breeding until they stop calving. A bull pardoned in the ring for exceptional performance can become a seed bull, and his offspring are later tested in the ring themselves. Ring bulls must be at least four years old.

### Conservation
Not applicable as a wild species; it is a **Domesticated** breed. Spain lists the Lidia as a native breed in its official catalogue of livestock breeds, and its genealogical book is kept by the Unión de Criadores de Toros de Lidia, founded in 1905, representing more than 300 ranches in Spain, Portugal and France. The breed's many small, isolated bloodlines are a genetic management concern.

### Special features
- Long horns in both sexes, which must be intact and even for fighting.
- Heavy neck and shoulder muscle hump (morrillo).
- Slightly downhill body profile with high withers.
- Strong, heritable tendency to charge.
- Explosive anaerobic muscle performance.

### Human interaction
Fighting bulls are raised for bullfighting, bull runs and other traditional festivals, which remain controversial. They cause serious injuries: a review of 2012–2019 recorded 1,239 injured patients, with vascular injuries and back gorings the strongest predictors of death. The encierro in Pamplona sees bulls run through city streets at up to about 25 km/h.

### Genuine fun facts
- Spanish law sets minimum weights for bulls by ring category: 460 kg for first-rank rings.
- Both bulls and cows of the breed carry long horns.
- Aggressiveness in the breed is partly heritable, with estimates around 0.2 to 0.36.
- The breed's genealogical book is managed by a breeders' union founded in 1905.
- Bulls in the Pamplona encierro run about 850 m of streets in two to three minutes.
- A bull that performs exceptionally can be pardoned and retired to breed, living 20 years or more.

### Concise site-ready summary
The fighting bull is a half-tonne Iberian cattle breed bred for courage: it charges again and again, hooks with long sharp horns and lifts its target off the ground. It is lighter and tires faster than wild buffalo and bison, but few animals match its willingness to attack.

### Rich narrative profile
The toro de lidia is a domestic animal with the temperament of a wild one. Raised free-range on the open oak pastures of Spain, Portugal and southern France, it has been bred for generations for one quality above all: the drive to charge. Breeders test cows for courage before keeping them, keep bloodlines separate, and measure behaviors such as aggressiveness, ferocity and mobility, which studies show are partly heritable.

The result is a compact, muscular bull of about half a tonne with a massive neck hump and long horns. Its attack is simple and brutal. It lowers its head, hooks with one or both horns, then drives its neck upward and swings its head in a circle, lifting and spinning whatever it has caught. Medical records from bullfighting show just how effective that is, with deep thigh and groin wounds and damaged blood vessels the typical result.

For ABS, the fighting bull's standout number is Ferocity. Its Attack sits just below the heavier bison and Cape Buffalo, and its Defense well below them, because it is lighter and has no horn boss. Its Stamina is limited: blood studies show fast metabolic stress in extended runs. It is a sprinter with horns, dangerous in the first minutes of any encounter and vulnerable to an opponent that can avoid the horns and wait it out.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 400–700 kg across the breed; 520 kg is a typical first-rank ring bull.
- **Length and height:** only tertiary Spanish summaries gave numbers; peer-reviewed morphometric tables were not read in this pass.
- **Speed:** the 25 km/h figure is a street-run value from encierro guides, not a maximum; rejected for the field.
- **Lifespan:** ring bulls die at four to six years for human reasons; 20 years reflects breeding bulls.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Cape Buffalo (750 kg; A76 D70 Ag55 St73 Fe82):** the buffalo is heavier and has a fused horn boss, so it stays above in Attack, Defense and Stamina. The fighting bull gets higher Ferocity (90.0 vs 82.0), because aggression is its bred trait, and higher Agility (61.0 vs 55.0) from lighter mass and bred mobility.
- **Versus Bison (900 kg; A72 D68 Ag64 St74):** the bison's extra mass keeps it narrowly ahead in Attack (72.0 vs 71.0) and well ahead in Defense and Stamina; the bison also keeps slightly higher Agility for documented pivoting.
- **Versus Gaur (1,000 kg; A78 D72 St69):** the gaur is about twice as heavy and stays clearly above in Attack, Defense and Stamina; the bull is more aggressive (Ferocity 90.0 vs 70.0).
- **Versus Water Buffalo (900 kg; A77 D71):** also heavier and better protected; the bull sits below.
- **Versus Moose (550 kg; A69 D50):** a similar-mass comparison; the bull's forward horns and bred aggression put it slightly above in Attack and its thicker neck and hide above in Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** ferocity is scored in Ferocity and feeds Attack once; it is not repeated in Special.
