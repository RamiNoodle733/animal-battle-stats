# Wild Horse (*Equus ferus przewalskii*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Wild Horse; specifically Przewalski's horse, also called the takhi, Mongolian wild horse or Dzungarian horse
- **Scientific name:** *Equus ferus przewalskii* Poliakov, 1881 (also written *Equus przewalskii*)
- **Exact animal represented:** Przewalski's horse, the horse shown in the site's live photo (a dun stallion photographed in the Tennenlohe wild-horse enclosure, Germany). Feral domestic horses such as mustangs and brumbies are escaped *Equus ferus caballus* and are not the animal represented here.
- **Status:** Living. Endangered; the species was lost from the wild in the late 1960s and has since been returned to Mongolia, China and Kazakhstan from zoo stock.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male (harem stallion), **320.0 kg**, **135.0 cm** at the withers and **210.0 cm** head-body length.
- **Sex choice:** stallions are the fighting sex. They herd, drive and defend their family groups, fight rival stallions for harems and confront threats, so a stallion is used. Sexual size difference is modest; the Smithsonian's 250–360 kg species range covers both sexes, and a stallion is placed above the roughly 300 kg species average given by Wikipedia.
- **Population notes:** about 1,900–2,500 animals exist worldwide. The Smithsonian says all come from 14 founders caught between 1910 and 1960; a 2022 review gives 12 wild-caught founders plus up to four domestic horses. Free-ranging groups live in Mongolia (Takhin Tal in Great Gobi B, Hustai National Park, Khomiin Tal), Xinjiang and Gansu in China, the Chernobyl exclusion zone, and semi-wild reserves such as Hortobágy in Hungary. Mongolians historically recognized a lighter steppe type and a darker mountain type.

- No armor. A thick, woolly winter coat, a short erect mane, a heavy neck and a sturdy 320 kg frame. Defense relies on vigilance, flight, family grouping and a dangerous rear arc.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
A Przewalski's stallion fights with its whole body. Rival stallions rear and strike with the forefeet, bite at the neck, head and legs, and wheel to deliver double-barrelled hind kicks. Against predators the same repertoire is used defensively: ADW's horse account records that the dominant stallion may attack a predator by biting and kicking. A 320 kg equid landing a clean hind kick can inflict serious blunt trauma on a medium carnivore, but the horse has no horns, tusks or cutting teeth, and every blow requires the attacker to be inside a narrow reach.

### Defense and durability
There is no armor; protection comes from mass, a thick neck, dense winter coat and a robust skeleton. The horses survive Gobi winters with temperatures far below freezing, but severe snow can still kill most of a local population (a 60% average loss in Great Gobi B in 2009/2010). Wolves rarely kill healthy adults; in Hustai National Park the recorded wolf kills of wild horses were foals (36 foal kill sites analyzed).

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The takhi is a cursorial equid built for open steppe: single-toed hooves, long lower limbs and a gallop suited to escaping over open ground. It is shorter-legged and stockier than light domestic breeds. A 2025 dissection study found forelimb muscle architecture broadly comparable across living wild equids when scaled for size, which supports cautious use of general horse locomotor knowledge. Turning and acceleration are good for a 300 kg ungulate but far below cats, canids and small antelopes.

### Endurance
Groups in Great Gobi B roam non-exclusive home ranges of **152–826 km²**. Energy management is a real strength: heart-rate telemetry on free-roaming takhi showed heart rate falling from a spring peak of **89 beats/min** in early May to **44 beats/min** in December–January, with daily activity dropping from **53%** to **23%** and subcutaneous temperature falling through winter. This seasonal hypometabolism lets the horse ride out lean months, although it is a survival specialization rather than sprint stamina.

### Senses
Equid vision gives wide surveillance around the body, mobile ears track sound, and smell is used socially. No exceptional sensory mechanism is documented for this subspecies.

### Intelligence and tactics
Horses show learned social relationships, long partner bonds and group coordination. Reintroduced takhi groups need roughly a year to settle into the behavior patterns of wild-born groups, and captive-bred groups interact more often but form weaker social networks than experienced wild-born groups. A weakness appeared in the 2009/2010 winter: groups used space very conservatively and did not move beyond their known home ranges, even as snow killed many horses, while sympatric Asiatic wild asses shifted west and lost few.

### Social structure and fighting
Family groups contain one adult stallion, one to three (historically up to more) mares and their young; bachelor stallions and deposed old stallions form bachelor groups. Stallions assemble harems or challenge a harem leader, and some authors expect takhi stallions to be more aggressive than feral horses based on semi-reserve observations. Stallion infanticide has been recorded as the main cause of foal deaths in one population study (83.3% of foal deaths, per Wikipedia's cited source).

### Predator defense
Main modern predator: the wolf, which mostly takes foals. In Hustai, foal kills clustered near forest, at higher altitude and where red deer were dense. In China's Anxi Nature Reserve, 12 horses killed by wolves made up more than 30% of recorded deaths. Stallions herd and guard the family; adults bunch and flee on open ground.

### Environmental strengths
- Open steppe, semi-desert and grassland with room to see threats and run.
- Cold, dry winters, aided by a woolly coat and seasonal energy saving.
- Sparse, coarse forage.

### Weaknesses
- No horns, tusks or cutting weapons; kicks need precise range and angle.
- No armor; the neck, flanks and legs are exposed to grappling predators.
- Heavy snow cover, confinement and dense forest remove its running options.
- Foals are vulnerable, and a stallion defending them must engage at close range.
- Conservative space use in catastrophic winters.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small and medium carnivores that must enter hoof range; lone canids; fragile opponents vulnerable to blunt trauma.
- **Mixed:** wolf-sized pack hunters in a strict one-on-one, where a healthy stallion's kicks and mass usually outweigh the wolf's bite.
- **Bad:** big cats able to seize the neck, large bears, heavily horned bovids, armored animals and any aquatic specialist.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Harem Stallion Onslaught** — a defending stallion rears, strikes with the forefeet, bites at the head and neck and wheels into hind kicks, the same repertoire used in fights for harems. It makes close approach costly for medium predators.
2. **Winter Metabolic Throttle** — in late winter heart rate falls to about half its spring level, activity drops and subcutaneous temperature declines, letting the horse conserve energy through long food shortages.

### Unique traits
1. **Last Truly Wild Horse** — the only horse regarded as never successfully domesticated, lost from the wild in the late 1960s and returned from zoo stock bred from about a dozen founders.
2. **Steppe Dun Build** — a stocky dun body, short erect mane with no forelock, pale mealy muzzle, dark lower legs and a tail with a short-haired dock.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Historically the steppes and semi-deserts of Central Asia, with the last wild animals surviving between the Takhiin Shar Nuruu and Bajtag-Bogdo ranges of the Dzungarian Gobi. Today free-ranging horses live at reintroduction sites in Mongolia (Great Gobi B, Hustai, Khomiin Tal), in Xinjiang and Gansu in China, in Kazakhstan, and in the Chernobyl exclusion zone, with semi-wild herds in Europe such as Hortobágy in Hungary and Döberitzer Heide in Germany.

### Diet and ecology
A grazer of grasses and sedges that also takes leaves of shrubby plants. Its winter diet resembles that of domestic horses; isotope work on the historical population suggests a switch to browsing shrubs in winter under heavy habitat pressure. It shares Gobi ranges with Asiatic wild asses and livestock and is prey for wolves.

### Social structure
Harem groups of one adult stallion, mares and offspring, plus bachelor groups. Young stay until two or three years old; young females join other harems and young males join bachelor groups. Mares often lead group movement, while the stallion herds and defends. Harem sizes in the 15-year Hortobágy dataset stayed stable even as the number of harems grew from three to 23.

### Reproduction and life history
Sexual maturity at about two years, though stallions rarely hold mates before about five. Single foals; foal mortality near 25% in one study, much of it from infanticide by incoming stallions.

### Conservation
Endangered on the IUCN Red List. The species was listed as Extinct in the Wild for over 30 years after the last sighting of a lone stallion in 1969, then downlisted from Critically Endangered to Endangered in 2011 as free-ranging numbers in Mongolia reached about 350. Threats include low genetic diversity, hybridization with domestic horses, disease, wolf predation, habitat degradation and catastrophic winters. Hunting has been banned in Mongolia since 1930.

### Special features
- Seasonal hypometabolism with a halving of winter heart rate.
- Woolly winter coat and erect mane suited to cold, dry steppe.
- Stocky, short-legged equid frame with hard single hooves.
- Stallion fighting repertoire of rearing, biting and kicking.
- Stable, long-term family bonds.

### Human interaction
Captured for zoos around 1900, the horse survived in captivity through a narrow bottleneck that included World War II losses. International studbook management, zoo exchanges and airlifts to Mongolia from 1992 onward restored wild herds. In China reintroduced horses still depend on winter feeding.

### Genuine fun facts
- "Takhi," the Mongolian name, is usually translated as "spirit."
- Every living Przewalski's horse traces to roughly 12–16 founder animals.
- The last confirmed wild individual before reintroduction was a single stallion seen in 1969.
- Free-roaming takhi drop their winter heart rate to about 44 beats per minute, half their spring rate.
- More than 100 Przewalski's horses were estimated to live in the Chernobyl exclusion zone in 2019.
- The 2009/2010 Mongolian winter killed about 60% of the Gobi B population on average, with large differences between east and west.
- Its mane stands upright and it has no forelock, unlike most domestic horses.

### Concise site-ready summary
Przewalski's horse is a stocky, dun steppe horse and the last truly wild horse. A 320 kg stallion defends his family with rearing strikes, bites and powerful hind kicks and survives brutal winters by throttling his own metabolism, but he has no horns or armor and relies on open ground to see and escape danger.

### Rich narrative profile
Przewalski's horse is the steppe version of the horse: shorter-legged and heavier-necked than a racehorse, dun-coated, with a stiff upright mane and a pale muzzle. It came close to vanishing. The wild population faded under hunting, livestock competition and hard winters, and after 1969 the only takhi left were in zoos. Careful studbook breeding from about a dozen founders rebuilt the population, and reintroduced herds now graze the Mongolian Gobi and grasslands again.

In a fight the takhi stallion is the dangerous member of the family. He herds the mares, faces down rivals and meets threats with a full equid arsenal: rearing forefoot strikes, gripping bites to the neck and legs, and hind kicks that can break bone. Wolves, the main modern predator, mostly target foals rather than healthy adults. Yet the horse has no horn, tusk or claw, so every blow needs close range, and a large cat or bear that controls its neck faces no armor at all.

Its most unusual strength is quieter. Heart-rate loggers on free-roaming takhi show the body running at roughly half speed in midwinter, with lower activity and cooler tissues, so the horse spends less energy when food is scarce. That makes the takhi a durable, patient survivor rather than a sprinter or a brawler, and on the absolute scale it sits alongside other mid-sized hoofed animals: tougher and stronger than a donkey, far below the heavy bovids and big predators.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Length:** Wikipedia's ~2.1 m and the Smithsonian's 2.2–2.6 m probably use different conventions; 210 cm head-body is used and the tail is kept separate.
- **Lifespan:** only a captive maximum (36 y) and domestic-horse typical values were found. The 20-year canonical value is an editorial estimate at low confidence.
- **Founder number:** 14 founders (Smithsonian) vs 12 wild-caught plus up to 4 domestic horses (Turghan et al.); both are reported.
- **Speed:** no instrumented data; not taken from domestic breeds.
- **Aggression:** whether takhi stallions are more aggressive than feral horses is based on semi-reserve and zoo observations, not wild comparisons.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Donkey (150 kg; Attack 48.0, Stamina 77.0):** the takhi is twice as heavy with a similar kick-and-bite arsenal, so Attack (54.0), Raw Power and Size sit higher. Stamina is set about equal (78.0): the donkey's rehydration physiology and the takhi's winter energy saving are different strengths of similar weight. The donkey keeps higher Recovery and Special.
- **Versus Pronghorn (51 kg; Agility 94.0, Stamina 96.0):** the pronghorn remains far above in Agility and Stamina because its speed and endurance are exceptional; the takhi is far above in mass, Raw Power and Attack.
- **Versus Bison (900 kg; Attack 72.0, Defense 68.0):** the bison's mass, horns and hump place it well above the takhi in Attack and Defense. The takhi is about the same in Agility (64.0 each), which fits two open-ground grazers of different build.
- **Versus Zebra (300 kg):** a close equid match. Zebra gets slightly higher Attack (56.0) and Ferocity for documented stallion counter-attacks on hyenas and wild dogs; the takhi gets slightly higher Defense (44.0 vs 43.0) for its heavier winter coat and mass.
- **Versus Okapi (275 kg; Attack 53.0, Defense 44.0):** similar mass and kicking defense; the takhi's harem fighting earns a point more Attack, with equal Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholders (Attack 11, Stamina 97.8, Speed 92.5) are not used. A 320 kg horse is not a near-ceiling endurance animal, and it is much more dangerous than an Attack of 11 implies.
