# Lynx

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Roster name:** Lynx
- **Scientific name:** *Lynx lynx* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** Eurasian lynx, not Canada lynx (*L. canadensis*), Iberian lynx (*L. pardinus*), or bobcat (*L. rufus*). The live roster explicitly identifies *Lynx lynx*.
- **Status:** living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy fully mature adult **male, 24.0 kg**, using a central-European/Swiss adult male as the best-measured representative control. A Swiss morphometric study measured adult males at median 22.4 kg (19.0-26.8 kg), while KORA gives adult males 20-26 kg. The 24 kg control is representative of a mature fighting male without using the species-wide 36 kg upper extreme.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** males are larger and more robust. ADW gives a species-wide 18-36 kg range and explicitly notes male-biased size; KORA gives Swiss males 20-26 kg versus females 17-20 kg.
- **Population variation:** Eurasian lynx spans an enormous Eurasian range. Body size, prey base, coat color/spotting, home-range size and movement ecology vary geographically. Northern and eastern animals can be larger than some central/southwestern populations. Scores use a representative mature male, not a record individual.

## 3. Weapons, defenses and combat mechanics

### Weapons and dimensions
The Eurasian lynx is a medium felid with sharp retractile claws, robust forelimbs, carnassial dentition and killing canines. No high-confidence species-specific claw or canine-length measurement suitable for a canonical millimeter field was recovered, so none is invented. Its 24 kg canonical mass places its absolute weapon force well below leopard, jaguar and large canids, but the claw-and-bite package is far more lethal than that of similarly sized herbivores.

### Offensive mechanics
This is a stalking predator. It uses cover and sensory detection to approach closely, then commits to a short rush or pounce, forelimb grapple and killing bite. Peer-reviewed prey-selection work confirms that Eurasian lynx routinely kill roe deer and select prey by size and vulnerability. They avoid the largest, most dangerous adult male red deer, which is important calibration evidence: the lynx can kill ungulates substantially larger than itself, but does not have unlimited prey-size dominance.

### Predation and killing behavior
Radio-collared lynx studies show sustained predation on roe deer. In one Scandinavian study, adult males killed an estimated 4.85 ± 1.30 roe deer per 30 days. A study of 387 documented lynx kills found active prey selection based on species, age, sex and behavior. This supports high tactical hunting competence and dangerous close-range weapon delivery, not large-cat-level absolute force.

### Intraspecific fighting
Adults are largely solitary and territorial, with most prolonged association occurring between mothers and dependent young. Males range more widely and make mating excursions. Direct combat between adults is not the defining weapon context in the way it is for male kangaroos or rutting ungulates, so Ferocity is not inflated simply because the species is territorial.

### Defense and toughness
There is no armor, thick hide, shell or specialized impact protection. Defense comes from compact felid construction, fur, agility, climbing ability, stealth and avoidance. The animal remains vulnerable to penetrating bites, crushing attacks and major size mismatches. Snow-adapted feet improve mobility, not tissue protection.

### Locomotion and agility
Long legs and broad furred paws are major adaptations for cold and snow. ADW and Felidae Conservation Fund describe large, fur-covered paws, and the latter notes interdigital webbing and dense winter hair that improve movement through deep snow. The species can climb and swim, and its stalking lifestyle demands balance, controlled foot placement and explosive short-range repositioning.

Agility is therefore very high, but the score is based on felid maneuverability, pouncing, balance and terrain control rather than an unsupported top-speed claim.

### Endurance and stamina
Eurasian lynx are not long-distance pursuit predators, but they are not low-stamina ambushers either. GPS/radio studies show substantial routine movement. In central Norway, adult males averaged about 5.9 km of linear daily movement in one study; Jura males moved farther between daily locations than females; far-northern animals maintain enormous home ranges. GPS-accelerometer work in the Bohemian Forest found an average **8.9 active hours/day** across six free-ranging lynx. Stamina is strong for patrol and repeated stalking, but below dedicated cursorial endurance specialists such as spotted hyena.

### Senses
Sight and hearing are central to hunting, with olfaction also used. Peer-reviewed activity work notes highly developed sight, smell and hearing and a strong crepuscular activity pattern. ADW specifically emphasizes night vision and hearing. Ear tufts are visually distinctive, but this report does not claim that the tufts themselves measurably amplify hearing without direct evidence.

### Intelligence and tactics
The lynx's strongest cognitive evidence is ecological and tactical rather than tool use. It selects prey by size/risk, stalks under cover, times attacks, learns hunting behavior during development, navigates large territories and revisits kills. This supports strong Tactics and moderate-high Intelligence, but not primate/corvid levels.

### Environmental strengths
- Boreal and temperate forest with stalking cover.
- Snow, where broad furred paws reduce locomotor disadvantage relative to many competitors and prey.
- Broken rocky or wooded terrain supporting ambush, climbing and concealment.
- Twilight/night conditions where stealth and sensory performance are useful.

### Environmental limitations and weaknesses
- Open terrain reduces stalking cover.
- Deep water removes much of its terrestrial agility advantage despite competent swimming.
- No armor and only 24 kg canonical mass create major problems against large predators and heavy ungulates.
- Short-burst ambush design is less suited to prolonged open pursuit.
- Its prey-selection behavior itself shows risk sensitivity around very large dangerous ungulates.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small-to-medium unarmored vertebrates it can ambush, grapple and finish with claws/bite.
- **Mixed:** similarly sized carnivores, where first contact, terrain, reach and grappling control dominate.
- **Bad:** leopard/jaguar-sized cats, large wolves or packs, bears, armored reptiles, and large hoofed mammals whose mass exceeds its control envelope.

## 6. Exactly two special abilities
1. **Snowshoe Stalker** - broad, furred paws with interdigital adaptation spread load and improve movement through snow, preserving stalking and pouncing mobility where many terrestrial opponents lose traction or sink more deeply.
2. **Risk-Selected Ambush** - Eurasian lynx use stalking and prey discrimination rather than blind pursuit. Kill data show selection by prey species, sex, size and behavior, allowing the predator to focus its limited body mass on favorable attack opportunities.

## 7. Exactly two unique traits
1. **Ungulate-Killing Mid-Cat** - despite a representative mass near 24 kg, Eurasian lynx routinely kill roe deer, demonstrating unusually effective prey control for a medium-sized felid without implying big-cat absolute power.
2. **Black-Tipped Bobtail and Ear Tufts** - the short black-tipped tail, facial ruff and prominent ear tufts form the species' characteristic silhouette. These are identification traits; no unsupported hearing boost is assigned to the tufts themselves.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Eurasian lynx occupies an enormous Eurasian range spanning boreal, temperate and montane landscapes. Forest cover is important across much of Europe, but far-northern telemetry demonstrates that the species can also occupy largely treeless alpine tundra. This breadth is why one local home-range or body-size value should not be treated as universal.

### Diet and ecology
It is an obligate carnivore. Roe deer are a major prey base in much of Europe, with chamois, hares and other mammals contributing regionally. The species is an important ungulate predator. Scandinavian work found measurable population-level effects of lynx predation on roe deer.

### Social structure
Adults are predominantly solitary and territorial. Male ranges are generally larger and can overlap those of females. A 2026 Europe-wide telemetry analysis found extra-territorial mating excursions in 33% of males versus 11% of females, with median distances beyond the home range of 6.8 km for males and 2.6 km for females.

### Reproduction and life history
ADW reports mating from February through April, gestation of 67-74 days and typically two to three kittens. Females mature around two years, males around three. Cubs remain dependent on their mother while learning hunting skills and become independent at roughly ten months.

### Conservation
The species is globally treated as Least Concern in current conservation summaries, but status varies sharply by population. European populations include reintroduced and fragmented groups, and local threats include illegal killing, habitat fragmentation, roads and conflicts with livestock/game management.

### Adaptations
- broad furred paws for snow travel
- long legs and explosive pounce mechanics
- retractile claws and felid killing dentition
- crepuscular/nocturnal sensory hunting
- variable cryptic coat pattern
- large spatial ranges and strong navigational/territorial behavior

### Human interaction
Eurasian lynx was extirpated from parts of western/central Europe and has been restored through natural recolonization and reintroduction in multiple regions. Human conflict centers more on livestock/game management and illegal killing than direct danger to people.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is the largest living lynx species.
- Swiss measured adult males had a median physiological body length of 103 cm and median mass of 22.4 kg.
- Its short tail is typically around 20-25 cm and ends in a black tip.
- Free-ranging GPS/accelerometer lynx in the Bohemian Forest averaged 8.9 active hours per day.
- At the northern edge of the range, studied male home ranges averaged more than 2,600 km².
- A Scandinavian predation study estimated adult males killed about 4.85 roe deer per 30 days.
- It can climb and swim despite being primarily terrestrial.
- Coat spotting varies dramatically among individuals and regions.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Eurasian Lynx is the largest lynx, a roughly 24 kg canonical male built for stealth, snow mobility and explosive ambush. Broad furred paws, acute senses, retractile claws and a killing bite let it routinely take roe deer larger than itself, but its lack of armor and modest absolute mass keep it below true big cats in frontal combat power.

### Rich narrative profile
The Eurasian lynx wins through geometry and selection rather than brute force. Its broad paws and long legs move a compact cat through snow and broken forest, while low-light senses and cover let it compress the distance to prey before committing. Once contact occurs, claws establish control and the jaws finish the attack. The result is a predator capable of killing ungulates while weighing only a fraction as much.

That efficiency should not be mistaken for pound-for-pound permission to score it like a leopard. The lynx has no armor, no massive skull, and no 60-100 kg body behind its weapons. Research showing avoidance of the largest dangerous red-deer males is a useful biological boundary on its combat envelope. Its ABS identity is therefore high agility, senses and tactics paired with moderate absolute attack and low physical protection.

### Source conflicts and uncertainty
1. **Body size varies geographically.** The Swiss measured adult-male median is smaller than the broad species maximum. The canonical 24 kg male deliberately represents a mature fighter without selecting a Siberian extreme.
2. **Length definitions vary.** Canonical 103 cm uses the measured physiological body-length definition from Marti et al.; tail is kept separate.
3. **Speed claims conflict and are weakly sourced.** Popular values of 50-80 km/h were not accepted without instrumented species-specific evidence.
4. **Bite-force claims are weak.** No defensible measured PSI value was found, so the field remains unsupported at 0.0.
5. **Lifespan is maximum longevity, not average survival.** ADW reports wild survival to 17 years, while KORA says up to 20.
6. **Snow adaptation is strong, but not invulnerability.** Broad paws improve snow travel; they do not eliminate energetic cost or guarantee superiority over every snow-adapted opponent.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes
- **Bobcat:** Eurasian lynx is larger and more capable against ungulate prey, so its absolute Attack/Raw Power should exceed the bobcat while remaining in the same general felid architecture.
- **Caracal:** the 24 kg lynx is heavier than the 17 kg caracal control and better adapted to snow, but the caracal's aerial leap specialization can remain competitive in certain agility contexts.
- **Leopard:** the 60 kg leopard control remains decisively above Lynx in Attack and Defense. Lynx prey-killing efficiency does not erase a 2.5x mass gap or leopard grappling power.
- **Jaguar:** Jaguar Attack 68.0 and Defense 48.0 remain far above Lynx because the jaguar has roughly four times the canonical mass and a much heavier cranial/forelimb weapon system.
- **Hyena:** Spotted Hyena Attack 61.0 and Stamina 83.0 remain above Lynx. The lynx is more maneuverable and stealth-oriented but lacks hyena mass, bone-processing jaws and pursuit durability.
- **Japanese Macaque:** Lynx Attack must be well above the 11.3 kg macaque's 31.0 because it is a larger obligate predator with dedicated claws and killing dentition, while macaque Intelligence remains much higher.
- **Kangaroo:** the 80 kg red kangaroo has more absolute raw power and much greater mass, but Lynx has more efficient penetrating/grappling weapon geometry. The matchup is dangerous for the lynx because its body-control capacity is limited by the kangaroo's mass.
