# Basking Shark (*Cetorhinus maximus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Basking shark
- **Scientific name:** *Cetorhinus maximus* (Gunnerus, 1765)
- **Exact animal represented:** the single living basking shark species, the second-largest fish alive after the whale shark. It is a filter-feeding mackerel shark (order Lamniformes) and the only living member of the family Cetorhinidae.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult, **790 cm** total length and **4,000 kg**, representing an average adult rather than the 12.27 m Bay of Fundy record.
- **Sex choice:** sex is left unspecified. Maturity is thought to come at 4.6–6 m and 6–13 years (Wikipedia), and a 2022 study of courtship groups measured mature-sized sharks of 5.4–9.5 m (mean 7.3 m) without a clear size split by sex. No documented fighting between the sexes makes one sex the obvious combat form.
- **Population notes:** historic fisheries took more than 100,000 mature animals in the north-east Atlantic alone between 1946 and 1997 (Sims 2008), and a Canadian government eradication programme killed basking sharks off British Columbia from 1945 to 1970, so very large individuals are now rare.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Average adult about 7.9 m and about **4.65 t** (Wikipedia, tertiary).
- Florida Museum: a **4-ton (3,629 kg), 8.2 m** basking shark yields about 1 ton of meat.
- Sims (2008) review: reaches up to 12 m and weighs **up to 4 tonnes**.
- Johnston et al. (2018) estimated an **8 m** breaching shark at about **2,700 kg**.
- A 6.1 m shark caught in Bass Strait in 2015 weighed about 3,500 kg (Wikipedia).
- **Canonical:** **4,000.0 kg** for a 7.9 m adult, in the middle of these figures.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate; published masses for 8 m animals range from about 2.7 to 4.65 t.

### Length
- Regularly 7–8.5 m, with some reaching 9–11 m; average adult about 7.9 m (Wikipedia). A growth and longevity study suggested individuals over about 10 m are unlikely.
- Largest credited: 12.27 m, trapped in a herring net in the Bay of Fundy in 1851 (Wikipedia).
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 790.0** total length.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
- Not meaningful for a fish. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- Feeding speed about **0.85 m/s** (about 3 km/h), observed for a 5 m shark filtering plankton (Wikipedia, citing Sims).
- Breaching: video and a data logger showed basking sharks rising at about **5 m/s** vertically during breaches, the same as great white sharks; one 8 m shark accelerated at 0.4 m/s² to reach 5 m/s (Johnston et al. 2018).
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 5.0**, the measured breach velocity.
- **Confidence:** Moderate; the burst requires a long run-up from depth.

### Lifespan
- Exact lifespan unknown; experts estimate about **50 years**, with a generation time of 22–34 years (Wikipedia).
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 50.0.**
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate (estimate, not validated ageing).

### Bite and dentition
- Teeth are tiny (5–6 mm), conical and hooked, often about 100 per row, and play no role in feeding (Wikipedia). The mouth can open up to about 1 m wide.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** It does not bite prey and no measurement exists.

### Defensive structure
- Highly textured skin covered in placoid scales and a mucus layer (Wikipedia). The Florida Museum warns that its large dermal denticles have injured divers and scientists. Lampreys attach to basking sharks but are thought unlikely to cut through the thick skin.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The basking shark has no feeding weapon. Its tiny teeth are unused, it has no spines or venom, and it never attacks other animals. What it does have is **mass and hidden power**. A 4-tonne body is dangerous to anything it collides with, and breaching data show that this apparently lazy fish can generate the same vertical speed as a great white shark: about 5 m/s, with a mass-specific power output of about 5.5 W/kg (Johnston et al. 2018). Its rough skin also scrapes anything that rubs against it.

### Defense and durability
Defense rests on size and skin. The skin is thick and rough with large denticles and mucus, enough that lampreys are thought unable to cut through it and that divers have been injured by brushing against it. A 7.9 m, 4-tonne body has great tissue depth. Its limits are shown by predators: killer whales have been observed feeding on basking sharks off California and New Zealand, and white sharks scavenge their carcasses.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Usually a slow cruiser, feeding at about 0.85 m/s with the mouth wide open and not bothering to move away from boats. Yet it can breach entirely out of the water, starting from about 20 m depth and sometimes several times in quick succession (Rudd et al. 2021). A four-animal, year-long logger study recorded 577 breaches, an average of 144 per shark per year, in all seasons and habitats (Biol Lett 2026). Turning is wide for an 8 m animal.

### Endurance
Endurance is a major strength. Seventy satellite-tagged sharks from Scotland and the Isle of Man moved a median minimum straight-line distance of 3,633 km (Doherty et al. 2017). Western Atlantic sharks crossed the equator to South America, spending weeks to months at mesopelagic depths (Skomal et al. 2009). They are active year-round rather than hibernating, and dive to at least 900 m in winter.

### Senses
Standard shark senses with relatively small eyes. Basking sharks are selective, feeding in zooplankton patches 75% denser than surrounding water and preferring patches dominated by calanoid copepods, so they detect and track prey concentrations well.

### Intelligence and tactics
The basking shark has the smallest brain for its body weight of any shark (Wikipedia, citing Kruska 1988). It follows ocean fronts and seasonal plankton, and courtship groups swim in organised rotating circles called tori. There is no evidence of combat tactics.

### Hunting behavior
A passive ram filter feeder: it swims forward with the mouth open, straining copepods, small fish and invertebrates on bristle-like gill rakers. A 5 m shark has been calculated to filter up to about 450 tonnes of water an hour. It sheds and regrows its gill rakers continuously.

### Intraspecific fighting and predator defense
No fighting documented. Courting groups circle in tori 17–39 m across with fin-to-fin and fin-to-body contact, rolling and breaching (J Fish Biol 2022). Predator defense is passive: size, skin and diving.

### Aggression
Not aggressive and harmless to people (Wikipedia, Florida Museum). It tolerates divers and boats.

### Environmental strengths
- Cool temperate seas and open ocean from the surface to about 900 m.
- Long-distance travel across and between oceans.
- Any contest decided by outlasting an attacker.

### Weaknesses
- No weapon and no attacking behavior.
- Slow routine speed and wide turning.
- Very small brain for its size.
- Docile; does not flee boats and was easily harpooned.
- Vulnerable to coordinated predators such as orcas.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small and medium predators that cannot get through its skin or mass.
- **Mixed:** single large predatory sharks, which can wound but struggle to kill a full adult.
- **Bad:** orca pods and any opponent that simply needs the basking shark not to fight back.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Latent Breach Power** — despite feeding at under 1 m/s, a basking shark can rush up from about 20 m and leave the water at about 5 m/s, matching a great white shark. Four tonnes arriving at that speed is a real hazard to anything in the way.
2. **Transequatorial Deep Run** — tagged basking sharks have crossed from North America to South America, spending weeks to months hundreds of metres down, and Scottish-tagged sharks travelled a median of more than 3,600 km. It can leave a fight for waters few opponents would follow.

### Unique traits
1. **Cavernous Filter Mouth** — a mouth up to about 1 m wide and gill slits that nearly encircle the head, with bristle-like gill rakers that are constantly shed and replaced.
2. **Abrasive Giant** — skin so rough with large denticles that it has injured divers who brushed against it.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Basking sharks live in boreal to warm-temperate waters around the world, on continental shelves and in the open ocean, and occasionally enter brackish water. They prefer water of about 8–14.5 °C, are often seen at the surface in summer at hotspots such as western Scotland, the Isle of Man and the continental shelf off the north-east United States and Canada, and move deeper, to about 900 m, in winter. Some cross the tropics at depth.

### Diet and ecology
A filter feeder on zooplankton, especially calanoid copepods, along with fish eggs, larvae and small invertebrates. It feeds selectively in the densest plankton patches, often along ocean fronts, and continues foraging during long migrations, including in the deep scattering layers of the mesopelagic.

### Social structure
Usually solitary but gathers in summer where plankton is dense. It forms small sex-segregated groups, and courtship tori of several sharks circling together have been documented in the eastern North Atlantic.

### Reproduction and life history
Ovoviviparous; embryos may use their tiny teeth to feed on unfertilised eggs in the womb. Only the right ovary appears to work in females. Maturity is thought to come at 6–13 years and 4.6–6 m, breeding every two to four years, with a long gestation. Lifespan is estimated at about 50 years.

### Conservation
Listed as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List (2019, amended 2021) and on CITES Appendix II. Centuries of harpoon and net fisheries for its liver oil, fins and meat collapsed several populations. It is fully protected in the United Kingdom and in the Atlantic and Gulf of Mexico waters of the United States.

### Special features
- Mouth up to about 1 m wide and gill slits almost encircling the head.
- Gill rakers shed and regrown continuously.
- Tiny, unused teeth about 5–6 mm long.
- Rough skin with large denticles and a mucus layer.
- Able to breach at the same vertical speed as a great white shark.

### Human interaction
A popular ecotourism species at summer hotspots such as western Scotland. Its slow speed and habit of not avoiding boats made it easy to harpoon and still expose it to boat strikes. Decomposing basking shark carcasses have been mistaken for sea monsters, including the "Stronsay Beast" of the early 1800s and the Zuiyo-maru carcass hauled up in 1977.

### Genuine fun facts
- A basking shark can burst out of the water at about 5 m/s, as fast as a breaching great white.
- One logger study counted 577 breaches by four sharks in a year.
- A 5 m basking shark can filter about 450 tonnes of seawater an hour.
- It has the smallest brain for its body weight of any shark.
- Rotting basking sharks have been mistaken for sea serpents and plesiosaurs.
- Some basking sharks cross the equator, travelling from New England to South America.
- Its skin denticles are rough enough to injure divers.

### Concise site-ready summary
The basking shark is the second-largest fish alive, a 7.9-metre, 4-tonne filter feeder that cruises open-mouthed through plankton. It has no weapons and never fights, but its thick, rough skin and huge size protect it, and it can burst out of the sea as fast as a great white shark.

### Rich narrative profile
The basking shark spends summer days at the surface with its mouth stretched open like a giant net, straining tiny copepods from the water. An average adult is about 7.9 metres long and weighs around four tonnes, yet it feeds at a slow walking pace and barely reacts to passing boats. That calm made it an easy target for centuries of harpoon fisheries hunting its oily liver.

Its lazy image hides surprising power. Loggers attached to basking sharks show them rushing up from around 20 metres and leaping clear of the sea at about 5 m/s, the same vertical speed as a great white shark, and doing so hundreds of times a year. Satellite tags show them crossing oceans and even the equator, diving hundreds of metres into the twilight zone and staying there for weeks.

In a battle, the basking shark is a pure endurer. It has no bite worth the name and no inclination to fight, but its thick, abrasive skin and massive body make it a very hard target for anything smaller than a killer whale. Orcas, working together, are the one predator known to take them, and against such opponents its only real options are its bulk and a dive into deep water.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** published figures for 8 m adults range from about 2.7 t (a modelled estimate) to 4.65 t; 4,000 kg is a middle value.
- **Filter rate:** Wikipedia's 450 t per hour for a 5 m shark and the Florida Museum's 2,000 tons per hour (probably for a larger shark) differ widely; the lower, sourced calculation is used in the text.
- **Lifespan:** 50 years is an expert estimate, not a validated age.
- **Speed:** the 5 m/s breach velocity is a vertical burst after a long acceleration, not a routine or horizontal speed.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Whale Shark (15 t; A48 D82 Ag32 St88 I45 Sp72):** the whale shark is nearly four times heavier with skin around 10 cm thick and armored eyes, so it stays above in Attack, Defense and Special. The basking shark's measured breaching power puts its Agility higher (40.0 vs 32.0). Stamina is close (86.0 vs 88.0). Intelligence is lower (37.0 vs 45.0) because the basking shark has the smallest relative brain of any shark.
- **Versus Manta Ray (1.4 t; A42 D57 Ag72 St89 I76 Sp70):** another docile filter feeder. The basking shark is about three times heavier and better protected, so Attack (44.0 vs 42.0) and Defense (70.0 vs 57.0) are higher, while the manta's agile "flight" and large brain keep it far ahead in Agility and Intelligence.
- **Versus Great White Shark (900 kg; A88 D68 Ag73 St91 I70 Sp87):** both breach at about 5 m/s, but the white shark is a fully armed predator and stays far above in Attack, Agility, Intelligence and Special. The basking shark's four-tonne body gives it slightly higher Defense (70.0 vs 68.0).
- **Versus Orca (5 t; A94 D78 Ag84 I99):** orcas prey on basking sharks; the basking shark is outclassed in every headline rating.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Size 89.0 and Raw Power 78.0 are high, but Attack stays at 44.0 because body mass is not a weapon and the animal never attacks. Speed 55.0 reflects a measured burst that needs a long run-up; routine speed is under 1 m/s.
