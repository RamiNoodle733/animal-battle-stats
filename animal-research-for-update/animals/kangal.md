# Kangal (*Canis familiaris*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Kangal (Kangal Shepherd Dog, Kangal Çoban Köpeği)
- **Scientific name:** *Canis familiaris* Linnaeus, 1758 (domestic dog; Kangal Shepherd Dog breed, FCI standard No. 331)
- **Exact animal represented:** the Turkish Kangal Shepherd Dog as a working livestock guardian from the Sivas-Kangal region of central Anatolia, not the broader "Anatolian Shepherd" show/pet population and not a crossbred guardian dog.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (domestic breed).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male working Kangal, **50.0 kg**, **74.0 cm** at the withers, in working condition rather than show or obese pet condition.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger and heavier than females in every breed standard and field measurement located. The UKC standard gives males 29–33 in (74–84 cm) and 110–145 lb (50–66 kg), with females smaller. Field measurements of village Kangals in Turkey are lighter than the UKC figures (male means of roughly 43–48 kg).
- **Population notes:** Turkish village working dogs measured in field studies are leaner and lighter than many US-registered dogs. The FCI recognized the Kangal as a distinct breed in 2018; earlier the same standard number was used for the "Anatolian Shepherd Dog", so older literature mixes the two names. Domestic dogs were bred from wild wolf stock; the Kangal is a landrace-derived guardian breed selected by Turkish shepherds for flock protection against wolves.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- UKC standard: males **110–145 lb (49.9–65.8 kg)** in good condition; larger dogs are not penalized if balanced.
- Wikipedia breed summary (tertiary): males **48–60 kg**.
- Turkish field measurements: village males in Kırıkkale province averaged **43.4 kg**; another Yılmaz study cited in the same literature reported **47.6 kg** for adult males.
- **Canonical:** **50.0 kg**. This sits just above the Turkish field means and at the lower edge of the UKC range, representing a fit working male rather than a heavy show dog.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high; there is a real gap between Turkish village dogs and US-registered dogs.

### Height
- UKC: males **29–33 in (73.7–83.8 cm)** at the withers.
- Wikipedia (tertiary): males **70–80 cm**.
- Kırıkkale village males: mean withers height **71.7 cm**.
- **Canonical:** **74.0 cm**.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Length
- Kırıkkale village males: mean body length (point of shoulder to buttock, livestock convention) **71.1 cm**, nearly equal to withers height (71.7 cm); head length **24.8 cm**; chest/body circumference **86.1 cm**.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 74.0 cm**, a derived estimate of trunk body length for the 74 cm canonical male using the near-square proportion measured in Turkish dogs. This is **not** head-body length (nose to tail base), which would be substantially longer; the breed literature does not report that measurement.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate; convention mismatch with wild canid head-body lengths is flagged in section 11.

### Speed
- No instrumented top-speed measurement for the Kangal was located.
- A GPS study of Kangals guarding 10 sheep flocks in Sivas recorded movement speeds of dogs, sheep and shepherd, finding lower dog speeds at night, but it reports guarding movement, not sprint maxima.
- Popular breed-facts websites repeat claims such as "more than 16 km per day" and "30 km/h" or higher sprint speeds; none was traced to a measurement.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (unresolved; no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- Breed-information sites commonly give **12–15 years** for Kangals (tertiary, companion-dog context).
- Working village dogs face injury, disease and predator risk, so the typical working life is probably shorter, but no field survival study was located.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 12.0**.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Bite force
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** The viral "743 PSI Kangal" figure is **not a peer-reviewed measurement**. Its origin cannot be traced to any published study, transducer protocol or contact area, and even popular pet sites now label it unverified.
- What real dog data exist are **forces, not pressures**: Lindner et al. (1995) measured **13–1,394 N** (mean **256 N**) in 22 conscious pet dogs of 7–55 kg; Ellis et al. (2008) measured anesthetized dogs under stimulation and reported a pooled canine-to-molar range of roughly **147–3,417 N**. Neither study measured a Kangal, and neither value can be converted to PSI without a tooth contact area.
- **Weapon dimensions:** no standardized canine-tooth length for the breed was located.

### Coat and defensive structure
- Short-to-medium dense double coat with a dark mask (UKC). No armor. Turkish shepherds traditionally fit spiked iron collars to protect the throat from wolf bites; the collar is human equipment and is **excluded** from Defense.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapon and offensive mechanics
The Kangal's weapon is the canid bite: grip-and-hold or grip-and-shake delivered by a broad-headed, heavy-boned molossoid mountain-type dog (FCI Group 2, Section 2.2). Its fighting method against predators is escalation: stand between flock and threat, bark, posture, charge, and bite only if the intruder does not withdraw. There is no second weapon system; forelimbs brace and push but do not grapple like a felid's.

### Guarding behavior as offense
US field trials run by USDA Wildlife Services with Kangals imported from Turkey in 2014 compared them with Karakachans, Transmontanos and the usual American "white dogs". Kinka and Young (2018) found similar responses to wolves across breeds, and a follow-up sheep-survival study reported reductions in depredation relative to white dogs, with the Kangal reducing losses to cougars, black bears and coyotes. This supports genuine deterrent value against large carnivores; it does not show that a single Kangal routinely kills wolves.

### Defense and toughness
A 50 kg heavy-boned dog has meaningful mass and a dense coat, but no armor. Wolves and bears can inflict serious wounds; the traditional spiked collar exists precisely because the throat is vulnerable. Defense is therefore moderate: better than a lighter wild canid of similar attack power, far below armored animals or large bears.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The UKC standard describes an efficient relaxed gait that single-tracks with speed. Kangals are reported as more athletic than heavier mastiff types, but they are not cursorial specialists like wolves, dingoes or coyotes. Agility is moderate.

### Endurance
Kangals patrol with flocks through long grazing days and at night, and the Sivas GPS study shows dogs ranging farther from the herd than the shepherd at night. This supports solid working endurance at guarding pace, not wolf-like long-distance pursuit endurance.

### Senses
Canid smell and hearing, used for nocturnal vigilance around the flock. No breed-specific sensory measurements were located, so the score follows general canid capability.

### Intelligence and tactics
Livestock guardian dogs make independent decisions without human direction: where to lie, when to patrol, when to escalate. The Sivas study found Kangals oriented more closely to the shepherd than to the sheep. This is useful practical cognition but not evidence of wolf-level cooperative hunting tactics.

### Intraspecific fighting
Male guardian dogs contest rank and territory with threat displays and fights. No quantitative study of injury rates in Kangal fights was located.

### Predator defense
The breed exists to confront wolves, and increasingly (in exported programs) cougars, bears and other carnivores. Turkish wolves are generally smaller than the 50 kg Yellowstone male used as the roster's Gray Wolf specimen, which matters when reading anecdotes of Kangals beating wolves.

### Aggression and ferocity
Kangals are bred to be confident and protective and will engage predators that press the flock. Most encounters, however, end at posturing and chasing; the guarding style rewards deterrence over risky fights.

### Environmental strengths
- Open steppe, upland pasture and rolling mountain grassland like the Sivas plateau.
- Cold continental winters and hot dry summers.
- Night guarding around a fixed flock or camp.

### Weaknesses
- No armor; throat and limbs are exposed without the human-fitted collar.
- A single bite weapon that requires close contact.
- Less speed, turning and pursuit endurance than wild canids.
- Behavior is tuned to deterrence and returning to the flock, not to finishing fights.
- Far outmatched by bears, big cats and large ungulates in absolute power.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** coyotes, jackals, foxes, dingoes and other lighter canids; small and medium carnivores without armor; opponents that can be driven off by an escalating charge.
- **Mixed:** a single wolf of similar size, hyenas, leopards, where bite placement, experience and commitment decide it.
- **Bad:** bears, lions, tigers, large ungulates, crocodilians, armored or venomous opponents.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Flock Shield Stance** — positions itself between the flock and an approaching predator and patrols farther out at night, as GPS tracking in Sivas showed. In a matchup this means it fights on its chosen ground and forces the opponent to come through it.
2. **Escalating Challenge** — a graded sequence of bark, posture, charge and bite that drives off most wolves, coyotes and even cougars and bears in field trials before a fight begins. It rewards deterrence rather than raw damage.

### Unique traits
1. **Black-Masked Mountain Molosser** — a heavy-boned, broad-headed mountain-type guardian with the breed's defining dark mask and ears, heavier than wild canids of the same height.
2. **Sivas Working Heritage** — a Turkish national breed kept for generations by shepherds in the Sivas-Kangal region, recognized by the FCI as a distinct breed in 2018 and exported for predator-conflict programs.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The Kangal originates in the Sivas province of central Anatolia, a high steppe and upland pasture region with cold winters. It is kept across Turkey and has been exported to North America, Africa and Europe as a livestock guardian.

### Diet
A domestic dog fed by its owners, on household scraps and prepared food in village practice or commercial diets abroad. It does not hunt for its living, although it will chase and bite intruding carnivores.

### Ecology
In its working role the Kangal is part of a shepherd-flock-dog system, reducing losses of sheep and goats to wolves and other carnivores. Exported Kangals have been studied as a non-lethal tool for reducing conflict between ranchers and wolves, bears, cougars and coyotes.

### Social structure
Kangals usually work in small groups with a flock and bond closely with the shepherd. They are territorial toward strange dogs and people and stay with the flock rather than roaming.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding is managed by people. Pups are raised with livestock so they bond with the flock. Adult size and working maturity are reached at about two years, which is why the UKC measures height at maturity from two years.

### Conservation
Not applicable as a wild species; it is a **Domesticated** breed. Turkey treats the Kangal as a national breed.

### Special features
- Heavy-boned molossoid mountain-type build (FCI Group 2, Section 2.2).
- Dark mask and dark ears over a pale dun coat.
- Dense double coat suited to cold continental winters.
- Strong protective instinct with independent decision-making.
- Relaxed, economical working gait that single-tracks at speed.

### Human interaction
Kangals are serious working guardians. They are generally calm with their own family and flock but suspicious of strangers and need space and training. The viral "strongest bite of any dog" claim has made the breed an internet celebrity, but that number is not supported by any published measurement.

### Genuine fun facts
- The FCI only recognized the Kangal as a separate breed in 2018; before that the same standard number covered the "Anatolian Shepherd Dog".
- Turkish shepherds fit Kangals with spiked iron collars to protect their throats from wolf bites.
- USDA researchers imported Kangals from Turkey in 2014 to test them against wolves in the western United States.
- GPS collars in Sivas showed Kangals patrol farther from the flock at night than the shepherd does.
- The widely shared "743 PSI" bite figure has never been traced to a published measurement.
- The Kangal appears on a Turkish postage stamp as a national breed.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Kangal is a heavy-boned Turkish livestock guardian dog bred to stand between sheep and wolves. It wins by deterrence, size and a strong grip-and-shake bite rather than speed or hunting skill, putting it roughly level with a wild wolf of the same mass, but it has no armor and is badly outmatched by bears and big cats.

### Rich narrative profile
The Kangal comes from the high steppe of Sivas in central Anatolia, where shepherds have long kept large, pale, black-masked dogs to guard their flocks from wolves. A fit working male weighs about 50 kg and stands about 74 cm at the shoulder. Turkish field measurements put village males a little lighter, while US-registered dogs run heavier, so the canonical specimen deliberately sits between the two.

Its fighting style is defined by its job. A Kangal does not hunt; it guards. It keeps itself between the flock and danger, patrols farther out after dark, and meets a threat with a graded escalation of barking, posturing and charging that ends most encounters before teeth are used. When it does commit, it brings a strong bite on a heavy frame, and field trials in the western United States showed Kangals reducing losses to coyotes, cougars and black bears and displacing wolves.

That makes it formidable against lighter canids and small carnivores, and roughly an even contest against a wolf of similar size, where the wild wolf's hunting experience offsets the dog's heavier bone. Against bears, big cats and large ungulates it is simply too small, and without the shepherd's spiked collar its throat is as exposed as any dog's. The famous 743 PSI bite claim does not change this: no published study has measured it.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Weight:** Turkish village males (43–48 kg) are lighter than UKC males (50–66 kg). 50 kg is a deliberate middle choice.
- **Length:** only trunk body length is measured in the breed literature; `length_cm` is a derived estimate and not head-body length.
- **Speed:** no measured maximum; popular speed and daily-distance claims were not traced to data.
- **Bite:** 743 PSI is an internet claim, not a measurement. `0.0` means "no reliable PSI", not "no bite".
- **Lifespan:** 12–15 years comes from companion-dog sources; working-dog survival is unmeasured.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Gray Wolf (50 kg; A55 D41 Ag66 St89 I78 Sp64):** same mass. The Kangal gets slightly higher Defense and Raw Power for heavier bone, but lower Attack, Agility, Stamina, Intelligence and Special because the wild wolf is a proven large-prey killer and endurance hunter. Keeping Kangal Attack (53.0) just under the wolf avoids inflating the breed's reputation.
- **Versus Dingo (18 kg; A40 D24) and African Wild Dog (25 kg; A47 D31):** the Kangal is two to three times heavier, so it sits above both in Attack, Defense and Raw Power, and well below them in Agility and Stamina.
- **Versus Coyote (15 kg; A38 D25 Ag82):** far stronger and tougher; much less agile and less tactically flexible.
- **Versus Tibetan Mastiff (55 kg):** similar size; the Kangal is rated more agile and slightly higher in Attack as the more athletic working dog, the mastiff higher in Defense and Special for its heavy mane and high-altitude physiology.
- **Anti-inflation check:** no score uses the 743 PSI claim, the spiked collar, or anecdotal "Kangal kills wolf" videos.
