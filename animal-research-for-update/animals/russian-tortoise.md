# Russian Tortoise (*Testudo horsfieldii*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Russian Tortoise (also Central Asian tortoise, Horsfield's tortoise, steppe tortoise, four-toed tortoise)
- **Scientific name:** *Testudo horsfieldii* Gray, 1844, family Testudinidae. Long placed in its own genus *Agrionemys*; since 2021 the Turtle Taxonomy Working Group and the Reptile Database treat *Agrionemys* as a subgenus of *Testudo*.
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild Central Asian tortoise of the steppes and deserts between the Caspian Sea and western China. "Russian tortoise" is a pet-trade name dating from the 1970s, when most exports left the Soviet Union. Five subspecies have been proposed, but they are not widely accepted and are not treated separately here.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **1.0 kg** with a **20.0 cm straight carapace length**, representing a well-grown wild female rather than a record animal.
- **Sex choice:** females are the larger sex. Wikipedia gives adult females 15–25 cm and males 13–20 cm, and a field study of more than 800 wild tortoises in Uzbekistan found that males weigh less than females both absolutely and relative to shell size. Males are the fighting sex (they ram and flip rival males), but they are smaller and lighter-shelled, and their only weapons (beak and shell) are the same as the female's. The heavier, larger female is therefore the stronger overall specimen.
- **Population variation:** size and growth vary geographically; the maximum reported female carapace length is 28.64 cm. The species reaches about 2,400 m elevation in Afghanistan.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- German Wikipedia (Steppenschildkröte): adults of 15–25 cm weigh roughly **0.5–2 kg**.
- Bonnet et al. (2001), more than 800 wild tortoises in Uzbekistan: males weigh less than females, absolutely and relative to shell dimensions; females are heavily built with wide shells.
- The two tortoises flown on Zond 5 in 1968 weighed **0.34–0.4 kg** each before flight, showing how light small adults can be.
- **Canonical:** **1.0 kg** for a well-grown adult female, in the middle of the 0.5–2 kg range.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate. No published mean adult female mass was recovered in this run.

### Length
- English Wikipedia: 13–25 cm overall; females 15–25 cm, males 13–20 cm (carapace length).
- Lee and Smith (2010): maximum reported female size **28.64 cm**; males mature at 10–11 cm, females at 13–14 cm.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 20.0 cm straight carapace length**, following the standard turtle convention used for the roster's other chelonians.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
The shell is flattened top to bottom (round from above) for digging, per Bonnet et al. No standard shell-height measurement was found. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
Bonnet et al. found that males walk faster than females, but no absolute walking speed was recovered. **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (unresolved). The species is a slow walker in any case.

### Lifespan
- Wikipedia states the species "can live up to 50 years", but that line carries no citation.
- Lee and Smith (2010) report sexual maturity after 10 to 15 years in the wild (Chernov 1959) and nine years for a female to reach 10 cm, which implies a long-lived species.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 0.0** (unresolved). No verified typical wild lifespan was found.

### Bite and dentition
- A toothless horny beak used for cropping plants. Males bite the forelegs of females during courtship and bite and ram rival males.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-force measurement exists for this species.

### Weapons and digging tools
- Four clawed toes on each front limb (most tortoises have five), with stout forelimbs used to dig. Males have longer claws and longer tails than females.

### Defensive structure
Bonnet et al. describe a thick, well-developed shell that is flattened and round, "closed" with small openings for the limbs. The head and limbs can be pulled in, and the scaled forelimbs then cover the front opening. At 1 kg, however, the shell is small and thin in absolute terms compared with the roster's large turtles.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The Russian tortoise is a herbivore with almost no offensive equipment. Its beak crops leaves and flowers, and its fights are ritual contests between males, who ram each other with the front of the shell and try to overturn the rival. Bonnet et al. note that these fights often end with a male flipped onto its back, where it risks overheating or predation. The same beak bites a female's forelegs during courtship. None of this is dangerous to a predator.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It is a slow, low-slung walker with strong digging forelimbs. The flattened, rounded shell helps it push into soil and move inside burrows. Males have longer legs, wider leg openings in the shell and walk faster than females, and they right themselves faster when flipped. Whole-body agility is very low on the roster scale.

### Endurance
Its endurance is a matter of waiting rather than effort. Lee and Smith summarise field work (Lagarde et al. 2003) showing that the tortoises were active only about three months of the year, and inactive 90% of the time even then. Summer dormancy can run straight into winter dormancy, and the species can spend up to nine months of the year inactive. Digging burrows up to 2 m deep is real sustained work, but high-output exertion is short.

### Senses
Vision and smell guide foraging. No sensory measurements were recovered for the species, so senses are scored as ordinary for a tortoise.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behaviour centres on burrow use, timing activity to cool parts of the day, and seasonal dormancy. It uses and revisits burrows, and several tortoises may share one overnight. No species-specific learning studies were found.

### Sociality and hunting
It does not hunt. It forages at dawn and dusk on herbs and succulent plants during a brief spring season. Wikipedia describes it as fairly social around burrows.

### Fighting and aggression
Male-male combat by ramming and flipping is the main aggression. Against predators the response is to withdraw into the shell or retreat into a burrow.

### Environmental strengths
- Dry, open steppe and desert with soil soft enough to dig.
- Burrows that buffer midday heat, night cold and winter freezing.
- Long periods without food or activity.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny absolute mass and no weapons.
- Very slow on the surface.
- A 1 kg shell can be carried off, dropped or crushed by large predators.
- Can be flipped; overturned animals risk overheating.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** almost none in a fight; it survives small, weak attackers by closing up.
- **Mixed:** small carnivores and birds that cannot open the shell and eventually give up.
- **Bad:** any large predator with crushing jaws, birds able to lift and drop it, and anything that can flip it and wait.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Sealed Steppe Shell** — the flattened, rounded shell closes with small openings, and the scaled forelimbs block the front when the head is pulled in. Small predators and pecking birds face a closed box rather than a meal.
2. **Deep Burrow Retreat** — strong four-clawed forelimbs dig burrows up to 2 m deep, where the tortoise escapes midday heat, night cold, winter and many predators. In a matchup it can end contact by going underground.

### Unique traits
1. **Nine-Month Sleeper** — in parts of its range it is active for only about three months a year, spending the rest in summer and winter dormancy.
2. **First Animals Around the Moon** — two Russian tortoises flew around the Moon aboard the Soviet Zond 5 probe in September 1968 and returned to Earth alive.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The Russian tortoise lives in dry, open steppe, semi-desert and foothill country from the Caspian Sea south through Iran, Afghanistan and Pakistan and east across Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan to Xinjiang, China, up to about 2,400 m. It prefers sandy or loamy soils it can dig into, and it needs some rain to soften the ground for burrowing. The climate has large daily and seasonal temperature swings and very dry summers.

### Diet and ecology
It eats herbaceous and succulent plants, flowers, twigs and some fruit, taking most of its water from food. Lee and Smith note that it avoids grasses and often eats plants that are toxic to grazing mammals, which reduces competition with livestock; other summaries list grasses in the diet, so the point is not settled. Almost all feeding is packed into a short spring season before summer heat drives the tortoises underground.

### Social structure
Tortoises visit neighbouring burrows and several may spend the night in one burrow. Males court females by head bobbing, circling and biting the female's forelegs, and males fight each other by ramming.

### Reproduction and life history
Growth is slow: a female may need about nine years to reach 10 cm, and maturity may take 10 to 15 years in the wild. Males mature at 10–11 cm and females at 13–14 cm carapace length. The slow growth and small clutches make wild populations very sensitive to collection of adults.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List, based on a 1996 assessment by the Tortoise and Freshwater Turtle Specialist Group that is now old. It has been on CITES Appendix II since 1977. Collection for the international pet trade has been the main threat since the 1970s: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service import data summarised by Lee and Smith show 142,475 wild-caught Russian tortoises imported into the United States between 2002 and 2008, almost all for commerce.

### Special features
- Four clawed toes on each front foot.
- Flattened, rounded shell with small limb openings.
- Burrows up to 2 m deep.
- Up to nine months a year in dormancy.
- Females larger and heavier; males longer-legged and faster.
- Horny beak with no teeth.

### Human interaction
It is one of the most common pet tortoises in Europe and North America. A UK veterinary records study found Horsfield's tortoise made up 31.9% of pet tortoises whose species was recorded, second only to Hermann's tortoise. A German survey of more than 1,000 keepers found that a diet of mostly grasses and weeds and outdoor enclosures gave the best health outcomes for *Testudo* tortoises.

### Genuine fun facts
- Two Russian tortoises were the first animals to fly around the Moon, on Zond 5 in 1968; each weighed only 0.34–0.4 kg before the flight.
- Unlike most tortoises, it has only four toes on each front foot.
- Field work found tortoises active only about three months a year, and resting 90% of the time even then.
- Its burrows can reach 2 m deep.
- Males often flip rivals onto their backs during fights, so males are built to right themselves faster than females.
- Between 2002 and 2008, 142,475 wild-caught Russian tortoises were imported into the United States.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Russian tortoise is a small, flat-shelled digger from the dry steppes of Central Asia. A 1 kg adult female has no real weapons, but it can seal itself inside a thick, closed shell or retreat into a burrow up to 2 m deep, and it survives by staying dormant for up to nine months a year. It is well protected for its size and hopeless as an attacker.

### Rich narrative profile
For most of the year the Russian tortoise is underground. On the Central Asian steppe, spring is short and summer is hot and dry, so the tortoise crams its feeding into a few weeks of green growth, then digs down and waits. Field studies have found tortoises active for only about three months a year, and even then they rest most of the time. Four stout claws on each front foot and a flat, rounded shell make it an efficient digger, and its burrows can reach 2 m deep.

As a fighter it has almost nothing to offer. The beak crops plants, and the only fights it takes part in are shoving matches between males, who ram each other and try to tip the rival over. A flipped male is in real danger from heat and predators, and males are built with longer legs and more freedom in the shell to right themselves quickly. Against a predator the tortoise simply closes up: the shell has small openings and the scaled forelimbs cover the front. That works against small attackers, but the whole package weighs about 1 kg, and a large predator can carry it off or crush it.

In ABS terms the Russian tortoise is a near-zero attacker with respectable Defense for its size, very low Agility, and solid Stamina built on patience rather than effort. Its Special score comes from its shell, its deep burrows and its long dormancy. It is also a space pioneer: two of its kind circled the Moon in 1968, before any human did.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** only a broad 0.5–2 kg range was found; the canonical 1.0 kg is a judgment within it.
- **Diet:** Lee and Smith say it avoids grass, while Wikipedia's diet summary includes grasses.
- **Lifespan:** the popular 50-year figure is uncited; lifespan stays unresolved.
- **Conservation:** the IUCN assessment dates from 1996 and is overdue for review.
- **Specimen sex:** males fight but are smaller; the female was chosen as the larger, heavier-shelled animal.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Galapagos Tortoise (102 kg; 31.0 / 76.0 / 17.0 / 80.0 / 48.0 / 75.0):** about one hundredth of the mass. Attack falls from 31.0 to 5.0 and Defense from 76.0 to 35.0 because the shell, though well closed, is tiny in absolute terms. Agility is similar and very low. Stamina is lower because the small tortoise's endurance is dormancy rather than long walks.
- **Versus Snapping Turtle (12 kg; 47.0 / 55.0 / 42.0 / 51.0 / 42.0 / 53.0):** the snapper has a real bite and twelve times the mass, so it is far higher in Attack and higher in Defense. The tortoise's Special is similar because its shell closure and burrowing replace the snapper's long-neck strike.
- **Versus Hedgehog (1 kg; Attack 11.0, Defense 43.0):** similar mass. The hedgehog keeps higher Attack (it is a predator of invertebrates) and slightly higher Defense because spines punish contact, while the shell only resists.
- **Versus Red-eared Slider (1.5 kg; Defense 32.0):** similar-sized chelonians; the tortoise's thicker, more closed shell earns slightly higher Protection, while the slider is far more agile in water.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Defense 35.0 does not treat a 1 kg shell as heavy armor. Stamina 55.0 reflects fasting and dormancy, not fighting endurance.
