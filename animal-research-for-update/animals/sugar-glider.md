# Sugar Glider (*Petaurus breviceps*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Sugar glider
- **Scientific name:** *Petaurus breviceps* Waterhouse, 1839
- **Exact animal represented:** the small gliding possum shown in the site photo and sold worldwide as the "sugar glider". The report follows the species name used on the site and on the photo's Commons page, *Petaurus breviceps*, while recording that the name now covers a narrower group than it used to (see population notes).
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male, **0.14 kg**, about 17 cm head-body with a tail of similar length, gliding membranes fully developed.
- **Sex choice:** males are the larger sex. Wikipedia's summary of the literature gives average adult weights of about **140 g for males and 115 g for females**, and males carry the scent glands used to mark clan members and territory.
- **Population variation:** a 2021 taxonomic revision (Cremona et al., *Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society*, cited by the Australian Museum) split the old, widespread "sugar glider" into three Australian species. *P. breviceps* in the strict sense now covers only coastal south-east Queensland and New South Wales east of the Great Dividing Range; Krefft's glider (*P. notatus*) and the savanna glider (*P. ariel*) take most of the old Australian range. A 2025 genetic study of captive animals in Europe and the United States found the pet-trade stock groups with New Guinean gliders and proposed calling it *P. papuanus*. The site photo shows a captive animal eating a mealworm, so its exact taxon under the new split cannot be confirmed. The ecology, size and behaviour of all these gliders are very similar, and the ratings below apply to any of them.

### Length
- Australian Museum fast facts: size range **16–21 cm**.
- Animal Diversity Web (broad species concept): head and body **120–320 mm**, tail **150–480 mm**; these wide ranges mix several species.
- Wikipedia gives nose-to-tail-tip length of about **24–30 cm**, which is short for a glider whose tail is about as long as its body; sources clearly mix measurement conventions.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 17.0 cm** head-body, tail recorded separately (roughly equal to head-body length).
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Speed and gliding
- The Australian Museum and Wikipedia both state glides of **up to 50 m** between trees.
- Bishop (2007, *Journal of Experimental Biology*) filmed short indoor glides in 3D: gliders flew at high angles of attack (about **44°**), produced lift coefficients around **1.48**, still accelerated downward about **1.0 m/s²** and forward about **2.1 m/s²**, and steered their body orientation through limb movements. Southern flying squirrels in the same set-up produced more lift and less drag, but glided only slightly more shallowly.
- No reliable top glide speed or running speed for the species was found.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- Wikipedia: up to **9 years in the wild**, typically up to 12 years in captivity, record 17.8 years.
- Animal Diversity Web: captive animals up to 14 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 9.0**, flagged as a wild upper value; typical wild survival is probably shorter given owl, snake, quoll and cat predation.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Bite and dentition
- Sugar gliders strip bark and bore holes in trees with their teeth to reach gum and sap (Wikipedia). The elongated fourth finger of the forefoot is used to pull insects out from under bark.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-force measurement exists for the species.

### Defensive structure
- Soft, thick fur only; no armor or tough hide. A furred gliding membrane (patagium) runs from the fifth finger to the ankle on each side.

### Other measurements
- Heart rate **200–300 beats per minute**, breathing **16–40 breaths per minute** (Wikipedia).
- Home range averages about **0.5 ha**; densities of **2–6 animals per hectare** (Wikipedia).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The sugar glider's only real weapons are sharp incisors and small claws on a 140 g body. It is a more active predator than its sweet-food name suggests: in summer it is mainly insectivorous, and it opportunistically takes lizards, small birds and eggs. In Tasmania, where gliders were introduced, they kill nesting swift parrot females and their clutches in tree hollows, and this predation has skewed the parrot's adult sex ratio (Wright 2019, summarising Heinsohn et al.). Under the 2021 split those Tasmanian gliders are treated as Krefft's glider, but the behaviour shows what this body plan can do to a trapped, smaller animal at night. Against anything larger than a small bird, the glider's absolute damage is close to nil.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The glider's defining feature is controlled gliding: it launches from a trunk, spreads its limbs at right angles to the body and can cover 50 m or more to another tree. Bishop's kinematic study shows active steering with the limbs rather than a passive fall, although flying squirrels of similar size generate more lift. It climbs with grasping hind feet that have an opposable toe and uses its partly prehensile tail for balance and steering. It rarely comes to the ground, where it is much less capable.

### Endurance and energy use
Sugar gliders forage through the night over small home ranges. Their main stamina tool is energy saving rather than sustained output: in cold weather they huddle and can drop into daily torpor lasting **2–23 hours** (Wikipedia). During a cyclone-strength storm in subtropical New South Wales, radio-tracked wild gliders stayed in their nests, entered deep torpor and all survived, returning to normal foraging the following nights (Nowack et al. 2015).

### Senses
Large eyes and swivelling ears suit nocturnal hunting. Behavioural tests, genetics and retinal staining showed sugar gliders are **trichromatic with ultraviolet sensitivity** (Nielsen et al. 2025), unusual for a nocturnal mammal.

### Intelligence and tactics
Gliders live in clans of up to seven adults plus young that share a nest; dominant males mark clan members and the nest area with scent, and clans defend their territory "aggressively and noisily" against other gliders and animals (Australian Museum). They switch diets by season and choose glide routes through the canopy. This supports solid social and spatial intelligence for a very small mammal, not problem-solving at primate or corvid level.

### Fighting and aggression
Territorial disputes involve chasing, vocal threats and biting. Captive gliders bite readily when handled roughly. The glider presses conflicts with rivals and with small prey but escapes, rather than fights, larger threats.

### Predator defense
Owls (*Ninox*) are the main natural predators; kookaburras, goannas, snakes, quolls and feral cats also take gliders (Wikipedia). Defense is almost entirely escape: gliding away, retreating into narrow hollows and sheltering in a group nest.

### Environmental strengths and limitations
- Tall, open forest with spaced trees is ideal: every trunk is a launch point and escape route.
- Ground fights and open country remove its gliding advantage.
- Cold and food shortage are handled through torpor and huddling rather than by staying active.

### Weaknesses
- Extremely low mass, power and durability.
- Weapons are only small incisors and claws.
- No armor; any solid bite from a medium predator is lethal.
- Heavily dependent on trees for its main advantage.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** insects, small nesting birds, eggs and other animals smaller than itself; anything that cannot follow it through the canopy.
- **Mixed:** other small arboreal mammals of similar size, where agility and first bite decide.
- **Bad:** owls, snakes, cats, quolls and essentially every medium or large animal on the roster in a direct confrontation.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Canopy Glide Escape** — spreads the furred membranes between wrists and ankles to glide 50 m or more between trees, steering with its limbs and tail. Against ground or trunk-bound opponents it simply leaves the fight.
2. **Storm Torpor** — drops its body temperature and metabolism for hours at a time; wild gliders used deep torpor to sit out a cyclone-strength storm and all survived. It turns bad conditions into a waiting game rather than a crisis.

### Unique traits
1. **Clan Scent Marker** — lives in nest-sharing clans of up to seven adults; dominant males mark group members and territory with forehead and chest glands, and clans defend their patch noisily against outsiders.
2. **Ultraviolet Night Vision** — a rod-dominated retina that still supports trichromatic colour vision with ultraviolet sensitivity, uncommon among nocturnal mammals.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Sugar gliders live in forests and woodlands, nesting in tree hollows lined with leaves. Under the 2021 split, *P. breviceps* in the strict sense occurs only in coastal south-east Queensland and New South Wales east of the Great Dividing Range, up to about 2,000 m in the eastern ranges. Krefft's glider and the savanna glider cover much of the rest of eastern and northern Australia, and further glider populations occur in New Guinea and nearby islands. Captive and pet populations are kept worldwide.

### Diet and ecology
A seasonal omnivore. In summer it eats mainly insects and other arthropods; in winter it relies on plant exudates such as acacia gum, eucalyptus sap, manna, honeydew and lerp, stripping bark or boring holes with its teeth to reach them. Nectar, pollen, acacia seeds, fungi, fruit, bird eggs, lizards and small birds are taken when available. Heavy pollen feeding makes it a likely pollinator of *Banksia*. It shares forests with squirrel gliders and yellow-bellied gliders by using food resources differently.

### Social structure
Groups of up to seven adults and their young share a nest as a clan. Within the clan they are playful and social; toward other clans and intruders they are territorial. Dominant males scent-mark clan members and the nest area.

### Reproduction and life history
Females have a well-developed pouch and commonly raise twins, which stay in the pouch for just over two months and then begin foraging, usually with the mother (Australian Museum). Females mature late in their first year (Animal Diversity Web).

### Conservation
The broad, pre-split species was assessed as Least Concern by the IUCN in 2016. The narrowly defined *P. breviceps* has a much smaller range, and Wikipedia notes it is sensitive to disasters such as the 2019–20 Australian bushfires; a formal reassessment of the split species is still needed. Road construction, drought and habitat clearing affect local populations.

### Special features
- Furred gliding membrane from the fifth finger to the ankle.
- Long, partly prehensile tail used for steering and balance.
- Opposable toe on each hind foot for a secure grip.
- Elongated fourth finger for extracting insects from under bark.
- Daily torpor and group huddling to save energy.
- UV-sensitive trichromatic vision.

### Human interaction
Sugar gliders are among the most widely kept exotic pets. Genetic work shows that European and US pet stock came from New Guinean gliders, not the eastern Australian animals that now carry the strict name. Introduced gliders in Tasmania are a serious predator of the critically endangered swift parrot, prompting nest-box and predator-control research.

### Genuine fun facts
- The Latin name *Petaurus breviceps* translates roughly as "short-headed rope-dancer", a nod to its canopy acrobatics.
- A single glide can cover **50 m** or more.
- In a storm with cyclone-strength winds, wild gliders switched to deep torpor and all survived.
- Its heart beats **200–300 times a minute**.
- Behavioural tests show it can see ultraviolet light as well as three colour channels, at night.
- Pet "sugar gliders" in Europe and the US turn out to be New Guinean gliders, a different species from the eastern Australian *P. breviceps* under the 2021 split.
- Introduced gliders in Tasmania kill so many nesting female swift parrots that the parrots' adult sex ratio has become male-biased.

### Concise site-ready summary
A 140-gram gliding possum that wins by leaving: the sugar glider can steer a 50-metre glide between trees, sees ultraviolet light at night and can drop into torpor to wait out storms. It is a real hunter of insects, lizards and nesting birds, but in a straight fight almost any larger animal overwhelms it.

### Rich narrative profile
The sugar glider is a pocket-sized specialist of the forest canopy. Weighing around 140 g, it has no meaningful armor, strength or reach, and on the absolute roster scale its Attack and Defense sit near the floor with other tiny mammals. What it does have is a furred membrane stretched from wrist to ankle. From a high trunk it can launch, hold its limbs out at right angles and steer a glide of 50 metres or more, adjusting its body angle through small limb movements that high-speed filming has captured in detail.

That mobility makes the glider very hard to pin down in the right habitat. An opponent on the ground or on a single trunk may never get a second chance at contact, because the glider has already crossed to the next tree. Its senses support the same strategy: large eyes, swivelling ears and, unusually for a nocturnal mammal, colour vision that reaches into the ultraviolet. At rest it shares a leaf-lined hollow with a clan of up to seven adults, and dominant males mark the group and territory with scent.

Its diet is broader and fiercer than its name. Gum and sap carry it through winter, but in summer it hunts insects, and it will kill lizards and small birds and raid eggs. Introduced gliders in Tasmania prey so heavily on nesting swift parrots that they threaten that species. Away from trees, though, the glider has few answers: owls, snakes, quolls and cats all take it, and any medium-sized opponent that catches it ends the contest immediately. In a battle ranking it is a textbook example of elite Agility and Special paired with near-minimum Attack and Defense.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Taxonomy:** the site name *P. breviceps* now refers to a small eastern Australian range; the photographed animal is probably pet-trade stock from New Guinea. Size and behaviour are similar enough that the ratings do not change.
- **Length:** published ranges mix head-body and total length and mix the old and new species concepts; 17 cm head-body is used.
- **Lifespan:** only a wild upper value is available; 9 years overstates typical survival.
- **Speed:** no instrumented glide speed was located, so `speed_mps` stays 0.0.
- **Swift parrot predation:** documented in Tasmania for gliders now assigned to Krefft's glider; used only as evidence of the body plan's predatory capability.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Flying Squirrel (0.16 kg; A9 D8 Ag96 St61 I66 Sp87):** the closest roster match in size and lifestyle. Bishop's direct comparison found flying squirrels produce more lift and less drag, so the glider sits a few points lower in Agility (93.0) and Special (84.0). Its more predatory diet and clan territoriality justify slightly higher Attack (10.0) and Ferocity; Defense is slightly lower (7.0) for a lighter body.
- **Versus Quoll (3.5 kg; A32 D22):** the quoll is a real predator of gliders and 25 times heavier; it must stay far above the glider in Attack, Defense and raw power.
- **Versus Koala (12 kg; A28 D23 Ag54 Sp62):** a fellow marsupial arboreal specialist, but the koala's mass gives it vastly more absolute force; the glider wins Agility and Special by a wide margin because it can glide.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholders (Attack 2.5, Agility 52.5, Special 12.3) are not used. Attack stays near the roster floor on absolute grounds, while the gliding membrane earns high Agility and Special, as it did for the flying squirrel.
