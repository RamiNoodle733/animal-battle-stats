# Gibbon / White-handed Gibbon (*Hylobates lar*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Gibbon, standardized here to **white-handed gibbon / lar gibbon**.
- **Scientific name:** *Hylobates lar* (Linnaeus, 1771), family Hylobatidae.
- **Exact animal represented:** the live schema uses only the family-level label `Hylobatidae`, which is too broad for species-level physical fields. This staging report resolves the roster animal to *Hylobates lar*, a widespread and well-studied small gibbon, rather than mixing measurements among siamangs, hoolocks, crested gibbons and *Hylobates* species.
- **Status:** living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **6.5 kg** and **52.0 cm head-body length**. ADW reports males at 5.0-7.6 kg and 43.5-58.4 cm, slightly larger than females at 4.4-6.8 kg and 42.0-58.0 cm. The canonical male is deliberately robust but not a record individual.
- **Dimorphism:** modest male-biased size according to ADW. Pelage color is not a reliable sex marker in this species: both sexes can occur in light and dark color morphs.
- **Population variation:** *H. lar* spans mainland and Sumatran populations and includes named subspecies. Body measurements overlap substantially; no single extreme population is used as the roster baseline.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass and body length
ADW gives males **5.0-7.6 kg** and females **4.4-6.8 kg**, with male body length **43.5-58.4 cm** and female **42.0-58.0 cm**. Copenhagen Zoo gives a broader 4-8 kg and 41-58 cm range. Canonical values are therefore **6.5 kg** and **52.0 cm**, representative of a mature male rather than the upper endpoint.

### Height
No standardized shoulder-height field is biologically useful for this long-armed ape, and sources mix standing height with head-body length. `height_cm` remains **0.0** rather than pretending those measurements are interchangeable.

### Arm span and locomotor anatomy
Copenhagen Zoo reports an arm span of about **1.5 m**. Smithsonian notes gibbon arms average roughly 1.5 times the length of their legs. The hands are long and hook-like, shoulders highly mobile, and the species lacks a tail. These proportions support brachiation, not exceptional absolute striking strength.

### Speed
Institutional sources converge on very fast canopy travel. Smithsonian states gibbons can reach about **35 mph**, while Copenhagen Zoo gives **55 km/h** and the American Museum of Natural History gives up to **56 km/h**. Because the values are institutional but appear to be generalized gibbon performance rather than a controlled *H. lar* maximum-speed trial, the canonical recommendation is a cautious **15.3 m/s (55 km/h)** with **medium-low confidence**, explicitly tagged as canopy brachiation rather than terrestrial sprint speed.

### Lifespan
ADW reports about **30 years in the wild** and up to **44 years in captivity**. Zoo sources vary, with Copenhagen giving 25-35 years and Columbus reporting a lower managed-population median. Canonical `lifespan_years` is **30.0**, representing wild potential rather than a captive maximum or median survival statistic.

### Bite force and dentition
Gibbons possess functional canine teeth and can inflict serious bite wounds, but no defensible species-specific bite pressure in PSI was found. The live 100 PSI placeholder is rejected. **`bite_force_psi: 0.0`**. No canine-length number is proposed because this run did not locate a sufficiently representative sex- and age-controlled *H. lar* measurement.

### Defenses
There is no armor, shell, thick hide or large fat layer. Dense fur offers environmental protection and may visually enlarge the animal, but the main defensive system is avoidance: rapid three-dimensional canopy escape, balance, reach, vigilance, territorial calling and access to supports unavailable to many predators.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The primary physical weapons are the jaws and canines, backed by grasping hands and feet. Long arms can pull, shove, grapple and control position, but their strongest specialization is locomotor reach rather than gorilla-like absolute force. A 6.5 kg gibbon should never be power-scaled like a great ape simply because both are apes.

### Offensive mechanics
At close range a gibbon can seize, bite, rake or strike while using branches to alter angle and distance. The strongest direct evidence that its aggression can be consequential comes from a peer-reviewed report of **lethal territorial aggression in *H. lar***: an adult male was seriously wounded by a neighboring adult male during intergroup conflict and disappeared 24 days later after marked deterioration. The death was probably direct or indirect from the wound and subsequent infection. This establishes real injury potential without implying huge instantaneous damage.

### Defensive adaptations and durability
Defense is dominated by not being hit. There is little passive protection. Against a similarly sized terrestrial opponent, the gibbon's light frame is vulnerable if pinned or bitten. In a complex canopy, however, long arms, hook-like hands and rapid support-to-support transitions provide exceptional escape routes.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Brachiation is the defining system. Peer-reviewed kinetic work on *H. lar* identifies two mechanically distinct brachiation gaits: slower continuous-contact movement and faster ricochetal brachiation with an aerial phase. At faster speeds, translational and rotational kinetic energy are exchanged in a whip-like mechanism that helps control the swing and the next hand contact. Separate work documents one-armed brachiation and the ability to preserve forward momentum while one hand is occupied.

Institutional accounts report canopy speeds around 55-56 km/h and gap-crossing launches of roughly 10 m or more. These figures justify elite maneuverability in the correct environment, but not elite Attack or Defense.

### Endurance
White-handed gibbons spend substantial portions of the day moving, feeding, grooming and defending a canopy home range, but brachiation can exploit pendular energy exchange and does not imply pursuit-endurance comparable to cursorial mammals. ADW also reports long nightly resting periods in sleeping trees. Stamina is therefore good but not extreme.

### Senses
Vision, hearing, touch and proprioception are all important for rapid canopy travel. Accurate branch targeting at speed requires excellent visuomotor control. Loud vocal duets function in long-range territorial communication. There is no basis for extraordinary olfaction, echolocation or another rare sensory mechanism.

### Intelligence, learning and tactics
As a lesser ape, *H. lar* has flexible locomotor planning, social recognition, learned communication and strong spatial demands. Still, the report avoids inheriting the live site's extremely high Intelligence/Tactics placeholders. Evidence here supports sophisticated primate cognition, but not parity with the roster's strongest tool-using great apes, corvids or other demonstrated problem-solving specialists.

### Intraspecific fighting and territorial behavior
ADW describes defended home ranges, boundary singing and male participation in chasing neighboring groups. The Palombit case confirms that adult male territorial fights can cause grave injury. This makes the species more than a purely evasive canopy specialist, but serious physical combat appears costly rather than routine.

### Hunting and diet
The species is primarily frugivorous, supplementing fruit with leaves, flowers, shoots, insects and eggs or other small animal matter depending on source. It is not a specialized vertebrate predator. Combat behavior should therefore be based more on territorial defense and escape than on predatory killing technique.

### Environmental strengths
- Continuous forest canopy with many overhead supports.
- Vertical terrain where brachiation and gap crossing create routes unavailable to terrestrial opponents.
- Encounters where rapid disengagement, angle changes and reach matter more than mass.

### Environmental limitations
- Open ground removes most of the brachiation advantage.
- Water, featureless arenas and environments without overhead supports sharply reduce mobility specialization.
- A lightweight frame is vulnerable to large bites, crushing, horns, talons and heavy impacts.

### Major weaknesses
Low absolute mass, little passive armor, no specialized killing weapon beyond teeth, dependence on usable supports for peak locomotion, and poor matchup prospects if a much larger opponent secures a hold.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Best:** slower similarly sized animals in complex arboreal terrain, where repeated repositioning and disengagement are possible.
- **Mixed:** agile small carnivores or raptors, which can threaten the light body despite its maneuverability.
- **Worst:** medium and large predators, great apes and megafauna in a confined or terrestrial arena. The gibbon cannot convert proportional arm performance into comparable absolute force.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Ricochet Brachiation**: at high travel speeds the gibbon uses aerial phases and whip-like energy transfer between swings, enabling exceptionally rapid canopy repositioning and evasive angle changes.
2. **Canopy Gap Launch**: long arms, mobile shoulders and hook-like hands allow ballistic transitions between separated supports, creating escape and approach routes unavailable to most similarly sized mammals.

### Unique traits
1. **Hook-Hand Suspension Frame**: elongated fingers, highly mobile shoulders and very long forelimbs form an anatomy optimized for hanging travel rather than terrestrial force production.
2. **Territorial Duet Network**: loud pair and group vocalizations advertise occupancy and coordinate boundary defense over forest distances before physical contact is required.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and geographic range
White-handed gibbons inhabit tropical evergreen, dipterocarp, monsoon and related forests in Southeast Asia. ADW places them mainly in the high canopy and notes occurrence up to roughly 1,200 m elevation. Modern range descriptions include Thailand, Malaysia, Myanmar, Laos and northern Sumatra, with historical loss from portions of the former range.

### Diet and ecology
Fruit is the major food, supplemented by leaves, flowers, shoots, insects and eggs or other small animal foods. Their frugivory makes them important participants in tropical forest food webs and likely seed movement. They are diurnal and strongly arboreal.

### Social structure and life history
Groups generally center on a breeding pair and offspring, although gibbon social systems are more flexible than the old simplistic 'always monogamous' label suggests. Territorial songs and duets are prominent. Gestation is around seven months in zoo references. Young remain dependent for an extended period, consistent with slow ape life history.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Endangered** in current institutional/IUCN-linked references. Major pressures include forest loss, fragmentation, hunting and capture for the illegal pet trade.

### Adaptations
- Very long arms and hook-like hands for suspension and brachiation.
- Highly mobile shoulder and wrist mechanics.
- Upright balance during bipedal branch or ground walking.
- Loud, structured territorial vocalizations.
- Strong visuomotor coordination for high-speed branch targeting.

### Human interaction
Gibbons are heavily affected by habitat conversion and wildlife trade. Rehabilitation centers often receive animals taken for the pet trade after adults were killed or displaced. Captive individuals require complex vertical environments because locomotor specialization is central to their welfare.

### Genuine fun facts
- White-handed gibbons are apes, not monkeys, and **have no tail**.
- Their arms are extraordinarily long relative to their legs, and institutional sources report arm spans around **1.5 m**.
- Fast brachiation is mechanically distinct from slow brachiation and includes an **aerial phase**.
- A gibbon can perform one-armed brachiation while carrying food, with kinematic adjustments that preserve forward momentum.
- Institutional sources report canopy travel around **55-56 km/h**, remarkable for an animal weighing only several kilograms.
- Territorial conflict is not purely ritual: a peer-reviewed field report documented an adult male receiving wounds severe enough to plausibly cause his later death.
- Both light and dark pelage morphs occur, so coat color should not be used to infer sex.

### Concise site-ready summary
The white-handed gibbon is a small Southeast Asian ape built for extreme canopy mobility. A healthy adult male weighs only about 6.5 kg, so its absolute strength, durability and weapon damage remain modest, but its long arms, hook-like hands and specialized shoulders make it one of the roster's most maneuverable animals in trees. It can brachiate at high speed, launch across canopy gaps, communicate territorially through loud songs and, when conflict escalates, use grasping and canine bites capable of serious injury.

### Rich narrative profile
The gibbon's battle identity is defined by separation between mobility and power. Its skeleton and soft tissues are organized around suspension: a compact torso hangs beneath elongated forelimbs while the hands repeatedly become hooks, release, and recapture supports. At low speed, the animal can use continuous-contact pendular movement. At high speed, *H. lar* shifts into ricochetal brachiation with aerial phases and a more dynamic transfer of rotational and translational energy. In a forest canopy, that gives the gibbon a route-planning advantage that few mammals can match.

That spectacular locomotion must not leak into Attack or Defense. At roughly 6.5 kg, a gibbon is far below large carnivores and great apes in absolute force and trauma tolerance. Its canines are real weapons and territorial males can inflict grave wounds, but it has no armor and no heavy crushing apparatus. A matchup that becomes a stationary grapple against a much larger animal is unfavorable. Its best combat is positional: evade, change level, exploit overhead routes, bite if cornered, and disengage.

Cognition and communication strengthen that positional style. Gibbons maintain spatial knowledge of a complex canopy, coordinate family life and advertise territories through conspicuous vocal routines. Those capacities justify high intelligence and tactics relative to most mammals, while remaining below the roster's strongest evidence-based problem solvers. The resulting ABS profile is intentionally polarized: elite Agility, strong cognition and environment-dependent Special, but only low absolute Attack and Defense.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Species identity:** the live profile says only Hylobatidae. This report deliberately resolves it to *H. lar*. Production migration should explicitly approve that taxonomic decision rather than silently applying *H. lar* data to all gibbons.
- **Speed:** 55-56 km/h appears in multiple major institutional summaries, but a controlled species-specific maximum trial was not located. Keep `15.3 m/s` medium-low confidence and canopy-specific.
- **Lifespan:** wild potential, zoo median and captive maxima answer different questions. `30.0` years follows ADW's wild figure rather than mixing them.
- **Bite force:** no credible PSI measurement was found, so zero means unavailable, not biologically bite-less.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Flying Squirrel:** Gibbon has much higher absolute power, Attack, Toughness and cognition, but both are elite movement specialists. Flying Squirrel retains a stronger unusual locomotor Special because gliding changes access without continuous handholds; Gibbon is more maneuverable on dense supports.
- **Versus Gazelle:** Gazelle is far heavier and should retain greater absolute Attack/Defense and running endurance. Gibbon can exceed it in fine three-dimensional maneuverability and cognition, especially in canopy terrain.
- **Versus Gecko:** both are surface-access specialists, but Gecko's adhesive system is a rarer biological mechanism and merits higher Special. Gibbon is vastly stronger in absolute physical terms and cognitively more flexible.
- **Versus large primates elsewhere in the roster:** do not let the word 'ape' compress the power gap. A 6.5 kg gibbon must remain far below chimpanzees and gorillas in Raw Power, Attack, Defense and Toughness.
- **Inflation check:** Agility 98.0 is intentionally extreme because it rates control and evasive maneuverability, not strength. Attack 28.0 and Defense 22.0 preserve the enormous absolute gap between this small ape and serious medium/large combat animals.
