# Sloth Bear — *Melursus ursinus*

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Roster name:** Sloth Bear
- **Exact taxon:** *Melursus ursinus* (G. K. Shaw, 1791), family Ursidae. The live schema already identifies this taxon correctly.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime fully mature **mainland adult male**, **120.0 kg**, **175.0 cm head-body length**, **80.0 cm shoulder height**. This is a strong representative male, not a record animal.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** meaningful. Animal Diversity Web reports females 55–95 kg and males 80–140 kg, with males about 30–40% heavier. San Diego Zoo gives 70–145 kg for males and 50–95 kg for females. IBA reports a wider male range/average summary of 80–175 kg. The 120 kg canonical male sits comfortably inside the overlapping institutional evidence rather than using the upper extreme.
- **Population variation:** two commonly recognized geographic forms are mainland *M. u. ursinus* and the smaller Sri Lankan *M. u. inornatus*. The canonical specimen is mainland because it better represents the larger fighting form. Sri Lankan telemetry and image evidence are retained as subspecies-specific rather than silently generalized.

## 2. Physical dimensions and factual measurements

### Mass, length and height
- ADW: **55–140 kg** species range, with males **80–140 kg**, females **55–95 kg**; **1.5–1.9 m** length; adults **60–90 cm** at the shoulder.
- San Diego Zoo: males **70–145 kg**, females **50–95 kg**; **1.4–1.9 m** length; **0.6–0.9 m** shoulder height.
- Smithsonian: approximately **1.5–2.0 m** long, **0.5–1.0 m** at shoulder, average **90–140 kg** in its general summary.
- IBA: body length **140–190 cm**, males **80–175 kg**, females **55–95 kg**.
- **Canonical recommendation:** 120.0 kg / 175.0 cm / 80.0 cm. Confidence high for dimensions and moderate-high for representative male mass because source conventions differ.

### Speed and locomotion
No instrumented, species-specific maximum running speed was found in the strong sources reviewed. San Diego Zoo and ADW state that sloth bears can run faster than a person, and peer-reviewed tiger-interaction work documents explosive close-range charges, but neither provides a measured maximum. The live 25 km/h value is therefore not retained as a verified fact. `speed_mps` remains **0.0 unresolved**.

Sloth bears have large feet, in-turned forefeet and long blunt claws specialized for excavation. Adults can climb, but the 2024 tiger-defense analysis notes that the long digging claws hamper rapid refuge climbing relative to more arboreal Asiatic black and sun bears. They can stand bipedally during threat assessment and defensive displays.

### Lifespan
IBA reports **25–28 years in the wild** and up to 40 years in captivity. San Diego Zoo reports **15.8 years median life expectancy**, which is a different metric and should not be mistaken for maximum wild lifespan. For a site field intended as a representative lifespan rather than a captive record, **26.0 years** is recommended with moderate confidence.

### Claws, teeth and feeding apparatus
- San Diego Zoo reports large, thick front claws around **3 in / 8 cm** long, used to tear open termite nests.
- Adults lack the two central upper incisors, creating a gap for suction feeding. They have large defensive canines despite reduced premolars/molars associated with myrmecophagy.
- The lips are highly protrusible, the palate extended, and nostrils can be voluntarily closed while feeding.
- No defensible direct species-specific bite-pressure PSI measurement was found. The live **900 PSI** value is unsupported and should be rejected. `bite_force_psi = 0.0` pending direct measurement or a validated force/contact-area model.
- Christiansen's comparative ursid work is cited by Sharp et al. (2024) as showing sloth-bear canines comparable in size/strength to those of carnivorous bears, but this does **not** establish PSI.

### Defensive structures
The sloth bear has no armor or heavy blubber. Its long shaggy coat is functionally important: it protects against biting insects, and Sharp et al. (2024) argue from observed tiger interactions that the long coat and especially neck ruff can interfere with predator bites. This is a real defensive contribution, but it is far below shell, osteoderm or massive-hide protection.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons
The principal weapons are long, heavy foreclaws and large canines. The claws evolved for breaking hard termite structures, so they are blunt compared with felid claws but long, powerful and attached to strongly built digging forelimbs. In close defense the bear rises, swipes, grapples and bites. Human attack records demonstrate that this combination can produce deep lacerations and fractures, especially to the face and arms, without requiring an invented bite-pressure number.

### Defensive aggression and tiger interactions
The strongest direct combat evidence comes from Sharp, Garshelis & Larson (2024), who analyzed **43 documented sloth bear–tiger interactions** from 2011–2023. Sloth bears were especially likely to stand and charge when first detecting a tiger at **<3 m**. **86%** of documented interactions ended without contact, while four interactions, about **9%**, ended in the bear's death. This supports unusually forceful predator deterrence while also proving that the tiger remains the superior lethal predator. Ferocity and defensive resolve should be high, but the bear must not be scored as tiger-equivalent in raw offense.

### Human-conflict evidence
A peer-reviewed Kanha-Pench study interviewed **166 victims** of sloth-bear attacks from 2004–2016. Most attacks occurred in forests and were associated with close encounters rather than predatory hunting. A Sri Lankan clinical series documented multiple soft-tissue and bone injuries, including severe facial/arm trauma. These data support dangerous close-range defensive capability, not a claim that the species routinely hunts large mammals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The bear can gallop and charge explosively, stand bipedally, swim, dig rapidly and climb to some degree. Its in-turned feet, shaggy mass and long blunt claws do not support cat-like turning or pursuit agility. Sharp et al. specifically connect its digging morphology to poorer refuge climbing against tigers. Agility should therefore be moderate, with better short-range reorientation than its awkward appearance suggests but no elite locomotor score.

### Endurance
Wild home-range evidence shows sustained terrestrial ranging, but not exceptional cursorial endurance. Chitwan estimates are around **9–14 km²**, Central India reports extend from about **12 to 85 km²**, and Sri Lankan telemetry on 10 adults found mean 95% fixed-kernel home ranges of **2.2 km² for females and 3.8 km² for males**. These differences strongly reflect habitat and food availability. Sloth bears do not hibernate, which supports year-round activity, but that should not be converted into extreme combat stamina.

### Senses
Smell is the dominant sense. Smithsonian describes a keen sense of smell, while San Diego Zoo states vision and hearing are not as strong as in some other bears. Sharp et al. note a tactical liability: bears often forage head-down while making substantial digging/blowing noise, which can reduce vigilance and allow tigers to approach surprisingly close. Thus sensory specialization is strong for food detection but imperfect for all-direction threat awareness.

### Intelligence and tactics
Sloth bears show flexible habitat use and complex defensive behavior, but direct cognition evidence argues against inflation. In a controlled novel-foraging problem, tested sloth bears failed to use a movable bucket to reach honey even after social cues or relevant experience. This single task does not define overall cognition, but it is stronger evidence than assigning a high Intelligence score from bear reputation alone. Tactical defensive behavior is more clearly demonstrated than abstract problem solving.

### Feeding and predation behavior
The species is omnivorous and strongly myrmecophagous. Ants and termites are major foods, with fruit, honey, flowers, crops, eggs and carrion varying by season and locality. IBA notes that mammal predation is rare. Sloth bears therefore should not receive predator-style Attack bonuses from diet. Their combat equipment is powerful because digging claws and defensive canines transfer effectively to fighting, not because they are habitual macropredators.

### Intraspecific behavior
Sloth bears are primarily solitary outside mating and mother-cub associations. Competition can occur around resources. Mothers are particularly defensive and uniquely among bears routinely carry cubs on the back for months, allowing a female to confront threats without leaving cubs on the ground.

### Environmental strengths
- Dry and moist tropical forests, scrub, savanna and grassland favor its digging and scent-based foraging.
- Broken terrain and cover help close-range defensive encounters.
- Heat tolerance is aided by a shaggy coat lacking the dense underfur typical of colder-climate bears.
- Termite-rich habitats reward its extreme feeding specialization.

### Environmental weaknesses
- Open terrain exposes it to faster large predators.
- Long blunt claws are excellent excavation tools but less effective for rapid tree escape.
- Head-down noisy foraging can reduce predator vigilance.
- It is not built for long pursuit of fast prey.
- Against substantially larger bears or big cats, its courage does not erase mass, weapon-speed and predatory-skill disadvantages.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Favored:** most smaller terrestrial mammals lacking armor or specialized ranged defenses; opponents that must enter foreclaw/bite range.
- **Competitive:** similarly sized carnivores where its mass, claws and exceptional willingness to countercharge can impose injury risk.
- **Poor:** prime large tigers and substantially heavier bears; heavy megafauna; opponents whose armor neutralizes claw/bite damage.
- **Context-sensitive:** fast evasive animals on open ground can exploit its limited pursuit specialization even when they cannot win a direct exchange.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings

- **Attack: 65.0** — a 120 kg ursid with powerful forelimbs, long claws and large canines can inflict major trauma, but it is not a specialized large-prey killer and remains clearly below tiger-class offense.
- **Defense: 57.0** — substantial mass, ursid robustness, shaggy coat and aggressive deterrence help, but there is no real armor and tigers can kill adults.
- **Agility: 55.0** — surprisingly explosive at close range, yet the digging-adapted feet/claws and terrestrial body plan prevent high-end maneuverability.
- **Stamina: 63.0** — active year-round with meaningful ranging capacity, but without evidence for elite pursuit or migratory endurance.
- **Intelligence: 59.0** — behaviorally adaptable and tactically effective in defense, intentionally moderated by direct failed novel-problem evidence and no basis for primate/cetacean-level cognition.
- **Special: 65.0** — powerful suction feeding, closable nostrils, extreme excavation claws and predator-deterrent defensive behavior make a distinctive package, but the feeding specializations are not equivalent to venom, electricity or echolocation in direct matchup impact.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Tiger-Check Countercharge** — when a major threat appears at very close range, the bear can rear and launch an explosive claw-and-bite countercharge; the 2024 interaction dataset shows this frequently deters tigers before physical contact.
2. **Termite-Vacuum Apparatus** — protrusible lips, missing upper central incisors, extended palate and closable nostrils create powerful insect suction after the foreclaws breach a nest. Primarily ecological, but it reflects unusually specialized craniofacial control.

### Unique traits
1. **Eight-Centimeter Excavator Claws** — long, thick foreclaws built to break hard termite structures double as formidable close-range striking tools, though their blunt digging form compromises rapid climbing.
2. **Cub-Carrying Shag Coat** — uniquely among living bears, mothers routinely carry cubs on their backs; the long coat also helps protect against insects and may reduce the effectiveness of some predator bites around the neck/body.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and geographic range
Sloth bears are endemic to the Indian subcontinent. Current range centers on India, Sri Lanka and Nepal, with possible/rare status in Bhutan and historical loss from Bangladesh. They occupy dry and moist deciduous forest, scrub, savanna, grassland and some evergreen forest. Habitat use is highly variable with food, cover and human pressure.

### Diet and ecology
They are omnivores with unusually strong specialization on ants and termites. Fruit can become a major seasonal food, alongside honey, flowers, crops, eggs, carrion and other opportunistic foods. Mammal predation is rare. Their excavation and fruit consumption influence soil disturbance and seed dispersal.

### Social structure and activity
Generally solitary except for mating and mothers with cubs. Activity can shift with human disturbance: animals may become more nocturnal around people while using daylight more readily in less disturbed areas. Home ranges vary dramatically among ecosystems and sexes.

### Reproduction and life history
San Diego Zoo reports maturity around **3–5 years**, gestation **4–7 months including delayed implantation**, and usually **1–2 cubs**, rarely 3. Cubs ride on the mother's back, a distinctive ursid behavior linked to predator defense and the mother's inability to rely on fast tree escape.

### Conservation
IBA identifies the species as **Vulnerable** with a decreasing overall trend and lists it in **CITES Appendix I**. Major threats include habitat loss/fragmentation, human-bear conflict, retaliatory killing and historical exploitation in the dancing-bear trade. A 2024 Nepal report cited a global estimate below 17,000 individuals, while IBA emphasizes that exact population estimates remain uncertain.

### Adaptations
The species represents an unusual bear trajectory toward myrmecophagy: long claws, in-turned feet, mobile lips, a dental gap, closable nostrils and strong suction. Unlike an anteater, however, it retains large ursid canines and a robust bear body, making defensive encounters much more dangerous than its insect-heavy diet might suggest.

### Human interaction
Sloth bears are responsible for severe conflict in parts of South Asia. The best evidence indicates many attacks are defensive close encounters rather than predation. This distinction matters for both safety and ABS calibration: high Ferocity reflects a threat-response strategy, not a tendency to hunt humans.

### Genuine fun facts
- Sloth bears are the only living bears that routinely transport cubs on the mother's back.
- Their front claws can reach about **8 cm** and are primarily termite-excavation tools.
- Adults lack the two central upper incisors, leaving a suction gap for insects.
- Their nostrils can close voluntarily while they blow soil and suck insects from nests.
- San Diego Zoo reports that feeding suction sounds can be audible from about **100 m** away.
- A 2024 study found that **86%** of documented tiger interactions ended without physical contact, showing how effective the bear's defensive display can be while still acknowledging tiger lethality.
- In Sri Lankan telemetry, adult male home ranges averaged only **3.8 km²**, among the smallest reported for bears, illustrating how strongly resource distribution changes ranging behavior.

### Concise site-ready summary
A shaggy South Asian bear specialized for termites but armed for a violent close-range defense. A prime male around 120 kg combines long excavation claws, large canines, powerful forequarters and an unusually aggressive countercharge strategy that can deter even tigers. It is not a large-prey hunting specialist, and its digging morphology limits pursuit and tree escape, but opponents entering grappling range face a dangerous ursid built to stand its ground.

### Narrative profile
The sloth bear is a useful warning against equating diet with combat capability. Its skull and limbs are heavily modified for insects, yet the animal retains the mass, canines and forelimb power of a medium-sized bear. The same claws that rip open termite architecture become damaging striking tools when the bear is cornered. Its shaggy coat is not armor, but field observations suggest that it can complicate a predator's bite, especially around the neck.

Its most remarkable combat adaptation is behavioral. Sloth bears coexist with tigers without possessing the climbing escape system of Asiatic black or sun bears. The 2024 tiger-interaction analysis supports a different solution: when surprised at close range, the bear often rears and countercharges. Most documented encounters ended before contact. That does not make a sloth bear stronger than a tiger; four bears in the dataset were killed. It does mean that Ferocity and close-range deterrence deserve far more weight than the species' insect diet would imply.

ABS normalization therefore places the sloth bear in a strong but not elite offensive tier. A 120 kg male is vastly above small carnivores in absolute force, yet clearly below a Siberian tiger's specialized predatory offense and below megafaunal durability. Its Special score comes from genuinely unusual feeding and defensive biology without double-counting claws across every category.

### Main conflicts and uncertainty
1. **Male mass:** modern sources overlap broadly but differ in upper bound, from roughly 140/145 kg to 175 kg in IBA's summary. The canonical 120 kg avoids treating the largest reported male as normal.
2. **Lifespan:** 15.8 y median managed-life expectancy and 25–28 y wild lifespan range are not equivalent statistics. ABS uses 26 y as a representative species lifespan field with moderate confidence, while retaining the metric conflict.
3. **Speed:** qualitative statements establish faster-than-human galloping and explosive charging but not a measured top speed. No conversion to `m/s` is justified.
4. **Bite:** large canines are well supported, but the site's 900 PSI is not. No PSI is proposed.
5. **Tiger defense:** the 43-interaction dataset is unusually useful behavioral evidence but is assembled from tourist videos and photographs, so percentages describe that sample rather than a complete population-level encounter rate.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Siberian Tiger:** the tiger remains far higher in Attack, Agility and specialized killing mechanics. The sloth bear's observed ability to deter tiger contact supports Ferocity and Defense, not tiger-equivalent offense. Four documented bears were killed in the 2024 sample.
- **Versus North American River Otter:** 120 kg versus 12 kg creates a large absolute power/durability gap even though the otter is dramatically more maneuverable in water. This prevents pound-for-pound leakage.
- **Versus Serval:** the serval is much faster/more agile, but a 14 kg cat cannot approach the bear's absolute raw force, durability or close-range damage capacity.
- **Versus Skunk:** skunk Special remains higher because directional chemical defense is a more unusual matchup-changing mechanism; the sloth bear dominates physical Attack and Defense.
- **Versus Sloth:** despite the similar English name, there is no combat-scale relationship. *Bradypus tridactylus* is a ~4.5 kg arboreal folivore; *Melursus ursinus* is a ~120 kg ursid. Large absolute gaps are mandatory.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Attack 65.0 deliberately stays well below Saltwater Crocodile 92.0 and Siberian Tiger 84.0. Defense 57.0 reflects robustness without turning shaggy fur into armor. Special 65.0 does not double-count claws or aggression already represented elsewhere.
