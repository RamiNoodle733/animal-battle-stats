# Bushmaster (*Lachesis muta*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Bushmaster (South American bushmaster, Atlantic bushmaster; surucucu in Brazil, shushupe in Peru, mapepire zanana in Trinidad)
- **Scientific name:** *Lachesis muta* (Linnaeus, 1766), family Viperidae, subfamily Crotalinae (pit vipers)
- **Exact animal represented:** the South American bushmaster of the Amazon basin, the Guianas, Trinidad and Brazil's Atlantic Forest, including the Atlantic Forest subspecies *L. m. rhombeata*. The Central American bushmasters (*L. stenophrys*, *L. melanocephala*) and the Chocoan bushmaster (*L. acrochorda*) are now separate species and are not used here.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult, **4.5 kg** and **225 cm** total length, sex unspecified. No reliable size difference between the sexes was verified in this pass.
- **Population notes:** adults usually measure 2–2.5 m, with 3 m "not too unusual". The largest reliably cited specimen is about 3.65 m; older claims of 4.27–4.5 m are unverified. Venom composition varies within the species and between the Amazonian and Atlantic Forest forms.

### Bite, fangs and venom
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-pressure measurement; the weapon is venom through hinged front fangs.
- No reliable fang-length measurement was located in this pass.
- **Yield:** **200–411 mg** per extraction (Brown 1973, repeated by Diniz-Sousa et al. 2020); one captive series fell from 233 mg to 64 mg as the snakes weakened in captivity.
- **Potency:** mouse LD50 about **1.5 mg/kg** intravenous, **1.6–6.2 mg/kg** intraperitoneal and **6.0 mg/kg** subcutaneous, weaker than many vipers per milligram.
- **Composition:** metalloproteinases, serine proteinases (including thrombin-like and kininogenase enzymes), phospholipase A2s, C-type lectins, L-amino acid oxidase and bradykinin-potentiating peptides; hyaluronidase and phospholipase B were found more recently.
- **Action:** pain, swelling, bruising, blistering and tissue death at the bite; incoagulable blood, bleeding and kidney failure; and a distinctive "vagal syndrome" of sweating, low blood pressure, slow heart rate, vomiting and diarrhea. A 2026 review of 44 confirmed bites (1950–2023) found vagal syndrome in 75%, severe envenoming in 47.7% and 9.1% mortality even with antivenom.

### Defensive structure
- Heavily keeled dorsal scales with bulbous tubercles give a rough, "pineapple" texture. They are not armor.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The primary weapon is a long-reach strike through hinged fangs delivering one of the largest venom loads of any viper, 200–411 mg. The venom destroys tissue, stops blood clotting and triggers a vagal reaction that drops heart rate and blood pressure. The secondary tool is its pair of facial heat pits, which let it aim at warm-blooded prey in total darkness.

### Offensive mechanics
Bushmasters wait coiled beside rodent trails, often for long periods, and strike passing mammals. A 2.25 m body gives a long strike reach. Per milligram the venom is weaker than many vipers', but the dose is large; a bite from a Central American relative overwhelmed an 80 kg man within 30 minutes (Ripa 2003, cited by Diniz-Sousa et al.).

### Defensive features and durability
A heavy, long body of about 4.5 kg with rough keeled scales, but no armor. Size and a vigorous tail-vibrating display are its main deterrents.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Mainly ground-dwelling and slow; it rests by day in a torpid state and hunts from ambush. Close-range strike control is good; general mobility is limited.

### Endurance
Low activity levels and a sit-and-wait lifestyle. It is sensitive to stress and rarely lives long in poor captive conditions. Not built for sustained exertion.

### Senses
Heat-sensing pit organs between eye and nostril, plus a vomeronasal (tongue-flicking) chemical sense used to follow prey and mate trails. An eyelid-like skin fold (epicanthic fold) protects the eyes when grappling with prey.

### Intelligence and tactics
Ambush site choice near rodent activity and patient waiting. Females guard their eggs. No evidence of flexible problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary except when mating. Eats mainly rodents and marsupials, including spiny rats, rice rats and agoutis, plus occasional birds, reptiles and frogs; recorded prey also include porcupines, squirrels, opossums and squirrel monkeys.

### Fighting and aggression
Reviews describe bushmasters as non-aggressive and rarely encountered; bites are rare. ADW repeats anecdotes of bushmasters following people for several meters, head held high, after being disturbed; these are not backed by controlled observations. Males are alert and ready for combat during the breeding season.

### Environmental strengths
- Primary and secondary lowland rainforest with dense cover and rodent runs.
- Night, when heat pits give it an advantage over prey.
- Warm, humid conditions of at least about 24 °C.

### Weaknesses
- Slow-moving, with limited escape or pursuit ability.
- No armor; a long body offers many targets to a large attacker.
- Venom acts over minutes to hours and is weaker per milligram than many vipers'.
- Poor tolerance of stress and cool or dry conditions.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rodents and other small to medium mammals that walk into strike range, especially at night.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized venomous snakes and mid-sized carnivores.
- **Bad:** large mammals that can crush or trample it before the venom acts, armored animals, and agile opponents that attack from outside its strike range in daylight.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Heat-Pit Night Strike** — paired facial pit organs sense the body heat of prey, letting the bushmaster aim a long-reach strike at rodents on dark forest trails.
2. **Vagal Shock Venom** — besides bleeding and tissue destruction, its venom often causes a "vagal syndrome" of slow heart rate, low blood pressure, sweating, vomiting and diarrhea, seen in 75% of confirmed human bites, and the yield can reach 411 mg.

### Unique traits
1. **Longest Viper on Earth** — adults average 2–2.5 m and the largest recorded reached about 3.65 m, making it the longest viper and the longest venomous snake in the Western Hemisphere.
2. **Egg-Guarding Pit Viper** — it is the only egg-laying pit viper in the Americas; females lay up to about 20 eggs and coil around them until they hatch, eating nothing during incubation.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The bushmaster lives in equatorial forests east of the Andes, in south-eastern Colombia, eastern Ecuador, Peru, northern Bolivia, eastern and southern Venezuela, Trinidad, Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana and much of northern Brazil, with a separate Atlantic Forest population in south-eastern Brazil. It occupies primary and secondary forest and nearby clearings, sheltering in fallen trees, burrows and rodent holes. It needs warm, wet conditions and is found only at low to moderate elevations.

### Diet and ecology
Mostly small mammals, especially rodents and marsupials, with occasional birds, reptiles and frogs. It is a sit-and-wait predator that hides beside places where rodents travel. Adults have few predators other than humans.

### Social structure
Solitary except during courtship and mating. Males follow females' scent trails; after mating, males may stay near females for some time.

### Reproduction and life history
Egg-laying, unlike all other pit vipers in the Americas. Clutches are about 5–20 eggs, incubated for about 60–79 days while the female guards them and leaves only to drink. Hatchlings are 30–50 cm long with bright orange or yellow tail tips that fade with age. Adult coloring comes at 1–2 years and sexual maturity at about 4 years.

### Conservation
Listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (2021 assessment, as reported by Wikipedia). Habitat destruction has reduced it in parts of its range, including Colombia and Brazil's Atlantic Forest.

### Special features
- Heat-sensing facial pits.
- Hinged fangs with venom yields up to about 411 mg.
- Rough, tubercular keeled scales.
- Tail vibration and a spiny tail tip that rustles loudly in leaf litter, though it has no rattle.
- Egg-laying with maternal nest guarding.

### Human interaction
Bites are rare because bushmasters live in remote forest and avoid people, but they are severe. A 2026 review found only 44 confirmed *Lachesis* bites in the literature from 1950 to 2023, with a 9.1% death rate despite antivenom. In Brazil, bites are treated with a combined anti-bothropic-lachetic antivenom and are often mistaken at first for lancehead bites. The genus name *Lachesis* refers to the Greek Fate who measured out each person's life, and *muta* ("mute") refers to its rattle-less tail.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is the longest viper in the world, with a record of about 3.65 m.
- Its Latin name *muta* means "mute": it shakes its tail like a rattlesnake but has no rattle.
- It is the only pit viper in the Americas that lays eggs, and the mother guards them.
- A single extraction can yield up to about 411 mg of venom.
- Bites often cause a "vagal syndrome" that slows the heartbeat.
- An eyelid-like skin fold protects its eyes when it grapples with prey.
- In Colombia it is called "verrugosa" for its warty-looking scales.

### Concise site-ready summary
The bushmaster is the world's longest viper, a 2–2.5 m South American pit viper that waits beside rodent trails and aims with heat-sensing pits. Its huge venom doses cause bleeding, tissue death and a heart-slowing vagal reaction. It is shy, slow and rarely bites, and like all snakes on this scale it is light for its length.

### Rich narrative profile
The bushmaster is the giant among vipers. Adults commonly stretch 2–2.5 m, and the longest reliably recorded reached about 3.65 m. It spends its days hidden in fallen trees or rodent burrows deep in lowland rainforest, and at night coils beside a trail used by spiny rats or agoutis, waiting. Heat-sensing pits between its eyes and nostrils let it aim at a warm body in total darkness.

When it strikes, it delivers one of the largest venom loads of any viper, commonly 200–400 mg. Per milligram the venom is weaker than that of many vipers, but the sheer dose causes bleeding, tissue death, kidney injury and a distinctive vagal syndrome in which the heart slows and blood pressure drops. Confirmed human bites are rare but frequently severe, and about one in eleven reported cases ended in death even with antivenom.

In battle terms the bushmaster's strengths are reach, venom volume and night senses. It is shy, rarely bites unless disturbed, and moves slowly, and its long body is still light by the standards of large animals, around 4.5 kg. A fast or heavy opponent that avoids the first strike has the advantage.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** Wikipedia's 3–5 kg is uncited and AnAge's 5.8 kg is an aggregate; 4.5 kg is a low-moderate confidence compromise.
- **Maximum length:** claims of 4.27–4.5 m are old and unverified; 3.65 m is used as the record.
- **Tail tips:** ADW says juveniles use their bright tail tips as lures, but Diniz-Sousa et al. report that tail luring has not been documented.
- **Aggression:** ADW's "follows people" anecdotes conflict with reviews describing a non-aggressive snake; the ratings follow the reviews.
- **Conservation:** ADW's older account gives a threatened listing, while the 2021 IUCN assessment reported by Wikipedia is Least Concern; the newer listing is used.
- **Fang length:** no reliable measurement located.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Gaboon Viper (7 kg):** the Gaboon viper is heavier with fangs about 5 cm long, the largest venom glands and extreme leaf-litter camouflage, so it keeps higher Weaponry and Special. The bushmaster gets slightly higher Attack for a far longer strike reach and heavier use of its strike, and higher Agility and Senses (heat pits).
- **Versus Rattlesnake (2.5 kg):** both are pit vipers with heat pits and tail vibration. The bushmaster is longer and heavier with larger doses, so it gets higher Size, Raw Power and Weaponry; the rattlesnake keeps higher Agility, Ferocity, Tactics and Special because of its rattle and readier defense. Attack is close, one point apart.
- **Versus Russell's Viper (1.3 kg):** Russell's viper venom is far more potent per milligram and its strike is more readily used, so it keeps higher Attack; the bushmaster gets higher Defense, Size and Senses.
- **Versus Green Anaconda (50 kg):** both are giant snakes of South American rainforests, but the anaconda is about ten times heavier and kills by constriction, so it stays far above in Raw Power, Defense and Attack.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "longest viper" is a length record, not a mass record; at about 4.5 kg the bushmaster stays in the low physical bands, and its rare but severe bites are not counted as extra Attack.
