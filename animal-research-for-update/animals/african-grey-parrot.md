# African Grey Parrot (*Psittacus erithacus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** African grey parrot (grey parrot, Congo African grey)
- **Scientific name:** *Psittacus erithacus* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the Congo grey parrot, *P. erithacus*, a light-grey, red-tailed parrot of the central and West African forest belt, including the island of Príncipe. The smaller, darker Timneh parrot of the far west (formerly *P. e. timneh*) is now usually treated as a separate species and is not the canonical animal. Captive-bred pets are the same species.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male, **400 g (0.4 kg)** and **33 cm** long, with a **46–52 cm** wingspan, matching Wikipedia's typical 400 g and the World Parrot Trust's 400 g and 33 cm; ADW gives up to 407 g.
- **Sex choice:** the sexes look almost identical; ADW calls the species "somewhat sexually dimorphic". A male is used for roster consistency only.
- **Population notes:** there are no well-separated size classes within *P. erithacus*. Wild birds are shy and hard to study, so much of the behavioural detail comes from captive research (Wikipedia; ADW).

- No armour. Defense is vigilance, flock alarm and contact calls, flight and, at close range, a strong hooked bill.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The weapon is a heavy, hooked black bill backed by a calculated bite of about 84 N, used to crack oil-palm nuts and other hard seeds. That is roughly half the sulphur-crested cockatoo's calculated 167 N and a fifth of the scarlet macaw's 444 N. Zygodactyl feet grip and manipulate but are not talons. The bill can crush a finger or damage a small animal, but a 400 g bird cannot deliver much absolute force.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Strong, direct flier that travels long distances each day between roosts and feeding areas (ADW), and a skilled climber that uses bill and feet together in the canopy. Wild flocks descend to forest clearings in waves, so the whole group is never on the ground at once, and react instantly to movement or sound (ADW). No flight-speed measurement exists.

### Endurance
Daily foraging flights over long distances from communal roosts, with seasonal movements out of the driest parts of the West African range (ADW). No telemetry was found, so endurance is scored as solid but not exceptional.

### Senses
Keen vigilance while feeding on the ground and at clearings; flocks shorten clearing visits when predators or people appear (Fastré et al. 2024). The parrot must pick sounds out of background noise, categorize and memorize them, and match its own output to a stored template in order to imitate (ADW, citing Bottoni et al.).

### Intelligence and tactics
This is the grey parrot's standout feature. Alex, studied for decades by Irene Pepperberg, learned to label about 50 objects, 7 colours and 5 shapes, and quantities up to six, and combined these to identify, request, refuse and categorize about 100 objects (ADW); Wikipedia adds a zero-like concept and simple addition. A grey parrot named Griffin beat 6- to 8-year-old children at a "shell game" of tracking hidden objects through swaps and matched adults on most conditions (Pailian et al. 2020). Grey parrots voluntarily passed tokens to partners so the partner could buy food, and helped affiliated birds more (Brucks and von Bayern 2020); prosocial choices rose when the birds took turns (Krasheninnikova et al. 2019). Among companion parrots they have the largest mimicry repertoires, and most can rearrange words and use them in appropriate contexts (Benedict et al. 2022).

### Sociality and hunting
Grey parrots eat fruit, nuts, seeds and some leaves, bark, flowers and insects; they do not hunt vertebrates. They roost in large, noisy single-species flocks, often in trees over water or on river islands, and split into smaller groups to forage by day. Young birds stay in family groups for years and must learn which plants are toxic, how to find water and how to avoid predators (ADW).

### Fighting and aggression
Young birds are appeasing toward adults; as they mature they become more aggressive with other greys, and competition for nest holes makes the species "extremely aggressive" in the breeding season (ADW). With predators they are shy and flee.

### Environmental strengths
- Moist lowland forest, forest edges, gallery forest and mangroves, up to 2,200 m in the east of the range (ADW; World Parrot Trust).
- Canopy and clearing environments, where vigilance, flock alarm and fast flight into cover work best.

### Weaknesses
- Only 400 g; far below any serious predator in absolute power.
- No protection beyond feathers.
- Partly a ground feeder, which exposes it at clearings.
- Wild populations are heavily depleted by trapping.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small animals that cannot follow it into the canopy, and opponents that can be out-thought or out-waited.
- **Mixed:** other large birds of similar size, where the bill and cunning offset lack of talons.
- **Bad:** raptors, palm-nut vultures, snakes, cats and any medium predator that can pin it.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Referential Speech** — grey parrots do not just copy sounds: Alex used English labels for objects, colours, shapes and quantities to request, refuse and categorize, and trained greys have tried to direct their trainers as a problem-solving strategy (ADW). In a matchup this is situational awareness and flock communication, not damage.
2. **Nutcracker Bill** — a heavy hooked bill with an 84 N calculated bite (61–96 N by finite-element modelling) cracks oil-palm nuts and hard seeds and can deliver a painful defensive bite at close range.

### Unique traits
1. **Shell-Game Memory** — a grey parrot outperformed 6- to 8-year-old children at tracking hidden objects through a series of swaps, matching adult humans on most test conditions.
2. **Red-Tailed Forest Voice** — wild greys roost in large, noisy flocks and have been recorded with repertoires of over 200 call types, including imitations of other forest birds and a bat.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The grey parrot lives in the forest belt of central and West Africa, from the eastern border of Côte d'Ivoire through Ghana, Nigeria, Cameroon and the Congo Basin to Kenya, plus the island of Príncipe (ADW; Wikipedia). It prefers moist lowland forest but also uses forest edges, clearings, gallery forest, mangroves, wooded savanna, farmland and gardens, up to 2,200 m (ADW; World Parrot Trust). Escaped or released birds have been seen in South Florida since at least 1984, without evidence of breeding (Wikipedia).

### Diet and ecology
Mainly fruit and nuts, especially oil-palm, supplemented with seeds, leaves, bark, flowers and some insects (ADW). The grey parrot is partly a ground feeder and regularly visits natural forest clearings: at one Congolese clearing an average of 40 birds arrived each day, perched and called in the surrounding trees, then landed to feed, drink and interact, with arrival times shaped by weather and season (Fastré et al. 2024). Natural predators include palm-nut vultures and several raptors (Wikipedia).

### Social structure
Highly social. Large single-species flocks roost together and break into smaller foraging groups by day. Each breeding pair holds its own tree within a loose colony. Young birds remain in family groups for years, socialize in "nursery trees", and learn food, water, predator and nest skills from adults (ADW).

### Reproduction and life history
Pairs form lifelong monogamous bonds at sexual maturity, at 3–5 years. Females lay 3–5 eggs in tree cavities, sometimes old woodpecker holes; incubation takes about 30 days while the male feeds the female, chicks leave the nest at about 12 weeks and reach independence at about 3 years (ADW).

### Conservation
Endangered on the IUCN Red List since 2016 (Wikipedia; World Parrot Trust), with a decreasing and unknown wild population. More than 359,000 grey parrots were traded internationally between 1994 and 2003, and about 21% of the wild population was harvested each year; in 2016 CITES moved the species to Appendix I (Wikipedia). In Ghana, surveys found greys in only 10 of 42 forest areas, and roosts that once held 700–1,200 birds held 18 in total (Wikipedia).

### Special features
- Heavy hooked bill with an 84 N calculated bite.
- Referential use of learned human labels in trained birds.
- Visual working memory rivalling adult humans in a shell-game task.
- Very large vocal repertoire, including imitations of other species in the wild.
- Flock vigilance and staggered descents to feeding clearings.

### Human interaction
One of the most popular pet parrots because of its speech; companion greys have the largest mimicry repertoires of any parrot species surveyed (Benedict et al. 2022). The intelligence that makes them popular also makes them hard to keep well: they need intensive social and behavioural enrichment or become distressed (Wikipedia). Trapping for the pet trade, together with timber felling, is the main cause of wild declines.

### Genuine fun facts
- Alex the grey parrot could label about 50 objects, 7 colours and 5 shapes, and count up to six.
- A grey parrot named Griffin beat 6- to 8-year-old children at a shell game and matched adults on most rounds.
- Grey parrots will hand a token to a partner so the partner can buy a treat, even with no reward for themselves.
- Two wild greys recorded at a roost in Congo used over 200 different calls, including nine imitations of other birds and one of a bat.
- A grey parrot can live 40–60 years in captivity.
- Flocks drop into forest clearings in waves so that the whole group is never on the ground at once.
- Wild greys once roosted in groups of 700–1,200 at Ghanaian sites that now hold only a handful.

### Concise site-ready summary
The African grey parrot is one of the smartest animals alive, a 400-gram forest parrot that can use words for objects, colours and numbers and track hidden objects better than young children. Its hooked bill bites hard for its size, but on the absolute scale it is a small, unarmoured bird that wins by staying alert and flying away.

### Rich narrative profile
The African grey parrot is a medium-sized, red-tailed parrot of the African rainforest, and it has become the model for what a bird's mind can do. Decades of work with Alex showed that a parrot could learn English labels for objects, colours, shapes and quantities and use them to ask for things, refuse them and sort them into categories. Later tests found a grey parrot matching adult humans at a shell game, and captive greys voluntarily helping partners obtain food.

In the wild, those abilities serve a cautious forest life. Greys roost in large, noisy flocks over water, fan out by day to feed on oil-palm nuts and fruit, and drop into forest clearings in careful waves while sentinels watch the edges. Young birds stay with their families for years, learning which foods are safe and how to avoid palm-nut vultures and hawks. That same intelligence made the species a prized pet, and trapping has driven it to Endangered status.

As a fighter, the grey parrot is limited by size. Its bill produces about 84 newtons, enough to crack palm nuts and hurt a hand, but it is a 400-gram bird without armour or talons. It rates at the very top of the roster for Intelligence and well above average for Agility, while its Attack and Defense sit among the small birds.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 333 g (Dunning, used by Harrison et al.) versus 400–407 g (Wikipedia, ADW, World Parrot Trust); 400 g is used as the typical adult.
- **Lifespan:** the 22.7-year wild average (n = 120) is repeated by ADW and Wikipedia, but the original study was not read; captive maxima of 60 years are exceptional.
- **Speed:** no measurement exists; 0.0 means unmeasured.
- **Cognition:** several landmark results come from single, intensively trained birds (Alex, Griffin); group studies show prosocial helping but also limits in understanding.
- **Taxonomy:** older sources that include the Timneh parrot as a subspecies mix in data from a smaller bird.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Cockatoo (0.8 kg; A21 D13 Ag86 I94 Sp76):** half the mass and half the calculated bite, so Attack 15.0 and Defense 11.0 sit below. Intelligence is placed one point higher (95.0 vs 94.0) for controlled evidence of referential labelling, number concepts and working memory; the cockatoo keeps higher Special for its bill-foot technical innovation.
- **Versus Raven (1.3 kg; A17 D18 St87 I95):** the raven is three times heavier and a far stronger long-distance flier, so it keeps higher Attack, Defense and Stamina. Intelligence ties at 95.0, a deliberate judgment: both have top-tier controlled evidence in different domains (planning and caching versus referential communication and working memory).
- **Versus Kea (0.9 kg; A22 D14 Ag85 St66 I92):** the kea is heavier and uses its bill offensively on sheep, so its Attack is higher; Stamina matches (66.0); the grey sits above in Intelligence.
- **Versus Macaw (1.2 kg; A24 Ag86 I86):** the macaw's 444 N bite and triple mass keep it well above in Attack.
- **Versus Crow (0.5 kg; A14 D9 I96):** similar mass; a heavier bite gives the grey slightly higher Attack; the crow keeps a one-point Intelligence edge for tool manufacture.
- **Anti-inflation check:** fame for talking is scored in Intelligence and Abilities only; Attack reflects the measured bite on a 400 g bird.
