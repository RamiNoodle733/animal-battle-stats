# Canary (*Serinus canaria*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Canary (wild form: Atlantic canary, also wild, island or common canary; domestic form: domestic canary)
- **Scientific name:** *Serinus canaria* (Linnaeus, 1758); the domestic form is sometimes written *Serinus canaria* forma *domestica*, which is not a formal subspecies.
- **Exact animal represented:** the living finch *Serinus canaria*, covering both the wild Atlantic canary of the Canary Islands, Azores and Madeira and the domestic canary bred from that wild stock over roughly 500 years of captivity.
- **Status:** Living; Least Concern (IUCN).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male common domestic canary, **0.02 kg**, **13.0 cm** total length. Males are used because they sing, hold breeding territories and fight other males, sometimes with bloody beak fights. The value also sits inside the wild weight range.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** in the wild, males are yellower on the face and underparts and females duller and greyer (Wikipedia). Domestic canaries are hard to sex by sight; most males sing loudly and most females do not.
- **Population notes:** wild birds weigh **8.4–24.3 g**, averaging about **15 g**, and are **10–12 cm** long. Domestic breeds range from about 10 cm (Fife Fancy) to about 23 cm (Lancashire), so type-bred giants and miniatures are excluded from the baseline.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The canary has almost no weapons. Its small conical bill husks seeds and can peck, and during the breeding season males defend territories fiercely, which can lead to beak fights and chases that draw blood (Wikipedia). Outside the breeding season, flocks settle into a hierarchy and most disputes end with threats: an open bill and raised wings, after which the weaker bird gives way.

### Offensive mechanics
Fights are pecks and chases between birds of the same size. Much of the contest is vocal: in a study using tiny backpack recorders, competing males overlapped their songs in aggressive "duels", and the leading male lengthened his songs to outlast the follower (Alcami et al. 2025).

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A small, quick finch that flies between shrubs and trees and forages on the ground and in low vegetation. It is agile in cover but has no special aerial skill.

### Endurance
Wild canaries are resident on their islands and are not long-distance migrants. They forage in flocks through the day. No endurance data were located.

### Senses
Good vision and very good hearing for a songbird; it discriminates and memorizes song sequences and responds to the alarm calls of other bird species (Wikipedia). One song breed, the Belgian Waterslager, has abnormal inner-ear hair cells that contribute to high-frequency hearing loss.

### Intelligence and tactics
The canary is a classic model for vocal learning. Young males learn their song in stages over about 300 days, adding learned syllables to an inborn pattern, and adults can change their songs from year to year. Adult canaries keep producing new neurons in the song-control nucleus HVC (Goldman and Nottebohm 1983), a discovery that helped overturn the idea that adult brains cannot add neurons. Canaries can copy other birds' sounds and occasionally human speech. There is little evidence of physical problem solving.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Gregarious outside the breeding season; pairs keep an individual distance even from each other. In the breeding season males hold small territories and defend them fiercely against other males.

### Predator defense
Hiding in foliage, flocking, alertness, alarm calls and quick flight.

### Environmental strengths
- Pine and laurel forest, orchards, copses, parks and gardens on its home islands.
- Dense shrubs and trees for cover and nesting.

### Weaknesses
- About 20 g; among the lightest vertebrates on the roster.
- No armor and no real weapon.
- Very sensitive to airborne toxins.
- Domestic birds lack wild survival skills.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** insects and other tiny animals.
- **Mixed:** other small songbirds.
- **Bad:** almost everything else, including raptors, snakes, cats and rats.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Two-Voice Song** — the canary sings with both sides of its syrinx, the left side producing low notes and the right side high notes, and can alternate them so fast that it produces special high-repetition "sexy" syllables that females prefer.
2. **Song Duel** — rival males overlap their songs in aggressive vocal exchanges, and the leader stretches his songs to outlast the follower, settling contests without always coming to blows.

### Unique traits
1. **Canary in the Coal Mine** — because canaries breathe rapidly and react to toxic gases quickly, miners carried them underground as carbon monoxide alarms from 1896 until Britain ended the practice in December 1986.
2. **Brain That Adds Neurons** — adult canaries keep producing new neurons in their song-control centre, a discovery that helped overturn the old belief that adult brains cannot add neurons.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The wild Atlantic canary is found only in Macaronesia in the eastern Atlantic: the Canary Islands, the Azores and Madeira. It lives in a wide variety of habitats from pine and laurel forests to sand dunes, most commonly in semi-open areas with small trees such as orchards and copses, and in parks and gardens, from sea level to above 1,500 m in the Canary Islands (Wikipedia). It has been introduced to Midway Atoll (since 1911); a Bermuda population founded in 1930 died out by the 1960s.

### Diet and ecology
It feeds in flocks on the ground or in low vegetation, mainly on seeds of weeds and grasses and on figs, plus other plant material and small insects (Wikipedia). Domestic canaries eat seed mixes plus greens, egg food and calcium sources.

### Social structure
Gregarious; wild canaries often nest in loose groups, each pair defending a small territory. Domestic canaries can live in aviary groups outside the breeding season, where a hierarchy forms and fights become rare.

### Reproduction and life history
The cup nest is built 1–6 m up in a tree or bush, well hidden in leaves. Clutches are 3–4 (sometimes 5) pale blue eggs, incubated for 13–14 days; chicks leave the nest after about 14–21 days, and pairs raise 2–3 broods a year (Wikipedia). Male canaries learn their full song over their first year.

### Conservation
The Atlantic canary is listed as **Least Concern** by the IUCN. Populations are estimated at 80,000–90,000 pairs in the Canary Islands, 30,000–60,000 pairs in the Azores and 4,000–5,000 pairs in Madeira. The domestic canary is one of the most widely kept cage birds in the world.

### Special features
- Complex learned song of 30–40 syllable types.
- Two-sided syrinx producing low and high notes independently.
- Adult neurogenesis in the song-control centre.
- Rapid breathing that makes it a sensitive detector of toxic gases.
- Hundreds of domestic colour, song and type breeds.

### Human interaction
Canaries were taken into captivity after the European conquest of the islands and became prized pets in 17th-century Europe. They were bred into song breeds such as the Harz Roller, Belgian Waterslager and Spanish Timbrado; colour breeds including the red factor canary; and type breeds from the tiny Fife Fancy to the large Lancashire. From 1896, after work by physiologist John Scott Haldane, miners in Britain, the United States and Canada used canaries to detect carbon monoxide, and Britain ended the practice in December 1986. Canaries remain a major laboratory animal for the study of song learning and the brain.

### Genuine fun facts
- The colour "canary yellow" is named after the domestic canary; wild canaries are mostly green-yellow with brown streaks.
- British coal mines used canaries as gas detectors until December 1986.
- Adult canaries grow new brain cells in their song-control centre.
- A canary can sing two notes at once, one from each side of its voice box.
- Rival male canaries hold song duels, trying to outlast each other's songs.
- The Harz Roller sings with its beak closed at 40 or more notes per second.
- A domestic canary in 1960s Leningrad learned to weave its owner's words into its song.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Canary is a 20 g finch from the Canary Islands, Azores and Madeira, bred for centuries into the world's best-known songbird pet. It is a master of learned song and a famous brain-science model, but in a fight it has only a tiny seed-cracking bill and is one of the lightest, most fragile animals on the roster.

### Rich narrative profile
The canary began as a small, streaky green-yellow finch in the laurel forests, pine woods and gardens of the Atlantic islands. Sailors and colonists brought it to Europe, where its song made it a treasured pet by the 1600s. Breeders then shaped it into hundreds of forms: bright yellow and red birds, crested and frilled birds, and song breeds trained to roll, bubble or ring.

Its voice is the canary's real specialty. A male learns his song over most of his first year, building 30–40 syllable types on an inborn framework, and uses both sides of his voice box independently to produce rapid two-note syllables that females find especially attractive. Rival males hold song duels, overlapping and stretching their songs. Research on canary brains showed that adult birds keep adding neurons to their song-control centre, changing how scientists think about the adult brain.

In an absolute matchup the canary has almost nothing to fight with. It weighs about 20 g, its bill is built for seeds, and it is so sensitive to toxic air that it served for nearly a century as a living gas alarm. Its survival strategy is to hide, flock and fly.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hummingbird (0.0038 kg; A5.0 D8.0 Ag98.0 St82.0 I61.0 Sp79.0):** the canary is about five times heavier with a seed-cracking bill, so Attack is slightly higher (6.0). Defense is set at 7.0, a point below the hummingbird's 8.0; that existing value looks generous for a 3.8 g bird and is flagged for the roster audit. The hummingbird stays far above on Agility, Stamina and Special because of its hovering and migration, and slightly above on Intelligence because of its spatial learning.
- **Versus Magpie (0.23 kg; A15.0 D12.0 Ag84.0 I91.0 Sp58.0):** more than ten times lighter with a far weaker bill and much less cognitive evidence, so lower on every rating.
- **Versus Rosy-faced Lovebird (0.055 kg; A8.0 D8.0 Ag85.0 I74.0):** lighter, with a finch bill instead of a strong parrot bill, so lower Attack; less acrobatic and less tested in cognition.
- **Anti-inflation check:** fame as a songbird and brain-science model raises Intelligence only modestly and does not raise any physical rating.
