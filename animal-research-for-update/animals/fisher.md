# Fisher (*Pekania pennanti*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Fisher (also pekan or fisher cat, although it is a mustelid, not a cat)
- **Scientific name:** *Pekania pennanti* (Erxleben, 1777); formerly *Martes pennanti*, the name Animal Diversity Web still uses
- **Exact animal represented:** the North American fisher of boreal and mixed forests across Canada and the northern United States, including the reintroduced Pacific populations of Washington, Oregon and California.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **5.0 kg**, inside the 3.5–6.0 kg male range (Wikipedia) and the 3.5–5.0 kg male range (ADW). The largest male on record weighed 9 kg and is not used as the baseline.
- **Sex choice:** strongly dimorphic. Males weigh 3.5–6.0 kg and measure 90–120 cm in total length; females weigh 2.0–2.5 kg and measure 75–95 cm (Wikipedia). Males are nearly twice the mass of females.
- **Population notes:** Washington describes fishers as 4.4–13 lb (2.0–5.9 kg) across both sexes. Coats are medium to dark brown with gold to silver frosting on the head and shoulders and black legs and tail.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The fisher is a generalist forest predator whose main prey are snowshoe hares and porcupines, supplemented with smaller mammals, birds, carrion, insects, nuts, berries and mushrooms. It is one of very few predators that routinely kills porcupines: observational studies show repeated biting attacks on the porcupine's face until it dies after about 25–30 minutes (Wikipedia, citing Powell). Occasionally it kills larger animals, including wild turkeys, raccoons, foxes, martens, otters, bobcats and Canada lynx. A Journal of Wildlife Management study documented **14 fisher kills of Canada lynx** in northern Maine from 1999 to 2011; the fishers attacked lynx bedded down in snowstorms with a quick, powerful bite to the neck. Local trappers and hunters in Quebec also describe the fisher as the main predator of the American marten (Journal of Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine 2017). For a 5 kg animal this is exceptional killing ability, but it depends on ambush and persistence rather than absolute strength.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Agile on the ground and in trees. Its reversible ankles let it descend trunks head-first, it can catch martens both on the ground and in trees, and it swims when needed. Its long, flexible body and short legs give it the typical mustelid ability to twist and change direction quickly.

### Endurance
Fishers are active by day and night and travel widely: hunting ranges are about 6.6 km² in summer and 14.1 km² in winter, up to 20 km² in poor habitat (Wikipedia). The porcupine attack itself shows persistence, lasting up to half an hour. Mustelid metabolism supports sustained activity through snowy winters.

### Senses
Keen smell and hearing typical of forest mustelids, used to find hares and porcupines in dense cover and snow. No measured sensory thresholds were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Its porcupine technique (circling and attacking the unprotected face repeatedly rather than grappling the quilled back) is a learned, specialized tactic. It also exploits bedded or snowbound prey, forages around fallen trees, and uses a mosaic of forest ages. This is sophisticated solitary predation.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Adults hold home ranges and come together only to mate. Mating in late winter is followed by about ten months of delayed implantation, so pregnancy spans almost a year.

### Fighting and aggression
Bold for its size: it will attack animals twice its weight when it has an advantage, and suburban fishers have been reported killing cats and small dogs. Adults are relatively safe, but predators recorded include bears, coyotes, eagles, lynx, bobcats, cougars and wolverines (Wikipedia); ADW lists hawks, red foxes, lynx and bobcats as predators of young fishers.

### Environmental strengths
- Continuous mature forest with fallen logs, large trees and deep cover.
- Snowy winters; crust on the snow improves its movement.
- Both ground and canopy.

### Weaknesses
- Small absolute mass (5 kg).
- No armor.
- Avoids open ground, clear cuts and farmland.
- Larger carnivores can kill it.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** hares, squirrels, martens, porcupines, and larger animals caught bedded or snowbound.
- **Mixed:** similar-size or somewhat larger mesocarnivores such as raccoons, foxes or small lynx in a straight fight.
- **Bad:** bobcats and cougars ready for it, coyotes, bears, wolverines, eagles, and any much larger or armored opponent.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Quill-Dodge Assault** — it circles a porcupine and bites its face again and again, avoiding the quilled back, until the porcupine dies after about 25–30 minutes. Against defended prey, this patience turns a dangerous opponent into a meal.
2. **Snowstorm Neck Bite** — in northern Maine, fishers killed at least 14 Canada lynx between 1999 and 2011 by attacking them bedded down in snowstorms with a quick, powerful neck bite. It shows how ambush can let a 5 kg animal kill a much larger cat.

### Unique traits
1. **Reversible Hind Feet** — its ankles rotate the hind paws almost 180°, so it can run down a tree trunk head-first like a squirrel.
2. **Year-Long Pregnancy** — after mating in late winter, the embryo waits about ten months before implanting, so the whole pregnancy lasts nearly a year.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Boreal and mixed deciduous-coniferous forest across Canada from Nova Scotia to British Columbia and north to Alaska, south into the northern United States, the Appalachians, the Great Lakes region, and the Pacific states. It prefers continuous forest with high canopy cover, large trees and fallen logs, and avoids clear cuts, grassland, farmland and heavily developed areas (WDFW). In the Sierra Nevada, fishers selected cooler, more forested and riparian areas during severe drought.

### Diet and ecology
Snowshoe hares and porcupines are the main prey, supplemented by squirrels, mice, birds, carrion, insects, nuts, berries and mushrooms. Where fishers were trapped out, porcupine numbers rose and damaged timber; when fishers returned, porcupine numbers fell again. A 17-year study found that fisher predation combined with severe winters cut adult porcupine survival by as much as 44% (Ecological Applications 2015).

### Social structure
Solitary and territorial. It overlaps with martens without strong separation in space or time in a 2019 Michigan camera-trap study, but it also kills martens.

### Reproduction and life history
Mating takes place from March to May. Implantation is delayed about ten months, active pregnancy begins around mid-February, and litters are born in spring in tree dens. Kits begin to climb at about eight weeks. Females need moderately large trees for dens.

### Conservation
Listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List. It recovered across much of the east after overtrapping and deforestation, but some western populations remain endangered: Washington lists it as a state endangered species and reintroduced 90 fishers to Olympic National Park in 2008–2010; the southern Sierra Nevada population is listed as federally endangered.

### Special features
- Reversible hind ankles and head-first tree descent.
- Coarse hairs between foot pads for grip on slippery surfaces.
- Long, flexible, muscular mustelid body.
- Specialized porcupine-killing behavior.
- Delayed implantation of the embryo.

### Human interaction
Once heavily trapped for its fur. It sometimes raids chicken coops, and in suburban areas it has been reported killing cats and small dogs. It is useful to foresters because it controls porcupines. In California, a 2013 study examined how illegal marijuana grow sites inside fisher home ranges affect fisher survival (an average of 5.3 grow sites per home range).

### Genuine fun facts
- Despite its name, it rarely eats fish; the name probably comes from "fitch" or the French "fichet", words for the European polecat.
- It can turn its hind feet almost backwards and climb down trees head-first.
- It is one of the few animals that regularly kills porcupines.
- Fishers killed at least 14 Canada lynx in one Maine study area between 1999 and 2011.
- Its pregnancy lasts almost a full year because the embryo waits about ten months before implanting.
- Ninety fishers from British Columbia were released in Olympic National Park between 2008 and 2010 to bring the species back.

### Concise site-ready summary
A long, dark forest mustelid, the fisher is one of the few predators that kills porcupines and has even ambushed Canada lynx in snowstorms. Agile in trees and bold for its size, it is still a 5 kg animal, strong for its weight but small on the absolute scale.

### Rich narrative profile
The fisher is a forest hunter built for persistence. A large male weighs about 5 kg and stretches over a metre with its tail, a low, muscular mustelid that runs along logs, climbs trunks and turns its hind feet backwards to come down head-first. It hunts snowshoe hares, squirrels and mice, and it is famous for tackling porcupines: circling, biting at the face again and again, and finishing the fight after up to half an hour.

Its boldness goes further. In northern Maine, biologists documented fishers killing more than a dozen Canada lynx, attacking them where they lay bedded in snowstorms with a quick bite to the neck. Fishers also kill martens, raccoons and occasionally foxes and bobcats. These feats rely on ambush, timing and relentless attack rather than raw power.

On the absolute scale the fisher remains a small predator. Its 5 kg frame sits well below badgers and honey badgers in mass, and it has no armor. In a straight fight against an alert, larger carnivore, it is at a disadvantage; against animals it can surprise or outlast, it is far more dangerous than its size suggests.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Length:** sources report total length for males and head-body length for both sexes combined; the canonical 105.0 cm is total length and is flagged.
- **Porcupine kill method:** field observation describes repeated face attacks over about 25–30 minutes; the popular story of flipping the porcupine and "scooping out its belly" is folklore repeated in local accounts and is not treated as fact.
- **Lynx kills:** the Maine study involved Canada lynx, a much smaller species than the roster's Eurasian lynx, attacked while bedded in snowstorms; it does not show fishers beating alert lynx in open fights.
- **Predators of adults:** ADW says adults are generally safe, while Wikipedia and California studies list bobcats, cougars and others; both are kept.
- **Speed, height and bite:** no measurements; all 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Badger (13 kg; A38 D39 Ag52 St59 I50 Sp46):** the badger is more than twice as heavy and far sturdier, so it keeps higher Attack and much higher Defense; the fisher is far more agile (86.0), more enduring (70.0) and more specialized (Special 68.0).
- **Versus Wolverine and Stoat:** the live wolverine and stoat values are legacy placeholders (for example wolverine Attack 1.6, stoat Attack 2.7) with no research reports, so they are not used as anchors. A researched wolverine (about 18 kg) should sit well above the fisher in Attack and Defense, and a researched stoat (about 0.25 kg) far below it.
- **Versus Honey Badger (14 kg; A42 D45 Sp80):** the honey badger's mass, loose tough skin and extreme boldness keep it above the fisher in Attack and Defense.
- **Versus Raccoon (6.5 kg; A30 D29) and House Cat (5 kg; A24 D16):** similar weights. The fisher's proven killing of porcupines and larger mesocarnivores lifts its Attack above both, while the raccoon's heavier build keeps its Defense higher.
- **Versus Lynx (Eurasian, 24 kg; A48):** the Maine lynx kills involved the much smaller Canada lynx caught bedded; against the roster's 24 kg Eurasian lynx the fisher would be heavily outmatched, so no Attack inflation is applied.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "kills lynx and porcupines" is expressed in Ferocity (74.0), Tactics (64.0) and Special (68.0), while Attack stays tied to a 5 kg body.
