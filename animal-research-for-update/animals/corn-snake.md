# Corn Snake (*Pantherophis guttatus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Corn Snake (red corn snake)
- **Scientific name:** *Pantherophis guttatus* (Linnaeus, 1766), family Colubridae. Formerly placed in *Elaphe* as *Elaphe guttata*, a name still found in older literature. The Great Plains rat snake (*P. emoryi*), once treated as a subspecies, is now a separate species.
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild corn snake of the southeastern United States. Captive colour morphs are bred from wild stock and are not used as the combat form.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **0.5 kg** and **120.0 cm total length**, representing a well-grown wild adult rather than a record animal.
- **Sex choice:** Animal Diversity Web states that males are larger than females in this species.
- **Population variation:** total length ranges from 61 to 182 cm. Florida snakes, which eat more reptiles and amphibians, tend to be smaller, and the Florida Keys form is slimmer. Captive snakes are often heavier than wild ones.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The corn snake is a constrictor. It bites to get a grip, then quickly coils around the prey and squeezes until it is subdued; small prey may be swallowed alive. Rodents make up over half of the diet in most areas, along with moles, birds and their eggs, lizards and frogs. A study of kingsnakes and ratsnakes, including corn snakes, found similar pulling strength in all species but significantly higher constriction pressures in kingsnakes, so the corn snake is a capable but not exceptional constrictor. Its muscles sit at lengths that favour force as the coil tightens.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It is a versatile mover: it prowls through rodent burrows, crawls through fields and climbs trees, cliffs and buildings. Experiments on perches of different diameters and slopes showed it grips branches with a concertina-like movement, stopping and gripping as it climbs, and slides while braking on the way down. On steep, wide perches it could not climb at all.

### Endurance
It brumates in winter in colder parts of its range and eats every few days in the active season. It is not built for sustained high-output effort.

### Senses
Tongue-flicking chemical senses are the main way it finds prey, with vision secondary. It has no heat-sensing pits, which helps separate it from the venomous copperhead it is sometimes confused with.

### Intelligence and tactics
In a spatial learning experiment, 17 young corn snakes learned to find the one open shelter in an eight-hole arena, cutting their time and distance to the goal over 16 trials. It uses burrows, bark and buildings as hunting grounds and refuges.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. It is mainly nocturnal and secretive, spending much of its time underground in rodent burrows or under loose bark.

### Fighting and aggression
When threatened it vibrates its tail, which can sound like a rattle in dry leaves, and releases a foul-smelling musk. It can strike, and rat snakes can strike across one third to one half of their body length. Captive corn snakes are known for their calm temperament.

### Environmental strengths
- Overgrown fields, forest openings and pine flatwoods with rodent burrows.
- Trees and old buildings, where it can climb to reach nests.
- Night-time hunting underground.

### Weaknesses
- Small, slender body with little absolute power.
- No armor or venom.
- Hunted by foxes, opossums, skunks, bobcats, weasels and hawks.
- Constriction weaker than that of kingsnakes of similar size.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** mice, voles, small rats, nestling birds and small lizards.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized snakes and lizards.
- **Bad:** kingsnakes, birds of prey, carnivorous mammals and anything much larger than itself.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Branch-Grip Climb** — it climbs trees, cliffs and buildings by gripping branches in a stop-and-grip concertina movement, reaching nests and escaping ground predators.
2. **Musk and Rattle Bluff** — when threatened it vibrates its tail against dry leaves, making a rattle-like buzz, and releases a foul-smelling musk that can put off a predator.

### Unique traits
1. **Maze-Learning Snake** — young corn snakes learned to find a hidden open shelter in an eight-hole arena within 16 trials, evidence that snakes can learn spatial tasks well.
2. **Checkerboard Belly** — its belly is patterned with alternating black and white marks like kernels of Indian corn, one explanation for its name.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The corn snake lives in the southeastern United States from New Jersey to the Florida Keys and west to Louisiana, in overgrown fields, forest openings, pine and palmetto flatwoods, rocky areas and abandoned farms and buildings, from sea level to about 1,800 m. In colder areas it brumates through the winter; on the warmer coast it shelters in rock crevices and logs during cold spells.

### Diet and ecology
Over half the diet is rodents, such as hispid cotton rats and white-footed mice, plus other small mammals like moles. It also climbs to take birds and eggs. Young snakes eat lizards, small snakes and frogs, and in Florida the diet is mainly reptiles and amphibians. It helps control rodent populations and is in turn eaten by foxes, opossums, skunks, bobcats, weasels and hawks.

### Social structure
Solitary and secretive. It is active mainly at night and spends much of the day under bark, in burrows or in old buildings.

### Reproduction and life history
Mating follows winter brumation. About a month later the female lays 12–24 leathery eggs in a warm, damp hidden place and leaves them. The young hatch after about 10 weeks at roughly 20–38 cm long (Smithsonian and ADW figures; Wikipedia gives about 13 cm). Maturity depends on size rather than age.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List. It is common across much of its range. Outside its range it is a proscribed pest in much of Australia, where authorities run eradication campaigns, and it is at risk of becoming invasive in Brazil.

### Special features
- Checkerboard black-and-white belly.
- Round pupils and no heat-sensing pits.
- Tail vibration and musk as defenses.
- Branch-gripping concertina climbing.
- Constriction to subdue prey.

### Human interaction
It is one of the most popular pet snakes, second only to the ball python, prized for its calm temperament, manageable size and easy care. Decades of breeding have produced a wide range of colour morphs. In the wild it is often mistaken for the venomous copperhead, but its brighter colours, slim head and round pupils tell them apart.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its belly pattern looks like the kernels of Indian corn, which may be how it got its name.
- The oldest known corn snake lived 32 years and 3 months in captivity.
- When scared it shakes its tail against dry leaves, buzzing like a rattlesnake.
- It can climb trees to raid bird nests.
- Young corn snakes learned a shelter-finding maze in just 16 trials.
- It is so popular as a pet that it is a banned invasive pest in parts of Australia.

### Concise site-ready summary
The corn snake is a slender, brightly patterned rat snake from the southeastern United States. A 0.5 kg adult male climbs trees, prowls rodent burrows at night and kills mice and rats by constriction, but it has no venom or armor and is far too small to threaten large animals. When cornered it bluffs with a buzzing tail and foul musk.

### Rich narrative profile
The corn snake is a quiet specialist in small places. It spends much of its life underground, sliding through the tunnels of mice and cotton rats, or tucked under loose bark and in the walls of old barns. At night it hunts by scent, flicking its tongue to follow a rodent's trail, then bites, wraps its coils around the prey and squeezes. It is just as happy in a tree, gripping branches in a stop-and-go climb to reach a bird's nest.

Its weapons stop there. Kingsnakes, which often eat other snakes, squeeze harder than corn snakes of similar size, and the corn snake has no venom and no armor. When threatened it tries to look more dangerous than it is: it shakes its tail against dry leaves to make a rattling buzz and releases a foul musk. Foxes, bobcats, weasels, opossums and hawks all eat corn snakes regardless.

In ABS terms the corn snake has low Attack and Defense, solid Agility from its climbing and burrowing, and modest Stamina. Its Intelligence is decent for a snake, supported by experiments in which young corn snakes quickly learned to find a hidden shelter. Its Special score reflects constriction, climbing and its rattle-and-musk bluff rather than any rare weapon.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Rattlesnake (2.5 kg; 58.0 / 32.0 / 72.0 / 45.0 / 43.0 / 94.0):** the rattlesnake is five times heavier and venomous, so it is far above the corn snake in Attack, Defense and Special. The corn snake's rattle is only an imitation, counted as a small part of its Special.
