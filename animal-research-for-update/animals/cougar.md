# Cougar Research Overhaul

## Research status

- **ABS roster name:** Cougar
- **Canonical taxon:** *Puma concolor* (Linnaeus, 1771)
- **Living/extinct:** Living
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, 70.0 kg, representing a robust North American male without using record-sized individuals
- **Research confidence:** high for taxonomy, sex dimorphism, broad dimensions, predation ecology, jumping and behavior; moderate for canonical mass/length because geography strongly affects size; low for a standardized maximum sprint and bite pressure

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

The site schema identifies Cougar as *Puma concolor*. Cougar, puma, mountain lion and catamount are common names for the same species. The species spans an exceptionally broad portion of the Americas and shows substantial geographic size variation.

A mature **male** is the canonical combat specimen because males are materially larger and are the primary territorial fighting sex. Animal Diversity Web reports males at 36-120 kg and 102-154 cm head-body length, females at 29-64 kg and 86-131 cm. Yellowstone NPS gives adult males 145-170 lb (65.8-77.1 kg) and females 85-120 lb (38.6-54.4 kg). Bighorn Canyon NPS gives adult males 120-200 lb. A canonical 70.0 kg male is therefore a defensible robust adult without treating exceptional 90-120 kg animals as normal.

Population variation matters. Cougars toward higher latitudes are generally larger than many tropical populations, and the Florida panther population is smaller than large northern males. Scores therefore describe the stated 70 kg specimen, not every puma across its range.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass

- ADW male range: **36-120 kg**, female 29-64 kg.
- Yellowstone adult males: **145-170 lb (65.8-77.1 kg)**.
- Oregon Caves NPS gives an average male around **137 lb (62.1 kg)**.

**Canonical `weight_kg`: 70.0.** This sits near the middle of Yellowstone's adult-male range and below exceptional species-wide maxima.

### Height

NPS accounts commonly place large adults near **30 in / 76 cm at the shoulder**. Because clean population-specific male means are less consistently reported than mass, use **70.0 cm** as a representative adult-male shoulder height with moderate confidence.

### Length

Yellowstone reports **6.5-7.5 ft (198-229 cm) including tail** for adults. ADW separately gives male head-body length of 102-154 cm and male tail length 68-96 cm. A representative total length around 220 cm is appropriate for a robust male.

**Canonical `length_cm`: 220.0**, explicitly total nose-to-tail length.

### Speed

Many tertiary sources repeat 40-50 mph values, but this run did not locate a transparent instrumented, species-specific maximum-speed trial suitable for a factual canonical field. The cougar is clearly explosive over short distances, but a repeated internet maximum is not equivalent to measured evidence.

**Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0 pending stronger maximum-speed methodology.** This is an unresolved-data value, not a claim that the animal cannot run quickly.

### Lifespan

Yellowstone reports average male lifespan **8-10 years** and female lifespan 12-14 years. Golden Spike NPS gives 8-13 years in the wild more generally. For the canonical male:

**Canonical `lifespan_years`: 10.0.**

### Bite force

No defensible standardized in-vivo **PSI** measurement was located. Cougar cranial morphology, powerful jaw/neck musculature and suffocating neck-bite behavior are well documented, but force cannot be converted to pressure without defensible contact area.

**Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Legacy values such as 750 PSI remain unsupported unless a transparent measurement method is produced.

### Weapons and mechanical reach

The cougar has heavy compressed canines, carnassials, five retractile claws on each forepaw and four on each hind paw. Powerful forelimbs and neck/jaw musculature combine with the bite during grappling. NPS describes a close stalk to roughly 30 ft or less followed by a leap onto the prey's back and a suffocating neck bite. The hind limbs are disproportionately important for acceleration, jumping and pounce mechanics.

### Jumping

Rocky Mountain NPS reports jumps up to **18 ft (5.5 m) vertically** and **40 ft (12.2 m) horizontally**. These are institutional field-natural-history figures rather than controlled laboratory maxima, so they are used as strong capability evidence rather than as precision biomechanical limits.

### Defensive structures

Cougars have ordinary felid fur, skin, muscle and skeleton, with no armor, shell or unusually thick hide. Their defense is dominated by evasiveness, terrain use, balance and avoidance of damaging exchanges.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons

The primary weapon system is **forelimb grapple plus canine neck bite**. The cat closes from concealment, explodes into a pounce, anchors with claws and forelimbs, and uses powerful neck/jaw musculature to control the head/neck region. Secondary damage comes from clawing, raking, biting during a scramble and the impact of a body-mass pounce.

### Offensive mechanics

Cougars are ambush predators, not frontal pursuit specialists. Rocky Mountain NPS describes stalking through cover to 30 ft or less before the attack. This shortens exposure time, preserves energy and maximizes surprise. Cougars routinely kill deer-sized ungulates. A Sonora GPS/scat study found white-tailed deer at 67% of identified puma kill sites, and another Sonoran study documented bighorn sheep as a major prey item. USGS GPS work estimated adult males taking a large mammal about every 7.8 days in the sampled population.

This does not mean a 70 kg cougar should be scored as though it has the absolute damage of a lion, tiger, large bear or megafaunal herbivore. Its lethality is produced by efficient close-range mechanics and target selection, not extraordinary absolute mass.

### Defense and durability

A cougar is muscular and can survive conspecific conflict, but it has no armor. Yellowstone documents adult cougars being killed by wolves, and bears/wolves frequently displace them from kills. The animal's best defense is avoiding clean hits, disengaging, climbing or using broken terrain. Defense therefore remains much lower than Attack or Agility.

### Locomotion and maneuverability

Long hind limbs, flexible felid spine, strong paws, balance and a long tail produce excellent short-range acceleration and terrain control. Cougars climb, negotiate rocks and slopes, jump vertically and horizontally, and can reverse or disengage quickly. Agility is scored very high on this direct functional evidence without substituting an unverified top-speed number.

### Endurance and stamina

Cougars are active year-round and patrol large home ranges. NPS reports male territories commonly 100+ square miles and in some systems up to several hundred square miles. However, their hunting mode is stalk-and-burst rather than sustained cursorial pursuit. Stamina is therefore solid but not elite relative to wolves, African wild dogs or extreme migratory/endurance specialists.

### Senses

Low-light vision and hearing are major hunting assets. Bryce Canyon NPS specifically describes large eyes with rod-rich retinas supporting low-light detail and sensitive high-frequency hearing, while smell is comparatively less emphasized than in canids. These senses are highly useful for detecting prey and timing an ambush but are not exotic modalities such as echolocation or electroreception.

### Intelligence and tactics

Cougars show strong spatial memory, route choice, prey selection, caching and flexible responses to risk. USGS work documents caching as an energy-management strategy, particularly with intermediate-sized prey. Experimental playback research showed pumas strongly altering feeding behavior in response to human voices, demonstrating rapid risk-sensitive behavioral adjustment. This supports above-average predator tactics without implying primate-level cognition.

### Hunting and predation behavior

The sequence is concealment, patient stalk, explosive closing movement, pounce/grapple and a neck/throat bite. Kills may be dragged and cached under vegetation, allowing repeated feeding. USGS research documented caching in **61.5% of 352 kills**, including 71.6% of black-tailed deer kills in that study.

### Intraspecific fighting

Adult males are territorial and do not normally share space peacefully with rival adult males. Yellowstone notes male cougars may kill other males within their territory. The Santa Monica Mountains NPS study likewise identifies intraspecific strife as an important mortality source. This supports meaningful ferocity and fighting experience, but avoidance and signaling remain important because severe injury is costly to a solitary hunter.

### Environmental strengths

- Forest, brush, canyon, rocky and mountainous terrain with stalking cover
- Slopes and vertical structure where jumping/climbing matter
- Low-light conditions
- Short engagement distances where burst acceleration and grappling can be exploited

### Environmental limitations

- Open terrain reduces concealment
- Long pursuit favors cursorial endurance specialists
- Deep water removes most terrestrial advantages
- No armor and no ranged, venomous, electric or chemical mechanism
- A detected frontal approach against much larger armed animals is unfavorable

### Major weaknesses

- 70 kg canonical mass creates a hard absolute-power ceiling against large bears, bovids and megafauna
- No true protective armor
- Killing system requires close contact and sustained control
- Burst-oriented predation is less suited to prolonged pursuit
- Solitary fighting provides no pack/coalition advantage
- Larger carnivores can steal kills or kill cougars

### Matchup archetypes

**Strong against:** smaller mammals, similarly sized animals vulnerable to a neck bite, prey that can be ambushed in broken terrain, and opponents with poor turning/climbing ability.

**Weak against:** much larger bears and big cats, heavily armed bovids, armored taxa, megafauna, large aquatic predators in water, and opponents whose venom/electricity can punish close grappling.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities

1. **Cliffspring Pounce** — Long hind limbs and powerful jumping let the cougar close gaps, attack from broken terrain and rapidly change elevation. NPS reports up to 18 ft vertical and 40 ft horizontal leaps; ABS treats these as institutional capability figures, not laboratory maxima.
2. **Silent Neck Lock** — A close stalk followed by forelimb anchoring and a suffocating neck bite concentrates the cougar's weapons on a vulnerable target region and makes surprise disproportionately valuable.

### Unique traits

1. **Ghost-Range Generalist** — *Puma concolor* occupies an enormous geographic and habitat range, reflecting exceptional behavioral and ecological flexibility for a large solitary cat.
2. **Cache-and-Return Hunter** — Cougars commonly conceal substantial kills and return over multiple feeding bouts, reducing waste and scavenger loss while managing the high energetic cost of large-prey hunting.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and geographic range

Cougars occupy forests, mountains, deserts, scrub, swamps and other habitats from Canada through much of Central and South America. They require adequate prey and sufficient cover or terrain structure for stalking. CatSG lists the species as **Least Concern** globally, while emphasizing regional fragmentation, conflict, hunting and prey depletion.

### Diet and ecology

Cougars are obligate carnivores and major predators of ungulates. Deer are especially important in many North American populations, with elk, bighorn sheep, peccaries, livestock and smaller mammals also taken depending on region. Their kills support scavenger communities, making the cougar an important ecological energy-transfer agent.

### Social structure

Primarily solitary outside mating and maternal care. Adult male ranges overlap female ranges but males compete strongly with other males. Scent marks, scrapes and other signals reduce unnecessary direct fights.

### Reproduction and life history

Breeding can occur year-round. Females raise kittens without male parental care. Young remain dependent long enough to learn hunting and then disperse. Adult male lifespan is often shorter than female lifespan in hunted or competitive populations.

### Conservation and human interaction

The species remains broadly distributed but was historically eliminated from much of eastern North America. Roads, habitat fragmentation, prey depletion, persecution and livestock conflict remain important pressures. CatSG lists the puma as Least Concern globally and CITES Appendix II. Human attacks are rare relative to the species' geographic overlap with people, and cougars commonly avoid humans.

### Adaptations

- long powerful hind limbs for burst acceleration and jumping
- retractile claws and strong forelimbs for grappling
- long tail for balance
- low-light vision and sensitive hearing
- ambush-oriented stalking behavior
- flexible habitat and prey use
- caching of large prey

### Genuine fun facts

- Cougar, puma, mountain lion and catamount all refer to *Puma concolor*.
- Cougars can purr but cannot produce the true roar characteristic of *Panthera* big cats.
- Rocky Mountain NPS reports jumps as high as 18 ft vertically and 40 ft horizontally.
- Yellowstone males average shorter lives than females, roughly 8-10 versus 12-14 years.
- Santa Monica Mountains NPS reports mule deer accounting for 87% of more than 700 analyzed kills in that local study.
- In one California study, pumas cached 61.5% of 352 documented kills.
- Human-voice playback experiments caused pumas to flee more often and cut feeding time by more than half, showing strong behavioral sensitivity to perceived human risk.

### Concise site-ready summary

The cougar is a 70 kg-class solitary ambush cat built around stealth, explosive jumping, forelimb grappling and a killing neck bite. Its greatest matchup advantages are agility, terrain control and attack efficiency rather than armor or brute megafaunal force. It routinely kills deer-sized prey, caches carcasses and adapts to environments from mountains and forests to deserts, but avoids prolonged frontal conflict with larger carnivores when possible.

### Narrative profile

A cougar wins by controlling the opening seconds. It uses cover and low light to erase distance, then converts powerful hind limbs into a sudden pounce. Forelimbs and retractile claws stabilize the target while the jaws seek the neck. This is a highly optimized predatory sequence, but it should not be confused with unlimited raw strength. At roughly 70 kg, the canonical male remains physically outscaled by large bears, the largest big cats, bovids and megafauna.

Its defensive strategy is similarly active rather than structural. A cougar has no armor. It survives through detection, positioning, balance, climbing, jumping and a willingness to disengage when a fight becomes uneconomical. That is why its Agility is elite while Defense is only low-to-moderate on the absolute roster scale.

Behavior adds another layer. Cougars select ambush sites, cache prey, return to kills, adjust activity around competitors and people, and defend territories. Those behaviors justify good Intelligence/Tactics without importing human-like cognition. Overall, the cougar is a dangerous medium-large predator whose battle profile is strongest when surprise and terrain let it apply a precise close-range kill system before a heavier opponent can impose a frontal contest.

### Evidence conflicts and uncertainty

- Species-wide mass ranges are much broader than population-specific adult-male ranges. The 70 kg canonical value intentionally represents a robust adult male, not the 120 kg extreme.
- Length sources mix head-body and nose-to-tail conventions. This report explicitly uses 220 cm **total** length while retaining separate ADW head-body/tail ranges in the evidence.
- Top-speed values are widely repeated but methodologically weak. `speed_mps` stays 0.0 pending defensible measurement.
- Bite force is biologically important, but force and pressure are not interchangeable. `bite_force_psi` stays 0.0.
- Jump figures come from NPS natural-history accounts and are treated as capability evidence, not precision laboratory records.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Caracal (17 kg, Attack 44.0):** Cougar must be materially higher in absolute Attack and Raw Power because it is roughly four times the canonical mass while retaining the same basic felid ambush toolkit. Attack 55.0 preserves that gap without treating mass as automatic lethality.
- **Cheetah (46 kg, Attack 41.0, Defense 24.0, Agility 97.0):** Cougar is heavier and more grappling-oriented, so Attack/Defense rise, while cheetah remains above it in instrumented speed/acceleration-derived Agility.
- **Clouded Leopard (22 kg, Attack 49.0, Agility 94.0):** Cougar has much greater absolute mass and power but lacks the clouded leopard's extreme arboreal/canine specialization. Cougar Agility 92.0 remains elite but does not erase that specialist distinction.
- **Chimpanzee (46 kg, Attack 46.0, Intelligence 96.0):** Cougar is a more specialized natural killer and receives higher Attack, while chimpanzee remains vastly higher in cognition.
- **African Lion:** Cougar should remain clearly below the much larger lion in absolute Attack, Defense and Raw Power once the same adult-male standard is applied.
- **Anti-inflation check:** No score is raised because the cougar is famous or an apex predator. Its elite value is Agility, where direct anatomy/jump/terrain evidence supports it. Protection and Defense remain low because the animal has no armor.
