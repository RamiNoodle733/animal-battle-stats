# Rosy-faced Lovebird (*Agapornis roseicollis*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Rosy-faced Lovebird (also peach-faced or rosy-collared lovebird)
- **Scientific name:** *Agapornis roseicollis* (Vieillot, 1818)
- **Exact animal represented:** the living small African parrot *Agapornis roseicollis* of the dry south-west of Africa, in its wild green form. Pet lovebirds, including the many colour varieties, are the same species and are covered where relevant.
- **Status:** Living; Least Concern (IUCN).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **0.055 kg**, **15.0 cm** total length. Females are slightly heavier than males (ADW) and control courtship, which goes ahead only with their approval.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** the sexes look almost identical; females are slightly heavier and a little duller in colour, with slightly longer wings (ADW average wingspan 102.6 mm in females versus 99.6 mm in males, as reported there).
- **Population notes:** two subspecies: *A. r. roseicollis* in Namibia, north-western South Africa and south-western Botswana, and the brighter *A. r. catumbella* in south-western Angola. Feral populations live in and around Phoenix and Tucson, Arizona.

### Bill and bite force
- Short, deep, horn-coloured bill used to husk seeds rapidly with tongue and mandible (ADW).
- **Measured in-vivo bite force: 18.3 N** (Dickinson et al. 2022, *Journal of Zoology*, six live birds on a force plate, as summarized by Harrison et al. 2025). Force fell as gape widened from 2.5 to 10 mm. The study found lovebirds bite harder than expected for their body size compared with more than 70 other bird species.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Newtons are not converted to PSI without contact area.
- **Additional proposed field:** `bite_force_n = 18.3` (measured in vivo, as summarized by Harrison et al. 2025).

### Feet and grip
- Zygodactyl feet. Grip force peaks on 5 mm perches and pull force on 2.5 mm perches, falling sharply above 10 mm (Dickinson et al. 2022, *Journal of Experimental Biology*).

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Protection is flight, flocking and mobbing.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The bill is the only weapon. Six live lovebirds bit with up to about 18 N, which is strong for a 55 g bird; lovebirds, grey parrots and monk parakeets all bite harder than expected for their size compared with other birds (Dickinson et al. 2022). In captivity lovebirds can be aggressive, bond to one individual, may not get on with other people or pets, and should not be kept with smaller birds (Wikipedia). In absolute terms the bite is a pinch that can draw blood from a finger, not a threat to anything large.

### Offensive mechanics
The most striking offensive behavior is mobbing. When a predator approaches, lovebirds stand upright and squawk; if it comes closer they flap their wings wildly, squeak more shrilly and move towards it as if to attack; if it still does not back off, large groups attack it (ADW).

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A fast, agile flier, recorded at 58 km/h (ADW), that glides between wingbeats on long flights. On short distances it walks or sidles. It can climb vertical walls using feet and beak while beating its wings (ADW), and it can travel hanging beneath a narrow branch using only its beak, a gait called "beakiation" in which the beak carries forces of about 150% of body weight (Dickinson et al. 2024).

### Endurance
Lovebirds must drink several times a day to cope with their hot, dry habitat (ADW) and stay near permanent water. They make regular flights between water, food and roosts but are not long-distance travelers. Stamina is scored moderate.

### Senses
Normal parrot vision and hearing. No special sense is documented.

### Intelligence and tactics
In a 2024 preprint, 28 lovebirds learned to link symbols to food quantities and then inferred the value of new symbols, following Weber's law in their accuracy (Wang et al.). Wikipedia repeats a claim that the species passed the mirror mark test, but its source is not a peer-reviewed study, so it is not used in scoring.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Pairs form very quickly, sometimes at only two months old, and bond strongly; they sit side by side, preen each other and roost together. Males court with head bobbing, feeding and sideways "sidling", and switch sides if the female looks aggressive (ADW). In cold weather birds huddle in groups of 4 or 5.

### Environmental strengths
- Dry woodland, semi-desert and rocky country near permanent water.
- Cliffs, rock crevices and communal weaver nests for shelter.
- Towns and farms with water and nest sites.

### Weaknesses
- About 55 g in absolute mass.
- Tied to water sources; must drink several times a day.
- No armor.
- Bite far too weak to injure large animals.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** insects and very small animals; small predators that can be driven off by a mobbing flock.
- **Mixed:** other small birds of similar size.
- **Bad:** raptors, snakes, cats and anything larger that can catch a single bird.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Mobbing Squadron** — lovebirds confront a predator by standing tall, squawking, flapping and advancing, and if it does not retreat, large groups attack it together.
2. **Beakiation** — a lovebird can travel hanging beneath a thin branch using only its beak, which carries about 1.5 times its body weight with each swing, and it can climb vertical walls with feet and beak.

### Unique traits
1. **Rump-Feather Nest Hauler** — the female cuts strips of bark and plant material, tucks them into her rump feathers and flies them back to the nest.
2. **Water-Tied Desert Parrot** — it lives in dry woodland and semi-desert of south-west Africa but must drink several times a day, so flocks gather in hundreds at pools.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The rosy-faced lovebird lives in dry, open country in south-western Africa, from south-western Angola across most of Namibia to the lower Orange River valley in north-western South Africa and around Lake Ngami in Botswana. It uses dry woodland, sub-desert steppe, savanna with sparse trees, and wooded belts along rivers and canyons, up to about 1,500–1,600 m, always near permanent water (WPT; ADW). Feral birds live in Phoenix and Tucson, Arizona, where they have been seen perching on air-conditioner vents in extreme heat.

### Diet and ecology
It eats mainly seeds, especially of grasses, *Albizia* and *Acacia*, plus buds, leaves and flowers, and will take maize, sunflower and millet crops. Seeds are picked from plants or the ground and husked rapidly with tongue and bill (ADW; WPT). At night lovebirds roost in the nests of white-browed sparrow-weavers and sociable weavers.

### Social structure
Very social. Groups of 5–20 are typical, with gatherings of 100 or several hundred where grass seeds ripen or at water. Pairs bond strongly and huddle together in cold weather.

### Reproduction and life history
Nests are cup-shaped, built in rock crevices, in chambers of the huge communal nests of sociable weavers, or in buildings and bridges. Clutches are 4–6 eggs, incubated for about 23 days; chicks fledge at about 43 days and young start forming pairs at about 2 months (ADW; WPT).

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** by the IUCN. Heavy trapping for the cage-bird trade caused historical declines in southern Angola, but new water points and buildings have helped it spread in other areas, including towards Lake Ngami (WPT; Wikipedia).

### Special features
- Measured bite force of about 18 N, high for its size.
- Beak-driven hanging locomotion ("beakiation") and wall climbing.
- Females carry nesting material tucked into their rump feathers.
- Coordinated group mobbing of predators.
- Dependence on frequent drinking.

### Human interaction
It is one of the most common pet parrots because it is small and easy to breed, and aviculturists have produced more colour mutations than in any other lovebird. Pet lovebirds can be aggressive and possessive, often bonding to one person or bird. Escaped birds have founded feral populations in Arizona and Hawaii. Lovebirds have also become a laboratory model for studies of parrot biting, gripping and climbing.

### Genuine fun facts
- Female lovebirds tuck strips of bark into their rump feathers and fly them back to the nest.
- A lovebird can swing along under a branch using only its beak, a gait scientists named "beakiation".
- Six lovebirds tested on a force plate bit with up to about 18 N, far more than most birds their size.
- Feral lovebirds in Phoenix, Arizona have been seen cooling off on air-conditioner vents.
- Lovebirds often sleep in the giant communal nests of sociable weavers.
- In a mobbing attack, a whole group of lovebirds will fly at a predator that refuses to leave.
- Pairs can form when the birds are only about two months old.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Rosy-faced Lovebird is a 55 g parrot from the dry south-west of Africa, a fast, acrobatic flier that can even swing along branches using only its beak. It bites hard for its size and flocks gang up to mob predators, but in an absolute fight it is one of the smallest and most fragile animals on the roster.

### Rich narrative profile
The rosy-faced lovebird is a small green parrot with a pink face and a blue rump that lives in the dry woodland and semi-desert of Namibia, Angola and South Africa. It must drink several times a day, so flocks gather in hundreds around pools and rivers, then scatter to feed on grass seeds and acacia pods. At night lovebirds sleep in rock crevices or inside the giant thatched nests built by sociable weavers.

For its size it is physically impressive. Force-plate tests show it bites harder than expected for a bird of 55 g, and biomechanics studies found it can climb walls with feet and beak and even move hand-over-hand beneath a thin branch using only its beak, taking about one and a half times its body weight on the bill with each swing. It is also bold: when a predator threatens, lovebirds squawk, flap and advance, and whole groups will attack a predator that refuses to leave.

In an absolute matchup, though, it weighs about as much as a large egg. Its bite can hurt a finger but cannot harm a large animal, and it has no armor. Its best weapons are speed, agility and the courage of its flock.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hummingbird (0.0038 kg; A5.0 D8.0 Ag98.0 St82.0 I61.0 Sp79.0):** the lovebird is about 14 times heavier with a measured parrot bite, so Attack is higher (8.0). The hummingbird's hovering, torpor and migration keep it far above on Agility, Stamina and Special. Defense is tied at 8.0; the hummingbird's value looks generous for 3.8 g.
- **Versus Cockatoo (0.8 kg; A21.0 D13.0 Ag86.0 I94.0 Sp76.0):** about 15 times lighter with a bite about one ninth as strong (18 N versus 167 N calculated), so far lower Attack; similar Agility; much less cognitive evidence.
- **Versus Sun Conure (0.12 kg; A11.0 D9.0):** about half the mass, so slightly lower Attack and Defense; bolder mobbing gives higher Ferocity.
- **Versus Canary (0.02 kg; A6.0):** heavier with a much stronger parrot bite.
- **Anti-inflation check:** a bite that is strong for its size and brave mobbing are not scored as absolute damage; Attack stays in the negligible band.
