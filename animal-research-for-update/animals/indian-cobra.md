# Indian Cobra (*Naja naja*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Indian Cobra (Spectacled Cobra)
- **Scientific name:** *Naja naja* (Linnaeus, 1758), family Elapidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the spectacled cobra of the Indian subcontinent, not the monocled cobra (*N. kaouthia*) or other Asian cobras.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy representative adult, **2.0 kg** and **150 cm** total length, sex unspecified. This pass did not verify a consistent size difference between the sexes, so no sex-based modifier is applied.
- **Population variation:** Britannica gives a typical length of about 1.0–1.5 m, with some animals, especially in Sri Lanka, reaching roughly 2.1–2.2 m. Venom composition varies strongly between regions of India, which affects how well antivenom works but not body size.

### Bite, fangs and venom
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No defensible bite-pressure measurement exists; the danger is venom.
- Fangs are fixed front fangs typical of cobras. No reliable species-specific fang length was located.
- **Venom composition:** proteomic studies of Indian *N. naja* find three-finger toxins at **58–91%** and phospholipase A2 at **4–26%** of venom protein, depending on region. An eastern Indian sample was **63.8%** three-finger toxins and **11.4%** PLA2. Other families include cobra venom factor, Kunitz inhibitors, metalloproteinases, CRISPs, nerve growth factor and L-amino-acid oxidase.
- **Neurotoxins:** two postsynaptic neurotoxins (a short-chain and a long-chain α-elapitoxin) make up only about **1%** and **3%** of the venom, yet a 2022 study found they are the only components that cause neuromuscular paralysis.
- **Regional variation:** a 2021 PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases study found marked differences in venom proteomes and effects across India, and worrying differences in how well commercial antivenoms neutralize them.

### Defensive structure
- Smooth scales only; no armor. Defense depends on the hood display, retreat and the danger of the bite.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The main weapon is a front-fanged bite delivering venom dominated by three-finger toxins. Postsynaptic α-neurotoxins block the acetylcholine receptors of muscle, producing paralysis that can reach the breathing muscles. PLA2 enzymes and other components add further effects. The secondary weapon is the hood display: the cobra raises its forebody and spreads its neck ribs into a hood marked with the spectacle pattern, which warns off threats and gives an elevated platform for striking.

### Offensive mechanics
Against rodents, frogs and lizards a single envenomating bite is enough. Against a large opponent, the cobra's modest size and short fangs mean the outcome depends on landing a bite on unprotected skin and on the venom acting before the opponent can crush or tear the snake.

### Defensive features and durability
A 2 kg snake with no armor is physically fragile. It survives through warning, retreat into cover, and the deterrent of its bite.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Typical of an active terrestrial elapid: good body control, the ability to rear and hold the forebody up, and rapid forward strikes from the raised posture. No measured top speed exists.

### Endurance
An active forager in fields, scrub and forest edges, but an ectotherm without evidence for high sustained output.

### Senses
Normal elapid vision and tongue-flick chemoreception; no heat-sensing pits. No species-specific thresholds were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
The cobra uses a graded defense (hood display and hissing before striking) and exploits rodent-rich farmland near people. There is no evidence for broad problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Hunts rodents such as the black rat, plus frogs, birds, lizards and other snakes.

### Fighting and aggression
It usually displays and retreats rather than attacking, but it lives close to people across its range, so encounters and bites are frequent. It is one of India's "Big Four" snakes responsible for most snakebite envenoming.

### Environmental strengths
- Farmland, village edges, scrub and forest where rodents are abundant and cover is close.
- Warm lowland climates that keep an ectotherm active.
- Close-range fights where the raised hood lets it strike downward.

### Weaknesses
- Low body mass and no armor.
- Short fangs and delayed venom action against large opponents.
- Its paralysis-causing toxins are a small share of the venom, so dose matters.
- Mongooses have receptors that α-neurotoxins bind poorly, blunting its main weapon.
- King cobras prey on Indian cobras.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small and medium unarmored mammals and birds that come within striking range; animals without neurotoxin resistance.
- **Mixed:** other venomous snakes of similar size.
- **Bad:** mongooses (neurotoxin-resistant receptors plus agility), king cobras (snake-eating specialists, much larger), heavily armored animals, raptors and any large animal that can crush it quickly.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Receptor-Blocking Venom** — α-neurotoxins in a three-finger-toxin-rich venom block the acetylcholine receptors of muscle, causing paralysis that can stop breathing. They are only a few percent of the venom but are its paralysis-causing core.
2. **Spectacled Hood Display** — the cobra rears, spreads its hood and shows the spectacle mark, warning threats away and striking downward from the raised position if the warning fails.

### Unique traits
1. **Egg-Guarding Mother** — females lay about 12–20 eggs in a hollow tree or in the earth and guard them through incubation, leaving only to feed, until they hatch after roughly 50 days.
2. **Village-Edge Rat Hunter** — the cobra thrives in cultivated land and around villages where rats are common, which makes it both a pest controller and one of the "Big Four" snakes behind most snakebites in India.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The Indian cobra is found across the Indian subcontinent, including India, Sri Lanka and neighboring countries. Animal Diversity Web and other sources describe it in wild forest and cultivated land, with records from grassland, scrub, savanna-like country, mangroves and rainforest. It is common near villages and farmland.

### Diet and ecology
It feeds mainly on rodents such as the black rat, and also takes frogs, birds, lizards and other snakes. As a rat predator in farmland it plays a real role in controlling crop and grain pests. It is itself eaten by king cobras and hunted by mongooses.

### Social structure
Solitary outside breeding.

### Reproduction and life history
Oviparous. Females usually lay 12 to 20 eggs in a hollow tree or in the earth and guard them throughout incubation, leaving only to feed. Hatchlings emerge after about 50 days with functional venom.

### Conservation
Listed as **Least Concern** by the IUCN. It remains widespread, though it is killed in large numbers when found near homes and is still used by snake charmers in some areas.

### Special features
- Front fangs delivering a three-finger-toxin-dominated neurotoxic venom.
- Expandable hood with the spectacle-shaped mark on its back.
- A wide black band on the underside of the neck.
- Ability to rear and hold a large part of the body upright during threat displays.
- Strong regional variation in venom composition across India.

### Human interaction
India loses tens of thousands of people to snakebite each year; a nationally representative study estimated about 1.2 million deaths between 2000 and 2019, around 58,000 a year. The Indian cobra and Russell's viper cause most envenomations because they are found almost throughout the country. The cobra is central to Indian culture and folklore and is closely associated with snake charmers. Research showing that venoms from different regions respond differently to the same antivenom has pushed for better, pan-India treatments.

### Genuine fun facts
- The spectacle-shaped mark on the back of the hood gives the species its other name, the spectacled cobra.
- Its paralysis-causing neurotoxins make up only about 1% and 3% of the venom, yet they are the only components found to cause neuromuscular paralysis.
- Venom from different parts of India can differ so much that the same antivenom neutralizes some samples far better than others.
- A female guards her eggs for the whole incubation, leaving only to feed.
- Mongooses resist cobra neurotoxins because the toxins bind poorly to their muscle receptors.
- It is one of India's "Big Four" snakes, together with the common krait, Russell's viper and saw-scaled viper.
- A reference genome of the Indian cobra was published to map its full set of venom genes.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Indian cobra is the spectacled cobra of South Asia, famous for its hooded threat display and its neurotoxic venom. At around 1.5 m and 2 kg it is small and unarmored, but its bite blocks the nerve signals to muscles and can stop breathing. It thrives around farms and villages hunting rats, which makes it one of the snakes behind most of India's snakebites.

### Rich narrative profile
The Indian cobra's reputation is built on a single, well-known image: a snake rising from the ground with its hood spread and the spectacle mark showing. That display is a real combat tool. It warns animals and people away before any contact, and if the warning fails the cobra strikes forward and down from its raised posture.

Behind the display is a venom dominated by three-finger toxins. The paralysis comes from a small fraction of them, α-neurotoxins that jam the receptors muscles use to receive nerve signals. PLA2 enzymes and other proteins add more effects, and the exact recipe changes from one region of India to another, sometimes enough to weaken antivenom treatment. For a matchup, the key point is simple: a clean bite on an unarmored animal can paralyze something far larger than the cobra.

On the absolute scale the cobra's limits are just as clear. A 2 kg snake has little strength and no armor, its fangs are short, and venom takes time. A heavy animal that crushes it fast, a raptor that strikes from above, or a mongoose whose receptors resist α-neurotoxins can beat it. Bigger snake-eaters such as the king cobra hunt it.

Its success comes from living where the food is. Farmland and village edges are full of rats, and the cobra follows them, which is also why people meet it so often. That closeness to people explains both its cultural fame and its large share of snakebite cases.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus King Cobra (8 kg):** the king cobra is about four times heavier, much longer, and specializes in eating other snakes, including Indian cobras. It keeps higher Size, Raw Power, Weaponry, Attack, Defense, Intelligence and Special. The Indian cobra sits a few points below it in Attack and Special.
- **Versus Black Mamba (1.6 kg):** similar body mass. The mamba is faster, more agile and carries a venom with several complementary neurotoxin types, so it keeps higher Attack, Agility and Special. The Indian cobra gets slightly higher Defense and Toughness for a sturdier body and its deterrent hood display.
- **Versus Mongoose (0.8 kg):** the mongoose is much smaller and lower in Attack, but its neurotoxin-resistant receptors and agility let it beat the cobra's main weapon. The cobra's Special is much higher in general, but this is an explicitly bad matchup for it.
- **Versus Rattlesnake (2.5 kg):** similar size. The rattlesnake has heat pits, a rattle and hinged long fangs; the cobra has a hood display and neurotoxic venom. Attack is close, with the rattlesnake one point higher for longer fangs and larger venom delivery.
- **Versus Inland Taipan:** the taipan's venom is far more potent in lab tests and it bites repeatedly, so its Attack and Special are clearly higher.
- **Anti-inflation check:** cultural fame and human death toll are not counted as Attack. The death toll mainly reflects how often people meet the snake. Physical ratings stay low for a 2 kg unarmored animal.
