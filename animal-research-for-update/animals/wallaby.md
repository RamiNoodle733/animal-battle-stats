# Wallaby (*Wallabia bicolor*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Wallaby (researched as the swamp wallaby, also called black wallaby, black-tailed wallaby or fern wallaby)
- **Scientific name:** *Wallabia bicolor* (Desmarest, 1804)
- **Exact animal represented:** the swamp wallaby of eastern Australia, the only living member of the genus *Wallabia*. The site's live photo is a swamp wallaby at Jenolan Caves, New South Wales, so this report rates that species rather than the many *Notamacropus* and rock-wallaby species also called wallabies.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male of about **17 kg**, **76 cm** head-body length and a tail of about the same length.
- **Sex choice:** males average about 17 kg and females about 13 kg; males are the larger fighters in any contest.
- **Population variation:** a rare golden color form lives on North and South Stradbroke Islands and the nearby mainland. The species has expanded its range in Victoria and South Australia since rabbit control improved.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The swamp wallaby is a browsing herbivore with no specialized weapons. Its only real striking tools are its long hind feet, which in macropods can deliver kicks, but at 17 kg any such blow is far weaker than a large kangaroo's. Its skull is built for chewing leaves, not for biting hard. It is not known to attack other animals, and its whole defensive strategy is to avoid being seen and to bolt.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It hops bipedally on its long hind feet with its head held low and its tail stretched out straight behind, a gait that differs from other wallabies and suits pushing through thick undergrowth. It can also move around on all fours using its forefeet (ADW). In dense ferns and scrub it relies on quick bursts and sharp turns rather than long open-ground runs.

### Endurance
GPS-collared swamp wallabies move within compact home areas, typically carrying fungal spores a few hundred metres and at most about 1.3 km before passing them, which reflects short daily movements. Hopping itself is economical in macropods, but there is no evidence that swamp wallabies make the long open-country journeys of large kangaroos.

### Senses
Smell matters more than in grazing kangaroos. In a field experiment, swamp wallabies used the odour of eucalypt seedlings alone to find hidden patches sooner and investigate them more often, and they reached their detection threshold with the odour of only a few seedlings; eastern grey kangaroos, which graze, showed no such response.

### Intelligence and tactics
Swamp wallabies change their habitat use by time of day: in a human-modified landscape, 48 GPS-tracked animals stayed near woodland, scrub and wetlands by day and used roads, housing estates and farmland more at night. They avoid areas patrolled by livestock guardian dogs entirely. This is sound risk management by a solitary browser, not complex problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Typically solitary and nocturnal, though several may gather at a good food source without territorial fights. The swamp wallaby is a browser of shrubs, ferns and other coarse vegetation rather than a grazer, and it will eat fungi, bark, algae and plants toxic to many animals.

### Fighting and aggression
Very low aggression. Animals feeding together show no territorial defense, and the species' response to threats is concealment and flight.

### Predator defense
Dingoes and wild dogs are its main predators; across nine regions of Victoria the black (swamp) wallaby was one of the key mammal foods in 5,875 dingo and wild dog scats. Eagles also take it. Its defense is to stay in thick cover by day and move out mainly at night.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense forest undergrowth, fern gullies, swamps and heath, where it hides and slips away.
- Night, when it forages with less risk.
- Mixed farmland and bush edges, where it switches habitat by time of day.

### Weaknesses
- Small absolute size and weak kicks and bite.
- No armor or dangerous weapon.
- Low willingness to fight.
- Vulnerable to dogs and dingoes in the open.
- Often killed on roads.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** very small animals, contests decided by hiding or escaping in dense cover.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized herbivores, foxes and small carnivores it can outrun in thick scrub.
- **Bad:** dingoes and dogs, large birds of prey, any medium or large predator, and fights in open ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Undergrowth Bolt** — hops fast with its head held low and tail straight out, a posture suited to bursting through ferns and thick scrub where larger animals cannot follow easily.
2. **Scent-Tracking Browser** — finds hidden seedlings by leaf odour alone, a skill grazing kangaroos lack; in a battle this means strong awareness at close range rather than extra damage.

### Unique traits
1. **Always Pregnant** — females mate and form a new embryo one to two days before giving birth, so they carry two pregnancies at once and stay pregnant and lactating throughout their reproductive life.
2. **Toxic-Plant Diet** — browses shrubs and ferns and tolerates plants poisonous to many animals, including bracken, hemlock and lantana.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Eastern Australia, from the tip of Cape York Peninsula in Queensland down the east coast to western Victoria and south-eastern South Australia, where its range has expanded greatly in recent decades. It lives in thick forest and woodland undergrowth, fern gullies, swamps and sandstone heath, and also uses farmland, mallee and sand hills; brigalow scrub in Queensland is a favoured habitat. It has been introduced to New Zealand.

### Diet and ecology
A browser rather than a grazer: shrubs, ferns, pasture plants, crops and native and introduced vegetation, including plants toxic to many other animals. It also eats fungi and disperses the spores of mycorrhizal fungi, which live in partnership with trees, hundreds of metres through the forest. It browses palatable tree seedlings such as blackbutt eucalypts, finding them by smell.

### Social structure
Solitary and mainly nocturnal. Unrelated animals may gather at shared food without territorial behavior.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeds year-round. Gestation is 33–38 days; the single young spends 8–9 months in the pouch and suckles until about 15 months. Because its pregnancy is longer than its oestrous cycle, a female ovulates, mates and forms a new embryo before the current fetus is born; the embryo then pauses its development (embryonic diapause) until the newborn leaves the pouch about nine months later. Males have an unusual sex-chromosome system with one X and two Y chromosomes.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (2016). It is common and has expanded its range, but many are killed on roads, and dingoes, wild dogs and eagles prey on it.

### Special features
- Hops with head low and tail held straight out.
- Browsing teeth with a lifelong cutting premolar.
- Tolerates bracken, hemlock and lantana.
- Finds food by leaf odour.
- Continuous overlapping pregnancies.
- Only living member of the genus *Wallabia*.

### Human interaction
The swamp wallaby lives in bushland close to towns and farms, is often seen by roads and is frequently hit by vehicles. In Queensland and New South Wales it is nicknamed "stinker" for its swampy smell. Livestock guardian dogs exclude swamp wallabies from grazing properties.

### Genuine fun facts
- Female swamp wallabies are pregnant almost continuously, carrying a new embryo while still pregnant with a full-term fetus.
- Males have two different Y chromosomes.
- Swamp wallabies can eat bracken, hemlock and lantana, plants that poison many animals.
- They can find hidden seedlings using smell alone.
- A rare golden form lives on Stradbroke Island.
- It is sometimes called the "stinker" because of its swampy odour.
- Swamp wallabies spread the spores of tree-partner fungi through the forest in their droppings, sometimes more than a kilometre away.

### Concise site-ready summary
The swamp wallaby is a 17 kg, dark-furred browser of eastern Australian undergrowth that hops with its head low and its tail straight out. It eats plants poisonous to many animals and finds food by smell, but with weak kicks, no armor and a strong preference for fleeing, it is easy prey for dingoes and dogs.

### Rich narrative profile
The swamp wallaby is a stocky, dark wallaby of about 17 kg in an adult male, with a rusty chest, a pale cheek stripe and a long tail often tipped white. It is the only living member of its genus and moves unlike other wallabies, bounding through ferns and scrub with its head low and tail held stiffly behind it.

It is a browser, feeding on shrubs, ferns and fungi, and it tolerates plants such as bracken, hemlock and lantana that poison many other herbivores. It finds seedlings by their leaf odour, a skill grazing kangaroos lack, and spends the day hidden in thick cover before moving out at night, even into farmland and suburbs. Its reproduction has been described as unique among mammals: a female forms a new embryo while still pregnant with a full-term fetus, so she is pregnant and suckling young throughout her adult life.

In a fight the swamp wallaby has little to offer. It can kick and push like other macropods, but at 17 kg its blows are light, its skull is built for leaves, and it has no armor. Dingoes and wild dogs eat it regularly. Its ABS profile therefore has low Attack and Defense, with good Agility for escape through dense undergrowth.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Height:** ADW's "70 cm tall" is undefined, so no height is proposed.
- **Speed:** no measurement exists; the Speed substat is an estimate from macropod hopping and habitat.
- **Lifespan:** ADW's 12–15 year wild figures look like longevity records, not averages.
- **Predators:** ADW says there are no known natural enemies, but scat studies show dingoes and wild dogs eat swamp wallabies regularly.
- **Length convention:** 152 cm includes the tail, matching the kangaroo and quokka reports; head-body alone is 76 cm.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Kangaroo (80 kg; A52 D38 Ag80 St86 I51 Sp68):** the swamp wallaby is about a fifth of the mass with the same kind of kick but far less force, so Attack (24.0) and Defense (21.0) are much lower. Agility is slightly lower (76.0) and Stamina much lower (62.0), since there is no evidence of the long open-ground travel that supports the kangaroo's elastic-tendon endurance.
- **Versus Quokka (3.5 kg; A18 D18 Ag73 St56 I49 Sp48):** the swamp wallaby is about five times heavier, so Attack and Defense sit a few points higher, with slightly higher Agility, Stamina and Special for its odour-guided browsing and toxic-plant tolerance.
- **Versus Coyote (15 kg; A38 D25):** a similar-mass predator; the coyote's bite and hunting behavior keep it well above the wallaby in Attack, while the two have similar Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the legacy Attack 1.9 was a placeholder and is replaced; the legacy Maneuverability 89 is reduced to 76.0 because no measurement supports near-elite agility.
