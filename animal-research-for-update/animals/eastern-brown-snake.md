# Eastern Brown Snake (*Pseudonaja textilis*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Eastern Brown Snake (Eastern Brownsnake, Common Brown Snake)
- **Scientific name:** *Pseudonaja textilis* (Duméril, Bibron & Duméril, 1854), family Elapidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the eastern brown snake of eastern Australia (with populations in southern New Guinea), not the other *Pseudonaja* brown snakes such as the western or dugite.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy robust adult, **1.0 kg** and **150 cm** total length, sex unspecified. This pass did not verify a consistent adult size difference between the sexes, so no sex-based modifier is applied.
- **Population notes:** the Australian Museum gives an average total length of about 1.5 m, with some animals reaching 2 m; the largest reliably measured specimen was 2013 mm. Venom bioactivity differs between populations according to a 2013 study, which affects prey incapacitation but not body size.

### Bite, fangs and venom
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No defensible bite-pressure measurement; the weapon is venom.
- No reliable fang-length measurement was located.
- **Potency:** a 2015 venomics study describes eastern brown snake venom as the second most toxic snake venom known and the most common cause of snakebite death in Australia.

In rodents, injected prothrombin activator proteins caused instant circulatory failure, which helps explain very fast prey incapacitation. Early cardiovascular collapse is a recognised cause of death in human bites.

### Defensive structure
- Slender body with smooth scales; no armor. Defense depends on speed, warning display and the bite.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The primary weapon is a front-fanged bite delivering a venom built around a prothrombin activator. It triggers runaway clotting that uses up the blood's clotting factors, and it can cause sudden cardiovascular collapse. Pre- and postsynaptic neurotoxins add paralysing effects. The secondary weapon is speed and a fast, well-aimed strike from a raised neck posture.

### Offensive mechanics
Against rodents the venom works very fast: purified prothrombin activator caused instant circulatory failure in rodent tests. Against larger opponents, effect depends on getting enough venom in. In field tests with human harassment, only about 15% of strikes had the potential to cause significant envenomation, because the snake often strikes without a firm bite.

### Defensive features and durability
Physically fragile: a 1 kg slender snake with no armor. It relies on detecting threats early, fleeing, a warning neck display and, as a last resort, striking.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Fast and agile for a snake, especially when warm. The slender body allows quick direction changes through grass and farmland cover. No measured top speed exists.

### Endurance
Active daytime forager, but an ectotherm whose performance depends on temperature. Not built for sustained exertion.

### Senses
Normal elapid vision and chemoreception; no heat pits. No species-specific thresholds were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Whitaker et al. found brown snakes surprisingly tolerant of harassment and usually giving a warning before striking, with defensive behavior changing with body temperature. This is a graded, context-dependent defense rather than blind aggression. There is no evidence for broader problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Actively hunts small mammals, especially introduced rats and mice, plus frogs, reptiles, reptile eggs and birds.

### Fighting and aggression
The species has a reputation for aggression, but controlled trials show reluctance to deliver firm bites even when continuously provoked. It is still responsible for more snakebite deaths in Australia than any other species, largely because it is common in farmland and suburbs where people meet it.

### Environmental strengths
- Open grassland, farmland and woodland with rodent prey and ground cover.
- Hot days, when it is fastest and most responsive.
- Close-range fights against unarmored mammals.

### Weaknesses
- Very low body mass and no armor.
- Many defensive strikes deliver little venom.
- Performance drops when cold.
- No constriction or mechanical weapon to hold a large opponent.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rodents and small to medium unarmored mammals and birds; opponents that must close in to bite or grab it.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized venomous snakes.
- **Bad:** snake-eating raptors, venom-resistant predators, armored animals and large animals that can crush it before the venom acts.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Clot-Cascade Venom** — a prothrombin activator that mimics the body's own clotting enzymes sets off runaway clotting, using up clotting factors and, in rodent tests, causing instant circulatory failure. Pre- and postsynaptic neurotoxins add paralysis.
2. **Warning-Then-Strike Defense** — the snake raises its neck in a threat display and usually warns before it strikes. Many defensive strikes carry little venom, but a committed bite is extremely dangerous.

### Unique traits
1. **Farmland Rat Hunter** — it thrives in farmland and disturbed land, where introduced rats and mice are its favorite prey, which puts it close to people.
2. **Warm-Weather Racer** — it moves surprisingly fast on hot days, and field trials show its defensive behavior changes with body temperature.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Eastern brown snakes live throughout much of eastern Australia and in parts of southern New Guinea. They use coastal heath, dry sclerophyll forest and woodland, and are frequently found in grassland, farmland and other disturbed land.

### Diet and ecology
Mostly small mammals, especially introduced house mice and rats, plus frogs, reptiles, reptile eggs and birds. Farmland with abundant rodents supports good numbers of brown snakes, which in turn control pests.

### Social structure
Solitary outside breeding.

### Reproduction and life history
Egg-laying. Sexual maturity is reached at about 2–3 years, and clutches of roughly 10–35 eggs are reported. Females develop yolking follicles from mid-September to late November and carry eggs from late October to late January.

### Conservation
The IUCN Red List category was not confirmed in this pass. The species is widespread and common in eastern Australia, including heavily modified farmland. Radio-tracking in the Murrumbidgee Irrigation Area found human-related causes among the main sources of death for large elapids there, and Shine (2024) argues that farmers should not kill venomous snakes.

### Special features
- Front fangs delivering a venom with a prothrombin activator and neurotoxins.
- One of the most toxic snake venoms known.
- Slender, fast body.
- Raised-neck warning display before striking.
- Tolerance of farmland and suburban edges.

### Human interaction
The eastern brown snake causes more snakebite deaths than any other species in Australia. Brown snakes accounted for 23 of 35 snakebite deaths recorded by the National Coronial Information System from January 2000 to December 2016, and about one to two deaths a year are attributed to eastern brown bites despite antivenom and first aid. Deaths are linked to venom-induced consumptive coagulopathy and early cardiovascular collapse. Most bites happen when people try to kill or handle the snake.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its venom has been described as the second most toxic snake venom known.
- Its prothrombin activator closely copies the body's own clotting enzymes (activated factor X bound to factor V).
- In harassment trials, only about 15% of its strikes had the potential to cause a serious envenomation.
- It usually gives a warning display before it strikes.
- Brown snakes caused 23 of 35 Australian snakebite deaths recorded from 2000 to 2016.
- The largest reliably measured eastern brown snake was 2013 mm long.
- It favors introduced house mice and rats, making farmland ideal habitat.

### Concise site-ready summary
The eastern brown snake is a fast, slender Australian snake whose venom sets off runaway blood clotting and can cause sudden collapse. Its venom is among the most toxic of any snake, and it causes more snakebite deaths in Australia than any other species. Yet at about a kilogram it is physically fragile, and it usually warns before it strikes.

### Rich narrative profile
The eastern brown snake shows why venom mechanism matters more than size for snakes. At around 1.5 m and a kilogram it is light and unarmored, with almost no raw strength. What makes it dangerous is a venom that attacks the blood itself: a prothrombin activator that copies the body's own clotting enzymes, starting a runaway clotting reaction that uses up the clotting factors and can collapse circulation. Rodents injected with this protein suffered instant circulatory failure.

Its behavior is more measured than its reputation. When researchers harassed brown snakes under controlled conditions, the snakes were surprisingly tolerant, usually warned before striking, and delivered potentially serious bites in only about 15% of strikes. The raised neck display is a warning first and a launch position second.

The snake's danger to people comes from where it lives. It thrives in farmland and suburban edges full of mice and rats, so people meet it often, and bites typically happen when someone tries to kill or catch it. For battle ratings, its strengths are a fast, agile body and a venom that can drop much bigger animals. Its weaknesses are a fragile body, small and often partial venom doses, and dependence on warm weather.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Black Mamba (1.6 kg):** similar size class. The mamba's neurotoxin mix and rapid repeat strikes keep its Attack and Special slightly higher; the brown snake's clotting venom is more toxic in mouse tests but its defensive strikes often carry little venom. Agility is close, with the mamba slightly ahead in Maneuverability.
- **Versus Rattlesnake (2.5 kg):** the rattlesnake is heavier with hinged long fangs, heat pits and a rattle, so it keeps higher Defense, Senses and Size. The brown snake gets slightly higher Attack for a faster-acting and more toxic venom, and higher Agility.
- **Versus Inland Taipan (1.8 kg):** the taipan's venom is more potent and it bites repeatedly, so its Attack and Special are higher. The brown snake is more agile and more ready to defend itself, so it gets higher Agility and Ferocity.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the human death toll reflects how often people meet it in farmland, not extra combat power, so it is not counted in Attack. Physical ratings stay in the lowest band for a 1 kg unarmored snake.
