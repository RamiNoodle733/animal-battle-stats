# Stonefish (*Synanceia verrucosa*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Stonefish (Reef Stonefish)
- **Scientific name:** *Synanceia verrucosa* Bloch & Schneider, 1801, family Synanceiidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the reef stonefish of the Indo-Pacific. Five species share the name stonefish in the genus *Synanceia*; this profile uses the reef stonefish because it is the most widespread and best documented. Venom studies on the estuarine stonefish (*S. horrida*) are used only where noted.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy representative adult, **35 cm** and **1.5 kg**, sex unspecified. No consistent size difference between the sexes was documented in the sources located.
- **Population notes:** FishBase gives a maximum length of 40 cm (standard length) and a maximum published weight of 2.4 kg; the Aquarium of the Pacific says the species commonly reaches about 40 cm.

### Bite, spines and venom
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-pressure measurement exists; the stonefish swallows prey by suction.
- **Spines:** the dorsal fin carries **12–14** stout spines (FishBase; the Aquarium of the Pacific cites 13). Each spine has grooves that act like a syringe channel and a large venom gland at its base. Venom is pushed into the wound when pressure is put on the spine, for example when it is stepped on or bitten.
- **Toxins:** stonustoxin (SNTX), the lethal factor in stonefish venom, is made of two subunits that belong to the perforin family of pore-forming immune proteins; it punches holes in cell membranes, destroys red blood cells and causes platelet aggregation. Verrucotoxin (VTx) from the reef stonefish is a four-part glycoprotein that is haemolytic, lowered blood pressure and was lethal in mice. A 2021 study characterised coagulotoxic and neurotoxic effects of reef stonefish venom, and a 2025 study examined small molecules present in *Synanceia* envenomation.

### Defensive structure
- The venomous dorsal spines are the main defense. The body is squat and heavy for its length, and its lumpy, rock-like appearance makes it hard to spot. No bony armor plates were documented.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The stonefish has two separate weapon systems. For feeding, it uses a sudden suction gulp that engulfs small fish and shrimp that swim within reach. For defense, it relies on 12–14 venom-loaded dorsal spines that inject venom when an attacker presses on them. The venom is not used to catch prey.

### Offensive mechanics
Offense is limited to prey small enough to swallow. It does not chase, and it cannot drive its spines into an opponent on purpose. Against anything larger than its mouth, it has almost no active attack.

### Defensive features and durability
Defense is where it shines. A predator that bites or grabs it, or an animal that steps on it, drives the spines in and receives venom that causes extreme pain, cell damage and falling blood pressure. Its rock-like disguise means most threats never notice it. Once found and attacked by something that avoids the spines, its small body has limited toughness.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A poor swimmer that spends most of its time motionless on the bottom, often partly buried. It can reposition short distances but has no speed or agility in open water.

### Endurance
Built for waiting, not moving. Energy use is low, but it cannot sustain activity.

### Senses
Vision is enough to time a strike at passing prey. No species-specific sensory studies were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
A single, highly effective strategy: stay still, look like a rock, and gulp whatever comes close. No evidence for flexible tactics.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Feeds on small fish and crustaceans such as shrimp.

### Fighting and aggression
Not aggressive. It does not attack large animals; stings happen when it is stepped on, handled or grabbed.

### Environmental strengths
- Shallow reef, rubble and sand bottoms where its disguise works best.
- Ambush range: anything that swims within reach of its mouth.
- Any fight where the opponent must bite or grab it.

### Weaknesses
- Almost no mobility or pursuit ability.
- No active way to deliver its venom.
- Small body with limited toughness if the spines are avoided.
- Useless out of water.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** predators that bite or grab first (fish, sharks, rays, octopuses, marine mammals that mouth prey), and small fish or shrimp that swim close.
- **Mixed:** animals that attack from the side or belly and avoid the dorsal spines.
- **Bad:** anything that can ignore it, attack from range, or crush it without touching the spines; any land-based fight.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Spine-Syringe Venom** — 12–14 grooved dorsal spines, each with a venom gland at its base, inject venom when pressed. Stonustoxin punches holes in cell membranes and verrucotoxin drops blood pressure, making a bite or grab very costly for any attacker.
2. **Lightning Suction Gulp** — from total stillness, the stonefish opens its large mouth in a split second, sucking in small fish and shrimp before they can react.

### Unique traits
1. **Most Venomous Fish** — the Australian Museum and the Aquarium of the Pacific describe the reef stonefish as the most venomous fish in the world; stings are excruciating and can occasionally be fatal to people.
2. **Living Rock Disguise** — its lumpy, rock-coloured body and habit of lying still, often half-buried, make it look like part of the reef floor.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The reef stonefish lives across the Indo-Pacific: from the Red Sea and East Africa east to French Polynesia, north to the Ryukyu and Ogasawara islands, and south to Queensland, Australia. It rests on shallow reef, rubble and sandy bottoms, often partly buried.

### Diet and ecology
It eats small fish and crustaceans such as shrimp, taken by ambush. As a well-hidden predator it has few natural enemies because the spines deter most fish predators.

### Social structure
Solitary.

### Reproduction and life history
Details of reproduction and growth were not documented in the sources located in this pass. A chromosome-level genome has been assembled for the species, mainly to study its stonustoxin genes.

### Conservation
The IUCN Red List category was not confirmed in this pass. The species has a very wide Indo-Pacific range and appears in fisheries and the aquarium trade, according to FishBase.

### Special features
- 12–14 stout, grooved dorsal spines with venom glands.
- Stonustoxin, a two-part toxin built like the perforin proteins of the immune system.
- Verrucotoxin, a four-part toxin that lowers blood pressure.
- Rock-like body and colour with long periods of stillness.
- Large mouth for suction feeding.

### Human interaction
Stings usually happen when people step on stonefish in shallow water or handle them. They are excruciatingly painful and occasionally fatal. Venom research has studied the blood, nerve, blood-pressure and cell-damaging effects of the venom, and the structure of stonustoxin has helped scientists understand how perforin-like pore-forming proteins work. It is also traded as a food fish and an aquarium fish.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is widely described as the most venomous fish in the world.
- Its main toxin, stonustoxin, is built like perforin, a protein the human immune system uses to punch holes in infected cells.
- Each dorsal spine has grooves that channel venom like a syringe.
- Verrucotoxin, found in the reef stonefish, lowered blood pressure and was lethal in mice.
- The heaviest published reef stonefish weighed 2.4 kg.
- Its range stretches from the Red Sea to French Polynesia.
- Its species name, *verrucosa*, means "warty" in Latin, fitting its lumpy skin.

### Concise site-ready summary
The reef stonefish is a squat, rock-like Indo-Pacific fish that lies motionless on the seabed and gulps passing prey in a split second. Its 12–14 dorsal spines carry what is often called the most potent venom of any fish, turning any bite or careless step into a painful, sometimes deadly mistake. It is a near-perfect defender but has almost no way to attack anything larger than its mouth.

### Rich narrative profile
The stonefish is built around doing nothing, very well. It lies still on the reef floor, often partly buried, with a lumpy body that looks like a rock. Small fish and shrimp that drift too close are sucked into its large mouth in a split second. That is its whole offense, and it only works on prey small enough to swallow.

Its defense is another matter. Along its back sit 12 to 14 stout spines, each grooved and backed by a venom gland. When a predator bites down or a foot presses on the fish, the spines drive in and venom follows. The main toxin, stonustoxin, belongs to the same family as perforin, the protein immune cells use to punch holes in infected cells, and it does something similar to the victim's cells. Verrucotoxin adds a sharp drop in blood pressure.

On the absolute battle scale, the stonefish is an extreme specialist. It scores very high in Special and respectable in Defense, because any opponent that attacks it head-on is likely to be stung. But it cannot chase, it cannot deliver venom on purpose, and it is small and slow. An opponent that simply avoids touching the spines, or attacks from below or at range, faces little danger. It wins by being attacked, not by attacking.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Weight:** only a maximum (2.4 kg) is published; the 1.5 kg canonical value is a scaled estimate.
- **Spine count:** 12–14 in FishBase and aquarist sources, 13 in the Aquarium of the Pacific account.
- **Lifespan:** unresolved; `0.0` is a gap, not a measurement.
- **Strike speed:** the popular 15 ms figure lacks a located primary source and is not used.
- **Venom studies:** some stonustoxin work is on the estuarine stonefish (*S. horrida*); the mechanism is shared across the genus, but potency may differ.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Lionfish (0.5 kg):** both use venomous spines mainly for defense. The stonefish is three times heavier, its venom is far more dangerous, and its disguise is better, so it gets higher Defense and Special. Lionfish is a more mobile, active hunter, so it keeps higher Agility, Stamina and Intelligence.
- **Versus Pufferfish (1.5 kg):** same mass. The pufferfish combines inflation with toxic tissues and is far more mobile, so it keeps higher Agility, Defense and Special. The stonefish actively injects venom through spines, so it has higher Weaponry and Attack.
- **Versus Stingray:** the stingray profile has not been re-researched yet (its live values are legacy placeholders). A large stingray's barbed tail can be swung at an attacker, which is more active delivery than the stonefish's passive spines; the stonefish should stay well below a large ray in size-driven ratings.
- **Versus Gaboon Viper:** both combine venom with camouflage-based ambush. The viper actively bites and injects venom into prey and attackers, so its Attack is much higher; the stonefish's Special is slightly lower because its venom cannot be aimed.
- **Versus Box Jellyfish:** both are passive-contact venom specialists. The jellyfish's tentacles cover a much wider contact zone and actively capture prey, so its Attack and Special stay higher, while the stonefish has a sturdier body and higher Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the "most venomous fish" title is carried by Protection, Defense, Abilities and Special. It does not raise Attack, because the venom is never used offensively.
