# Mako Shark (*Isurus oxyrinchus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Mako shark (shortfin mako)
- **Scientific name:** *Isurus oxyrinchus* Rafinesque, 1810
- **Exact animal represented:** the shortfin mako, the widespread and best-studied mako. Not the longfin mako (*Isurus paucus*), a separate, rarer deep-water species.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **275 kg** and **280 cm fork length** (roughly 3 m total length), matching the median size and weight at maturity for North Atlantic females.
- **Sex choice:** females are much larger. A NOAA-hosted North Atlantic reproductive study found that females matured between 263 and 291 cm fork length, with a median length at maturity of 280 cm FL and a median weight at maturity of **275 kg**, while males matured at 173–187 cm FL with a median of 182 cm FL and **64 kg**. There is no male-only fighting role, so the larger mature female is the representative combat body, as for the Great White Shark.
- **Population notes:** makos caught in fisheries are often juveniles or males. Typical adult summaries of 2.5–3.2 m and 60–140 kg (Wikipedia, tertiary) mix both sexes. Exceptional females exceed 4 m and about 570 kg; FishBase gives a maximum of 445 cm total length.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- NOAA-hosted North Atlantic study: median weight at maturity 275 kg (females), 64 kg (males).
- Typical adult 60–140 kg across sexes (tertiary); record animals over 570 kg.
- **Canonical:** **275.0 kg** mature female.
- **Confidence:** Moderate. It is a maturity threshold, so many mature females are heavier.

### Length
- Female median length at maturity 280 cm FL (NOAA-hosted study); FishBase maximum 445 cm TL.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 280.0**, recorded as fork length. Total length is somewhat greater (about 3 m); a verified FL-to-TL conversion was not retrieved in this run.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high for fork length.

### Height
- Standing height is not meaningful for a shark. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- Waller et al. (2023, Journal of Fish Biology) put speed-sensing bio-loggers on two free-swimming makos. Mean cruising speed was **0.90 ± 0.07 m/s**. The maximum burst was **5.02 m/s** from a 2 m female, and one burst was held for 14 s at a mean of 2.38 m/s. The authors describe this maximum as among the highest directly measured in sharks, tunas and billfishes.
- Popular claims of 70 km/h or more are not instrumented and are not adopted.
- Makos leap as high as about 6 m out of the water (Guinness World Records). Simple ballistics would require leaving the water at roughly 11 m/s to reach that height, which suggests the measured 5 m/s is a floor rather than a ceiling, but the leap height itself is not an instrumented measurement.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 5.0**, the highest directly measured burst.
- **Confidence:** High that 5.0 m/s was reached; low that it is the true maximum.

### Lifespan
- Bishop et al. (2006, New Zealand) aged makos to a maximum of 29 years for males and 32 years for females (search-result summary of species accounts).
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 30.0.**
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Bite and dentition
- Makos have long, slender, smooth-edged teeth suited to seizing fast fish, rather than the broad serrated cutting teeth of a Great White or Bull Shark.
- In 2020 a Discovery Shark Week crew used a custom device near Mayor Island, New Zealand, and reported a mako bite of about **13,000 N (about 3,000 lbf)**, described in the press as the strongest shark bite ever recorded (Newsweek). This was a television measurement, not a peer-reviewed study, and it gives no contact area.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** The television force figure is recorded as context only and is not converted to PSI.

### Defensive structure
- Skin covered in small dermal denticles. On the flanks and fins these scales are flexible and can bristle to angles above 40° only when water flows backward over them, which controls flow separation (Motta et al. 2012, Journal of Morphology; Lang and colleagues).
- No armor. Swordfish bills have been found broken off and embedded in mako heads and gills in Sicily (Wikipedia, tertiary), showing that prey can inflict serious wounds.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The mako's weapon is a fast, precise bite. Its long, slender, non-serrated teeth are built to seize slippery, fast-moving fish such as bluefish, tunas and mackerels; stomach studies also record billfish, including swordfish, and other sharks. A study of Atlantic stomachs found headless and tailless fish, consistent with makos biting off parts of prey too large to swallow whole. The mako does not have the Great White's broad cutting dentition, so it is less efficient at removing large chunks from big, tough opponents, but it delivers its bite at a speed few sharks can match.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The mako is the roster's clearest example of a high-performance shark. Its stiff, torpedo-shaped body, crescent tail and bristling flank scales support fast bursts: tagged makos cruise near 0.9 m/s and have been measured at 5.02 m/s in a burst, among the highest speeds ever directly measured in sharks, tunas and billfishes. Makos also leap spectacularly, up to about 6 m, and have landed in boats. Turning control is excellent for a 3 m animal, although the stiff body does not match the tight turns of hammerheads or small reef fish.

### Endurance
Makos are regional endotherms: counter-current heat exchangers keep their red swimming muscle warmer than the surrounding water, and a 2025 Journal of Animal Ecology study found that makos have enhanced thermoregulation, holding body temperature steadier across water temperatures. They travel huge distances; one New Zealand mako fitted with a GPS tag covered about 13,300 km in six months (Live Science). This is elite sustained output.

### Senses
Makos have the full shark sensory suite of smell, lateral line and electroreception, with large eyes used in visually guided pursuit of fast fish in open water. Species-specific sensory thresholds were not retrieved.

### Intelligence and tactics
Makos are often described as having the largest brain-to-body ratio of any shark, but that claim comes from a Discovery Shark Week post and was not traced to a primary study, so it carries little weight here. Behaviorally, makos pursue fast fish, time attacks on swordfish around their spawning period (as suggested by the Sicily observations), and have been seen circling injured marine mammals such as a finless porpoise. That supports a capable, flexible pursuit predator without evidence of complex tactics.

### Hunting behavior
Stomach analysis of 399 makos (67–328 cm) from Cape Hatteras to the Grand Banks found bluefish made up about 77.5% of the diet by volume (ReefQuest summary of Stillwell and Kohler). Elsewhere makos eat mackerels, tunas, herrings, squid, billfish and other sharks. Large makos occasionally take marine mammals.

### Intraspecific fighting and predator defense
No detailed intraspecific fighting is documented. Adult makos have few predators other than larger sharks and orcas, but swordfish can fight back hard enough to kill them.

### Aggression
Makos are bold, fast-committing predators and are famously explosive when hooked, leaping and occasionally landing in boats. Unprovoked attacks on people are rare.

### Environmental strengths
- Open, clear ocean where speed and vision decide pursuits.
- Cool water, where regional endothermy keeps muscles working.
- Long engagements that favor a high-output swimmer.

### Weaknesses
- No armor; a long spear such as a swordfish bill can penetrate the head and gills.
- Slender grasping teeth are less effective at cutting large, tough prey than serrated teeth.
- Much smaller than the Great White, so it loses head-on contests of mass.
- Must keep swimming to breathe efficiently.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** fast fish and medium-sized open-water animals it can outswim and seize; opponents that tire quickly.
- **Mixed:** similarly sized sharks and armed billfish, where one mistake can be fatal to either side.
- **Bad:** much larger predators (Great White Shark, Orca), and armored or heavily built opponents its teeth cannot cut.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Bristle-Scale Burst** — flexible flank and fin scales bristle above 40° when water starts to flow backward over them, helping keep flow attached during fast swimming and turns. In a matchup it supports the mako's bursts, measured at 5.02 m/s, among the fastest ever directly measured in sharks, tunas and billfishes.
2. **Rocket Breach** — makos can launch up to about 6 m out of the water and have landed in boats. The same explosive power lets a mako close distance vertically and surprise opponents from below.

### Unique traits
1. **Warm-Muscle Cruiser** — regional endothermy keeps the red swimming muscle warmer than the sea, supporting high performance in cool water and ocean-scale journeys such as 13,300 km in six months.
2. **Swordfish Hunter** — makos prey on swordfish and other billfish, and some have been found with swordfish bills embedded in their heads, a rare predator-prey duel between two high-speed fish.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The shortfin mako lives in tropical and temperate seas worldwide, mostly in open ocean from the surface to several hundred metres, and also along continental shelves. It avoids the coldest waters but uses warm muscles to hunt in cool water.

### Diet and ecology
Makos are mainly fish eaters. Bluefish dominate the diet in the northwest Atlantic; elsewhere mackerels, tunas, herrings, squid, swordfish and other billfish, and smaller sharks are common. Large individuals sometimes take marine mammals. The mako is a high-level predator in open-ocean food webs.

### Social structure
Makos are solitary. Sexes and size classes are often found in different areas, with large mature females less common in catches than males and juveniles.

### Reproduction and life history
Makos give birth to live young after the embryos feed on unfertilized eggs in the uterus. Females mature late, around 280 cm fork length, and reproduce only every few years, so populations recover slowly. Maximum known ages are 29 years for males and 32 for females.

### Conservation
The shortfin mako is listed as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List (2019 assessment), with an estimated global decline of about 47% and the highest probability of a 50–79% decline over three generations. Overfishing in longline fisheries is the main cause. NOAA completed an Endangered Species Act status review in 2022, and international fishery managers have since agreed stricter retention limits for North Atlantic makos (Oceana report).

### Special features
- Directly measured burst speed of 5.02 m/s, among the highest in pelagic fishes.
- Leaps up to about 6 m high.
- Flexible bristling scales on flanks and fins that control flow separation.
- Regional endothermy with warm red muscle.
- Long, slender, smooth-edged grasping teeth.

### Human interaction
Makos are prized sport fish because of their leaps and power, and they are fished commercially for meat and fins. They are rarely involved in attacks on people but can be dangerous when hooked and brought aboard.

### Genuine fun facts
- The shortfin mako holds the record for the highest-leaping shark, about 6 m.
- A GPS-tagged mako from New Zealand swam about 13,300 km in six months.
- Mako flank scales bristle like tiny flaps when water starts to flow backward, an idea engineers are studying for aircraft wings.
- Makos have been found with swordfish bills broken off inside their heads.
- In one study bluefish made up about 77.5% of the diet of northwest Atlantic makos.
- A 2 m female mako carried a tag that clocked a burst of 5.02 m/s.

### Concise site-ready summary
The shortfin mako is the ocean's speed shark: a 3-metre, 275 kg warm-muscled hunter that bursts faster than almost any fish ever measured and leaps up to 6 metres out of the water. Its slender grasping teeth are deadly to fast fish, but it lacks the mass and cutting bite of a Great White.

### Rich narrative profile
The shortfin mako is built for acceleration. Its body is a stiff torpedo driven by a crescent tail and by red muscle kept warmer than the sea around it. Tags on free-swimming makos have recorded bursts above 5 metres per second, among the fastest ever directly measured in sharks, tunas and billfishes, and makos can launch themselves around 6 metres into the air. Even the skin helps: tiny scales on the flanks bristle when water starts to flow backward, keeping the flow attached as the shark turns and sprints.

That speed serves a precise weapon. The mako's long, slender teeth are made for grabbing fast, slippery fish, and it hunts bluefish, tunas, mackerels and even swordfish. The swordfish fights back: makos have been found with bills snapped off in their heads and gills. It shows the mako's style, a high-speed predator that wins by closing distance faster than its prey can react rather than by overpowering it with mass.

In a battle the mako is dangerous and elusive. It can outswim and outlast most open-water opponents and strike with little warning. Its limits are size and armor: a 275 kg shark with grasping teeth cannot carve up a much larger animal the way a Great White can, and it has no protection against a determined counterattack. Against anything it can outpace it is lethal; against giants it must rely on speed to avoid a fight it cannot win.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Speed:** only one instrumented maximum (5.02 m/s) exists; leap heights and popular claims suggest more but are not measurements.
- **Bite:** the 13,000 N figure comes from television, so no PSI is adopted.
- **Mass:** 275 kg is a maturity threshold; many mature females are heavier.
- **Length:** the canonical value is fork length, not total length.
- **Brain size:** the "largest brain ratio among sharks" claim is not traced to primary data.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Great White Shark (900 kg; A88 D68 Ag73 St91 I70 Sp87):** the great white is three times heavier with serrated cutting teeth, so it stays well above in Attack (88.0 vs 75.0) and Defense (68.0 vs 53.0). The mako takes Agility (85.0 vs 73.0) because of its bursts and leaps. Both are regional endotherms with elite Stamina (88.0 vs 91.0); Special is just below the great white (86.0 vs 87.0).
- **Versus Tiger Shark (legacy, not yet researched; A87.9 D63.6 Ag85.1 St89.5):** the tiger shark's live values are legacy placeholders, not research anchors. A researched tiger shark (heavier, with serrated can-opener teeth) should still sit above the mako in Attack and Defense and below it in Agility.
- **Versus Swordfish (legacy, not yet researched; A21.8 D24.2 Ag92):** makos hunt swordfish and are sometimes killed by them. The legacy swordfish Attack of 21.8 is clearly too low and should be revisited when it is researched; the mako's ratings were not calibrated against those placeholders.
- **Versus Bull Shark (111 kg; A72 D52 Ag73 St69 I58 Sp82) and Hammerhead (230 kg; A74 D55 Ag84 St82 I63 Sp92):** the mako is heavier than the bull shark and similar in mass to the hammerhead. Attack 75.0 sits just above both; Agility 85.0 edges the hammerhead's 84.0; Stamina 88.0 is higher than both because of endothermy. Special 86.0 stays below the hammerhead's 92.0 cephalofoil sensory specialization.
- **Versus Marlin (250 kg; A58 Ag78 St90) and Sailfish (70 kg; Ag89):** the mako is a much better armed predator of similar mass to the marlin, so Attack is far higher; the sailfish remains the roster's agility benchmark among fishes.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Speed 78.0 is not set at the ceiling because only one instrumented burst exists. Intelligence stays at 64.0 because the brain-ratio claim is unverified.
