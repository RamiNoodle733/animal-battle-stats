# Jaguarundi (*Herpailurus yagouaroundi*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Jaguarundi
- **Scientific name:** *Herpailurus yagouaroundi* (É. Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, 1803); formerly placed in *Puma* as *Puma yagouaroundi*, the name Animal Diversity Web still uses
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild jaguarundi of Central and South America and Mexico, treated as one monotypic species (the IUCN Cat Specialist Group's 2017 revision found no genetic basis for subspecies).
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **7.0 kg**, the top of the usual 3.5–7 kg range (Wikipedia) and the middle of ADW's 4.5–9.0 kg range. Individuals near 9 kg are reported but are not used as the baseline.
- **Sex choice:** males are somewhat larger and use much larger home ranges (8.5–25.3 km² in Brazil and up to 88–100 km² in Belize, against 1.4–20 km² for females), so a male is used.
- **Population notes:** coat colour ranges from dark grey-brown to reddish-tawny; both colour types can occur in one litter. Its body plan is unusual among cats: long, low and slender, with short legs, a small flattened head, small round ears and a long tail.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The jaguarundi hunts mainly on the ground for prey under 1 kg: ground-feeding birds, rodents (especially rice rats, cotton rats and cane mice), reptiles, frogs and arthropods. It also takes larger prey such as poultry, fish, marmosets, rabbits, opossums and occasionally common tapeti, and small deer have been found in its diet (possibly as carrion). It eats about 400 g of vertebrate prey a day. Its weapons are ordinary small-cat claws and bite on a slender 7 kg body; it is effective against small animals but lacks the mass or grappling power for anything much larger.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Long, low, supple body; an efficient climber that walks easily along branches; a strong swimmer (one was recorded crossing Bolivia's Tuichi River); and a jumper that can leap about 2 m into the air to catch birds. Texas Parks and Wildlife notes it climbs trees easily and has been seen springing into the air after prey. This is high all-round agility, slightly below the most acrobatic small cats.

### Endurance
Active for most of the day: a Belize study found jaguarundis started moving before dawn and stayed active through most of the day until sunset, with a hunting peak from late morning to noon. Home ranges are large for a small cat, up to 88–100 km² for two Belizean males. This indicates good sustained activity.

### Senses
A daytime hunter that relies on vision and hearing. Camera-trap studies in Costa Rica and the Brazilian Caatinga found it mostly diurnal, unlike the nocturnal *Leopardus* cats around it (Journal of Mammalogy 2020; PLOS One 2023). No measured sensory thresholds were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Secretive and alert, notably wary of traps. It uses dense vegetation even in open habitats, crosses rivers, and persists in human-altered landscapes: in San Luis Potosí, Mexico, it was most likely to occur in mosaics of tropical forest, farmland, pasture and edges near roads (Acta Zoológica Mexicana 2019). It has one of the richest vocal repertoires of any cat, with 13 call types recorded.

### Sociality and hunting
Usually solitary or in pairs in the wild; captive animals are more tolerant of each other. Densities are usually low (about 0.01–0.05 per km² in Brazil).

### Fighting and aggression
Shy and reclusive. It is wary of larger predators; recorded predators include boa constrictors, cougars and domestic dogs. In the small Neotropical cat community, the ocelot is the dominant competitor that suppresses jaguarundi numbers (PLOS One 2023).

### Environmental strengths
- Forest edges, dense scrub and thickets near running water.
- Mixed farmland and forest mosaics.
- Both trees and water are usable escape routes.

### Weaknesses
- Low absolute mass and power.
- No armor.
- Outclassed by ocelots, cougars and dogs.
- Small prey focus limits its practice against large, dangerous opponents.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rodents, ground birds, lizards, frogs, fish and small opossums.
- **Mixed:** animals of similar size such as rabbits, small monkeys or medium snakes.
- **Bad:** ocelots and larger cats, dogs, large constrictors, armored animals.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Daylight Stalker** — unlike most small American cats it hunts mainly by day, with a peak from late morning to noon, which puts it in a different time slot from ocelots and margays and lets it surprise diurnal birds and lizards.
2. **Bird-Snatch Leap** — it can spring about 2 m into the air to knock down birds, and it climbs and swims well, so it can follow or escape an opponent across ground, branches and water.

### Unique traits
1. **Otter-Shaped Cat** — a long, low body, short legs, a small flat head, small round ears and a long tail make it look more like a weasel or otter than a typical cat.
2. **Thirteen-Call Voice** — thirteen distinct calls have been recorded, including chattering, purring, screaming, whistling, yapping and a bird-like chirp.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
From southern Texas (where it is now very rare or gone) and Mexico through Central America to northern Argentina and Uruguay, east of the Andes. It uses an unusually broad range of habitats for a small cat: tropical rainforest, deciduous forest, dry thorn scrub, savanna and semi-desert, usually near running water and dense cover, and it can live in reforested and farmed mosaics.

### Diet and ecology
Mostly small animals under 1 kg: rodents, ground-feeding birds, reptiles, frogs and arthropods, with occasional poultry, fish, rabbits, marmosets and opossums. It appears to take the most abundant and easily caught prey in each area.

### Social structure
Solitary or in pairs. Home ranges are large for its size: 1.4–18 km² for females and 8.5–25.3 km² for males in Brazil, and up to 88–100 km² for two males in Belize. It scent-marks by scraping, clawing logs, head rubbing and leaving feces uncovered.

### Reproduction and life history
Sexual maturity at one to three years. It breeds year-round in some regions. Captive individuals have lived 15 years or more.

### Conservation
Listed as **Near Threatened** in the IUCN Red List's 2026 assessment as shown in Wikipedia's species box; the article text still describes its earlier Least Concern listing, noting that assessors already considered Near Threatened justified. A 2024 continental analysis found its area of occupancy has shrunk over the last two decades (Global Ecology and Conservation). It is listed as endangered in the United States and threatened in Mexico. Threats are habitat loss and fragmentation, and persecution for killing poultry.

### Special features
- Long, low, unspotted body with a long tail.
- Daytime activity unusual among small cats.
- Strong climbing and swimming.
- Round pupils, shared with a few other cats such as the cougar.
- A very large vocal repertoire.

### Human interaction
Sometimes killed for raiding poultry. It tolerates light to moderate human disturbance and uses farm-forest mosaics, but road building and clearing of dense brush remain major threats. In the lower Rio Grande Valley, Texas, people are replanting native brush for jaguarundis and ocelots.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its name comes from the Old Guarani word for "dark jaguar".
- It can leap about 2 m into the air to catch birds.
- Jaguarundis have 38 chromosomes, while other small South American cats have 36.
- It has 13 recorded calls, including a bird-like chirp.
- It is one of the few small cats that hunts mainly by day.
- One was recorded swimming across Bolivia's Tuichi River.
- Grey and reddish kittens can be born in the same litter.

### Concise site-ready summary
A long, low, otter-shaped wild cat of the Americas, the jaguarundi hunts birds and rodents by day, climbs, swims and leaps 2 m into the air. It is agile and adaptable in habitat use, but at about 7 kg it is a small predator with modest absolute power.

### Rich narrative profile
The jaguarundi does not look like most cats. Its long, slender body, short legs, small flattened head and long tail give it the outline of a weasel or otter, and its coat is plain grey-brown or reddish with no spots. It is also unusual in its schedule: while ocelots and margays hunt at night, the jaguarundi is most active in daylight, peaking from late morning to noon.

It hunts mainly on the ground, pouncing on rodents, lizards and ground birds, but it climbs easily, swims across rivers and can leap two metres into the air after a bird. Radio-tracked males in Belize used ranges of up to 100 km², and the species turns up in rainforest, dry scrub and farmland alike, as long as there is dense cover and water nearby.

In a matchup, the jaguarundi is a quick, versatile small predator. At about 7 kg it is heavier than a house cat and better armed than a fox of similar weight, but it is roughly half the mass of an ocelot, the rival that suppresses it in the wild, and far below the cougar. Its best fights are against small, lightly armed animals; larger cats, dogs and big constrictors are dangerous to it.

- **Versus Ocelot (13 kg; A38 D27 Ag88 St64 I72 Sp72):** the ocelot is nearly twice as heavy and suppresses jaguarundi numbers where they overlap, so the jaguarundi sits below it in every headline category.
