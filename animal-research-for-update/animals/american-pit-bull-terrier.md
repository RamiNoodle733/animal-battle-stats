# American Pit Bull Terrier (*Canis familiaris*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** American Pit Bull Terrier (APBT)
- **Scientific name:** *Canis familiaris* Linnaeus, 1758 (domestic dog; American Pit Bull Terrier breed as registered by the United Kennel Club and the American Dog Breeders Association)
- **Exact animal represented:** the registered American Pit Bull Terrier as described in the UKC standard, not the broad legal or shelter category "pit bull-type dog", which also sweeps in American Staffordshire Terriers, Staffordshire Bull Terriers, American Bullies and many mixed-breed dogs.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (domestic breed).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male in good working condition, **23.0 kg**, **50.0 cm** at the withers.
- **Sex choice:** the UKC standard gives males a desirable weight of 35–60 lb (15.9–27.2 kg) and height of 18–21 in (45.7–53.3 cm); females 30–50 lb and 17–20 in. Males are the larger sex, so a male is used.
- **Population notes:** the breed is bred to a wide weight range, and the UKC stresses balance and proportion over absolute size. The APBT was bred in the 19th century from bulldog and terrier crosses in Britain and Ireland, brought to North America, and registered by the UKC in 1898. It is not recognized by the American Kennel Club. Visual breed identification is unreliable: a shelter study found staff labels of "pit bull-type" often disagreed with DNA breed signatures, so statistics that rely on visual labels do not describe the registered breed cleanly.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- UKC standard: desirable weight for a mature male in good condition **35–60 lb (15.9–27.2 kg)**.
- Wikipedia breed summary (tertiary): males **15.8–27.2 kg**, females 13.6–22.6 kg.
- **Canonical:** **23.0 kg** (about 51 lb), a fit, muscular male just above the middle of the UKC range, neither a small game-bred dog nor an oversized bully-type crossbreed.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high; the registry range is wide and body condition varies.

### Height
- UKC: males **18–21 in (45.7–53.3 cm)** at the withers.
- **Canonical:** **50.0 cm**.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Length
- UKC: the body is "just slightly longer than tall"; the front leg from elbow to ground is about half the withers height.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 53.0 cm**, a derived trunk length (point of shoulder to point of buttock) for the 50 cm male using the standard's slightly-longer-than-tall proportion. This is **not** head-body length.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate; derived from a standard's proportion, not measured.

### Speed
- No instrumented sprint measurement for the APBT was located.
- The breed competes in lure coursing, agility and dock jumping (UKC), which shows athletic running and jumping but gives no speed figure.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- Wikipedia (tertiary) gives an average lifespan of **12–14 years** and calls the breed hardy.
- The breed is banned or restricted in several countries, including the United Kingdom, so the large UK veterinary longevity datasets used for other breeds do not cover it.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 12.0**.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Bite force
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No peer-reviewed bite-force measurement of registered American Pit Bull Terriers was located.
- The widely repeated figure of about **235 lbf (1,050 N)** comes from the National Geographic television programme *Dangerous Encounters*, reported via Wikipedia. It is a television demonstration with no published protocol, sample size or contact area, and it is a force, not a pressure, so it cannot be turned into PSI.
- Real dog bite data are forces: Lindner et al. (1995) measured **13–1,394 N** (mean **256 N**) in 22 conscious pet dogs of 7–55 kg; Ellis et al. (2008) reported roughly **147–3,417 N** in anesthetized, stimulated dogs. Neither study isolated this breed.
- **"Locking jaw":** there is no locking mechanism in the jaw. The refusal to let go is behavioral (Wikipedia, "Pit bull", tertiary). A 2025 3D skull study of 40 breeds found that breeds historically used for bite work do not form a distinct skull-shape group.
- **Weapon dimensions:** the UKC calls the lower jaw "well developed, wide and deep" with prominent cheek muscles; no measured canine-tooth length was located.

### Coat and defensive structure
- Short, smooth, stiff single coat (UKC; ADBA as summarized by Wikipedia). No undercoat, no armor, and little padding over the neck and limbs.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapon and offensive mechanics
The APBT's weapon is the canid bite used as a catch-and-hold. The UKC standard says the dog "must have the functional capability to be a catch dog that can hold, wrestle (push and pull), and breathe easily while doing its job". Its style is to close, take a grip, and then shake, push and pull while holding. Farmers used APBTs as catch dogs for semi-wild cattle and hogs, which means gripping and holding animals much heavier than itself until handlers arrive.

### Fighting history
The breed was bred from British bulldog and terrier crosses kept for bull-baiting and then dog fighting after baiting was banned in Britain in 1835. Dog fighting is illegal, but the breed's history left a strong tendency toward dog aggression: the UKC standard states that "most APBTs exhibit some level of dog aggression". Wikipedia notes that pit bull-type dogs make up most dogs used in illegal dog fighting in the United States. This history is why the report rates its ferocity and persistence high while keeping its absolute power limited by its 23 kg frame.

### Defense and toughness
A dense, muscular 23 kg body carries a short single coat with no padding. Toughness comes from muscle and the breed's persistence under pain, not from structural protection. It is therefore tougher than a lighter wild canid of similar mass but still easy to wound.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The UKC describes a dog that "combines strength and athleticism with grace and agility" and "should never appear bulky or muscle-bound". It competes in agility, dock jumping and lure coursing. It is quick and powerful over short distances but not a cursorial runner like a wild dog or a sighthound.

### Endurance
Catch work and historical fighting both reward sustained grappling effort. No physiological endurance data for the breed were located, so the score reflects a well-conditioned medium dog with anaerobic grappling strength rather than long-distance travel.

### Senses
Ordinary domestic-dog smell and hearing; no breed-specific sensory data were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
The APBT learns readily and competes in obedience, rally and tracking (UKC). In a fight its tactics are narrow: close, grip, hold. It lacks the cooperative hunting behavior of wolves and wild dogs.

### Intraspecific fighting
Dog-directed aggression is the breed's defining combat trait and the reason it was used in dog fighting. Its fighting style against another dog is grip-focused and persistent rather than a series of slashing bites.

### Predator defense
Not a guardian breed. The UKC says it is "not the best choice for a guard dog since they are extremely friendly, even with strangers."

### Aggression and ferocity
Very high toward other animals and dogs when aroused, with a strong tendency to keep holding once committed. The UKC and ADBA standards treat aggression toward humans as uncharacteristic and undesirable; the ADBA counts human aggression as a disqualification.

### Environmental strengths
- Close, confined fights where grip and persistence count most.
- Temperate farms and homes; short coat suits warm climates.

### Weaknesses
- Small absolute mass; large carnivores and ungulates outweigh it many times over.
- Short single coat with no protection over the neck and limbs.
- Grip-and-hold style commits it to close contact and leaves little room to disengage.
- Its short single coat gives little insulation in severe cold.
- No measured speed or bite data to support the viral claims made about it.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** foxes, jackals, coyotes and other light canids; similar-size dogs; medium mammals without armor that can be gripped and held.
- **Mixed:** African wild dogs, dingoes and wolves, where the wild canid's mobility and larger size counter the APBT's grip; heavier guardian dogs like the Kangal.
- **Bad:** big cats, bears, large ungulates, crocodilians, armored or venomous opponents.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Catch-Dog Grip** — takes hold and keeps holding while pushing and pulling, the job the UKC standard says the breed must be built to do. In a matchup it can pin and wear down an opponent of similar size, but it also locks the dog into close contact.
2. **Game Persistence** — a bred tendency to keep fighting while injured and not break off, rooted in its dog-fighting history. It raises Ferocity and Toughness but does not add armor or raw power.

### Unique traits
1. **Bulldog-Terrier Build** — a medium, smooth-coated dog with a wide, deep lower jaw, prominent cheek muscles and thick thighs, bred from bulldog and terrier crosses to combine strength with agility.
2. **Most-Regulated Breed Type** — the target of breed-specific legislation in many countries, while the "pit bull" label itself is applied so inconsistently that DNA tests often disagree with it.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
A domestic breed kept as a companion and working dog, mostly in North America but also elsewhere. It lives in homes and on farms. Twelve European countries, Australia, Canada, parts of the United States and several other countries restrict or ban pit bull-type dogs.

### Diet
Fed by its owners on commercial dog food, meat and household scraps. It does not hunt for a living, though catch dogs are used on hogs and cattle.

### Ecology
As a domestic animal it has no wild ecological role. Its working roles have included catch dog for semi-wild cattle and hogs, farm dog and, increasingly, sport and therapy work.

### Social structure
Strongly bonded to its people and generally friendly toward strangers, according to the UKC standard. Many individuals are intolerant of other dogs, so they are often kept as single dogs or carefully managed with other pets.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding is managed by people. Litters of about 5–10 pups are typical (Wikipedia).

### Conservation
Not applicable as a wild species; it is a **Domesticated** breed.

### Special features
- Wide, deep lower jaw and prominent cheek muscles.
- Short, smooth, stiff single coat in many colors (merle not accepted).
- Muscular, athletic build that should never look bulky or fine-boned.
- Strong grip-holding behavior and persistence.
- Friendly toward people but often aggressive toward other dogs.

### Human interaction
The APBT is one of the most debated dog breeds. A CDC-linked study of 238 US dog bite-related deaths from 1979 to 1998 found pit bull-type dogs and Rottweilers were involved in more than half, while warning that breed is hard to determine and that fatal attacks should not drive dangerous-dog policy alone. A later study of 256 US deaths from 2000 to 2009 found that valid breed identification was possible in only 17.6% of cases and that most deaths shared preventable factors such as isolation from people and poor management. The breed's own standards treat aggression toward people as a serious fault.

### Genuine fun facts
- The UKC registered the American Pit Bull Terrier in 1898, but the American Kennel Club has never recognized it.
- Its UKC standard says it is "not the best choice for a guard dog" because it is so friendly with strangers.
- The "locking jaw" is a myth: holding on is behavior, not a special joint.
- APBTs compete in weight pulling, dock jumping, lure coursing and French Ring sport.
- In 1996 the San Francisco SPCA tried to rename pit bull terriers "St. Francis Terriers" to counter their fighting reputation.
- In a study of 120 shelter dogs, staff labelled 52% "pit bull-type", but DNA tests confirmed only 21%.

### Concise site-ready summary
The American Pit Bull Terrier is a muscular 23 kg bulldog-terrier breed made to grip and hold. It fights with a committed catch-and-shake bite and remarkable persistence, which makes it formidable against dogs of its size, but its small frame and thin coat leave it far below wolves, big guardian dogs and every large predator in absolute power.

### Rich narrative profile
The American Pit Bull Terrier comes from 19th-century British crosses between bulldogs and terriers, made to combine the bulldog's strength with the terrier's drive. After bull-baiting was outlawed in 1835, these dogs were pitted against each other, and immigrants brought them to North America, where the United Kennel Club registered the breed in 1898. A fit male weighs about 23 kg and stands about 50 cm tall, with a wide, deep jaw and thick, well-muscled thighs.

Its fighting method is simple: close in, take a grip and hold. The UKC standard says the breed must be able to catch, hold, push and pull while breathing easily, the job farmers used it for on semi-wild cattle and hogs. That grip, backed by a bred tendency to keep going when hurt, is why the breed is feared in dog fights. But there is no "locking jaw", and the viral bite-force numbers trace back to a television demonstration, not a study.

On the absolute scale, the APBT sits among the medium wild canids. It is heavier and more committed than a coyote or dingo, roughly level with an African wild dog in attack, and clearly below the 50 kg Kangal, Rottweiler and Gray Wolf. Against big cats, bears or large hoofed animals its grip does not make up for being several times smaller, and its short coat gives no protection against teeth or claws.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Identity:** "pit bull" in law, media and shelters is a broad label; this report covers the registered APBT only.
- **Weight:** the UKC range is wide (15.9–27.2 kg); 23.0 kg is a deliberate upper-middle choice.
- **Length:** derived from the standard's proportion; not measured.
- **Speed:** no measured maximum.
- **Bite:** the 235 lbf television figure is a force without a published method; `0.0` means no reliable PSI, not a weak bite.
- **Lifespan:** only tertiary figures; UK veterinary datasets exclude the breed because it is banned there.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus African Wild Dog (25 kg; A47 D31 Ag69 St82 I64 Sp54):** similar mass. The APBT matches its Attack through a committed grip and higher ferocity, edges it in Defense through denser muscle, and falls well below it in Stamina, Intelligence and Special because the wild dog is a pack endurance hunter.
- **Versus Dingo (18 kg; A40 D24 Ag82 St81 I75 Sp58):** heavier and more committed in close combat, so higher Attack and Defense; much lower Agility, Stamina and Intelligence.
- **Versus Kangal (50 kg; A53 D44):** less than half the mass, so clearly lower Attack and Defense despite higher Ferocity.
- **Versus Gray Wolf (50 kg; A55 D41 Ag66 St89 I78):** the wolf is more than twice as heavy and a proven large-prey killer; the APBT is rated lower in every category except Ferocity and ties it in Agility.
- **Versus Honey Badger (14 kg; A42 D45 Sp80):** the badger keeps higher Defense and Special for its loose, thick skin and claws; the APBT is heavier with higher Attack.
- **Anti-inflation check:** no score uses the television bite figure, the "locking jaw" myth, human-attack statistics or dog-fighting anecdotes.
