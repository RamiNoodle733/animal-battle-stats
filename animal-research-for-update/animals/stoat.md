# Stoat (*Mustela erminea*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Stoat (ermine, Eurasian ermine)
- **Scientific name:** *Mustela erminea* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the stoat of Eurasia (the site's photo shows the Japanese subspecies *M. e. nippon*). A 2021 study split the old circumpolar species into three: *M. erminea* in the strict sense (Eurasia and Alaska), the American ermine *M. richardsonii* (most of North America) and the Haida ermine *M. haidarum*. Measurements here favour Eurasian stoats.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male, **0.26 kg** and **27 cm** head-body length, matching the 258 g average male weight given by Wikipedia.
- **Sex choice:** males are about 25% longer than females and 1.5–2 times heavier (females weigh under 180 g), and males take larger prey such as rabbits more often, so a male is used.
- **Population notes:** size varies widely across the range and between subspecies. Wikipedia states that stoats get smaller towards the north, while a 512-skull study found skull length increased with latitude (LaPoint et al. 2017); the trend is therefore uncertain. ADW, whose account includes small North American animals, gives males only 67–116 g.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The stoat is a specialist killer of animals its own size and much larger. It approaches as close as possible, then with great speed grabs the back of the victim's head and neck in its teeth, wraps its body and feet around it and bites repeatedly at the base of the skull (ADW). Small prey die at once; rabbits, five or more times its weight, usually die of shock. New Zealand's Department of Conservation (DOC) notes that even a 3 kg takahē or a 2 kg kākāpō can be killed by a stoat. Its absolute power is still tiny: a 0.26 kg animal cannot hurt a healthy medium-sized predator.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Long, thin and flexible, it runs through rodent burrows, bounds across open ground in 50 cm leaps, climbs trees to raid nests and nest boxes, and swims well. DOC records stoats swimming across water gaps of up to 1.5 km to reach islands. This is elite small-animal agility.

### Endurance
ADW reports travel of up to 15 km in one night, and DOC notes juveniles dispersing more than 70 km in two weeks. Radio-tracked males in Fiordland had range lengths averaging 2.9 km (Purdey et al. 2004). Its high metabolism demands about 50 g of food a day, a quarter of its body weight.

### Senses
Keen smell, sight, hearing and touch (ADW): hares and rodents are mainly followed by scent, insects by sound and fish by sight. Captive and wild stoats spent more time investigating the body odours of other stoats, and even ferret odour, than control scents (Murphy et al. 2022).

### Intelligence and tactics
An opportunist that checks every hole and crevice, stands upright to survey its surroundings, kills surplus prey when it can and caches it for later. The famous "war dance" said to mesmerise rabbits may be linked to infection by a skull parasite (*Skrjabingylus*) rather than being a deliberate tactic.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Male territories overlap smaller female territories and are defended against other males; dominant older males hold territories up to 50 times larger than young males. Territories are marked with urine, faeces, anal drags and body rubbing.

### Fighting and aggression
Hisses when nervous, and barks, shrieks and screeches when aggressive, while releasing musk. DOC describes stoats as voracious and relentless hunters.

### Environmental strengths
- Rodent burrows, rock piles, log piles and dense cover.
- Snowy country, where the white winter coat hides it.
- Woodland and scrub with trees for nest raiding, and water it can swim across.

### Weaknesses
- Minute absolute size and strength.
- No armor; one solid bite or grab from a larger predator ends the fight.
- Very high food needs and a short life.
- Prey for foxes, sables, many raptors and even grey herons.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rodents, rabbits, ground-nesting and hole-nesting birds, and other small animals it can seize by the neck.
- **Mixed:** weasels, rats and animals of up to a few kilograms that fight back.
- **Bad:** foxes, cats, dogs, raptors, and essentially every medium or large predator on the roster.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Nape-Lock Kill** — it seizes the back of the head and neck, wraps its whole body and feet around the victim and bites repeatedly at the base of the skull, letting it kill rabbits and birds several times its weight.
2. **Ermine Coat** — in snowy regions it moults into a pure white winter coat with a black tail tip, then back to brown in spring, hiding it from prey and predators in both seasons.

### Unique traits
1. **Island Swimmer** — stoats have swum across water gaps of up to 1.5 km to reach islands, and young stoats have travelled more than 70 km in two weeks.
2. **Shrinking Skull** — like some shrews, stoats show Dehnel's phenomenon: the braincase shrinks in the first winter and grows back the next summer.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Europe (south to Portugal) and northern and central Asia, including the mountains of Japan, and Alaska; in the broad older sense also most of northern North America. It was introduced to New Zealand in the 1880s. It lives almost anywhere with cover and small prey: woodland edges, moorland, farmland, marshes, tundra, alpine meadows, beaches and scrub. It does not dig its own burrows but takes over those of the rodents it kills.

### Diet and ecology
Mainly small mammals, especially voles, mice, water voles and, in Britain, rabbits, with birds, eggs, fish, frogs, lizards and insects taken when mammals are scarce. In New Zealand national parks, 1,250 stoat guts held birds in 43% and insects in 41%, mice in 19% and rabbits or hares in 18% (King and Moody 1982). It eats about a quarter of its body weight each day.

### Social structure
Solitary and territorial, with males holding larger territories that overlap those of females. Each stoat has several dens, often lined with the fur of its prey.

### Reproduction and life history
Mating takes place from April to July. The embryo then pauses for nine to ten months before implanting, so total gestation is about 300 days and the young are born the following spring. Females can be mated at two to three weeks old, while still blind and deaf in the nest. Litters are large (a mean of 8.8 embryos in New Zealand stoats). Males play no part in rearing the young.

### Conservation
Listed as **Least Concern** by the IUCN because of its huge range. Some national populations are declining: Denmark listed it as Near Threatened in 2019, and an 18-month camera-trapping effort there recorded very few stoats (Konradsen et al. 2024). In New Zealand, where it is introduced, it is a major threat to native birds and is targeted by the Predator Free 2050 programme.

### Special features
- Long, flexible body with short legs and a black-tipped tail.
- White winter coat (ermine) in snowy regions.
- Large musk glands.
- Delayed implantation of the embryo.
- Seasonal shrinking and regrowth of the braincase.

### Human interaction
Its white winter fur, called ermine, trimmed royal and noble robes for centuries and appears in heraldry and in Leonardo da Vinci's *Lady with an Ermine*. In New Zealand, stoats released to control rabbits have devastated native birds such as kiwi, mohua and kākā and are listed among the world's 100 worst invasive species; DOC links them to the extinction of several bird subspecies.

### Concise site-ready summary
A slender, fearless mustelid of the northern world, the stoat kills rabbits and birds many times its size with a bite to the back of the neck, and swims, climbs and squeezes through burrows with ease. At about 0.26 kg, though, its absolute fighting power is tiny.

### Rich narrative profile
The stoat is a long, thin hunter built to follow rodents underground. It bounds across open ground in short leaps, stops to stand upright and look around, and pokes into every hole and crevice it passes. In snowy regions it turns pure white each winter, keeping only the black tip of its tail, a coat that humans have prized for centuries as ermine.

It lives fast. It eats a quarter of its weight every day, travels up to 15 km in a night, and most stoats live only a year or two. Females are mated in the nest before they can even see, and the embryos then wait nine or ten months before developing. When it finds prey, the stoat grabs the back of the neck, wraps its whole body around the animal and keeps biting, which lets a quarter-kilogram predator kill a rabbit many times its weight.

In an Animal Battle Stats matchup, the stoat is a ferocious, fast and very agile predator of small animals, but it is one of the smallest vertebrates on the roster. Foxes, cats, dogs and birds of prey eat stoats, and against anything larger than a few kilograms its bite and musk are not enough.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 258 g average males (Wikipedia, Eurasian data) versus 67–116 g (ADW, including small North American animals); the canonical 0.26 kg follows the Eurasian figure because the site's photo is a Eurasian stoat.
- **Latitude and size:** Wikipedia says stoats shrink towards the north, while LaPoint et al. found skull length increasing with latitude.
- **Speed and bite:** no measurements; both 0.0.
- **"War dance":** described as a hunting display but possibly caused by parasite infection; not scored as a special ability.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Ferret (1.5 kg; A17 D10 Ag89 St49 I67 Sp57):** the ferret is about six times heavier, so it stays above in Attack and Defense. The wild stoat travels much farther and has seasonal camouflage and a stronger hunting record, hence higher Stamina and Special; the domesticated, trainable ferret keeps higher Intelligence.
- **Versus Mongoose (0.8 kg; A20 D16 Ag86 St62 I64 Sp66):** three times heavier, with thick fur and snake-fighting skills, so it is above in Attack, Defense and Special; the stoat is slightly more agile and ranges farther.
- **Versus Honey Badger (14 kg; A42 D45 St70 Sp80):** both are famously fearless mustelids, but the honey badger is fifty times heavier with thick loose skin, so the gap in Attack and Defense must stay huge; ferocity is high in both.
- **Anti-inflation check:** killing rabbits several times its weight is a proportional feat; Attack stays at 14.0 because the absolute force of a 0.26 kg animal is very small.
