# Giant Forest Hog (*Hylochoerus meinertzhageni*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Giant forest hog
- **Scientific name:** *Hylochoerus meinertzhageni* Thomas, 1904
- **Exact animal represented:** the giant forest hog of the forests and forest-grassland mosaics of tropical Africa, from Guinea to the Ethiopian highlands, not the smaller bushpig or red river hog, nor the savanna warthog.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult boar of about **210 kg**, **95 cm** at the shoulder and **180 cm** head-body length, with full tusks and cheek swellings. This is the median male weight reported in field guides, below the heaviest eastern boars of 225–275 kg.
- **Sex choice:** boars are larger than sows (males often about 50 kg heavier; median about 210 vs 167 kg), carry much bigger tusks and large naked swellings under the eyes, and do the head-ramming fights.
- **Population variation:** the eastern subspecies (*H. m. meinertzhageni*) is the largest; the central *H. m. rimator* is slightly smaller and the West African *H. m. ivoriensis* much smaller, with a top recorded weight of about 150 kg. This is the largest living wild pig on average.

### Tusks, skull and bite
- **Tusks:** both sexes carry sharp tusk-like canines; the sows' are much smaller. Boars' tusks flare outward with a slight upward curve, commonly up to about **30 cm**, with a maximum recorded length of **35.9 cm**. They are proportionally smaller than a warthog's but bigger than a bushpig's.
- **Skull:** boars fight by forehead ramming. ADW notes this often fractures the skull, and when the hollows in two foreheads meet squarely the impact makes a loud report.
- **Snout:** an exceptionally large nasal disc, up to **16 cm** across.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite measurement exists; the tusks slash rather than bite.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Long, coarse black hair that thins with age, a mane of bristles up to 17 cm along the back that stands up when excited, a heavy head and a robust skull built for ramming.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The giant forest hog has two weapon systems. The first is the head itself: boars charge at each other from 20–30 m apart and slam foreheads together, a clash violent enough to fracture skulls. The second is a pair of outward-flaring tusks, up to about 30–36 cm in boars, used to slash an opponent after head pushing. Boars are known to attack people without warning, apparently to protect their group, and have been seen driving spotted hyenas off carcasses. Fights between males that end in the death of one participant are reported as not uncommon.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A heavy but compact pig that moves through dense forest, thickets and forest-edge grassland. There is no reliable speed measurement. Its charge is short and head-first; turning is quicker than a large bovid's but it is not a sprinter.

### Endurance
Where they are not hunted, sounders are active for up to 10 hours a day and travel **8–12 km a day**, alternating foraging and resting. Groups use home ranges up to about 10–20 km².

### Senses
The huge nasal disc points to a strong sense of smell for foraging and detection. No quantitative sensory data were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Group defense is the main tactic. All members of a sounder protect the piglets, piglets can suckle from any female, and the group defends young against hyenas and leopards. When pressed, forest hogs come to bay and fight at the edge of cover, which makes them easier for hunters to kill.

### Sociality and hunting
Sounders of up to about 20: females and young, usually with one old boar. Mainly herbivorous, grazing grasses and digging with its tusks for roots and minerals, but it also scavenges. In Kenya several groups may share an area, while in the Democratic Republic of the Congo groups defend a core territory.

### Fighting and aggression
Two forms of male contest are described: snout pushing, a test of weight, and forehead ramming from a run. Head clashes can fracture skulls, and some fights end in death. The species is more feared locally than the bushpig and red river hog.

### Predator defense
Spotted hyena clans and leopards are the main predators, with lions in some localities; lions and forest hogs are usually separated by habitat. Adults defend piglets as a group and boars will attack.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense forest and thickets, where it can charge from cover.
- Forest-grassland edges in highlands such as Mount Kenya.
- Night and cool periods, when it is most active.

### Weaknesses
- No armor or thick hide; flanks and belly are exposed.
- Tusks are modest for its size compared with a warthog's.
- Short head-first charge; little reach.
- Standing at bay makes it vulnerable to organized predators and hunters.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** single hyenas and medium predators; animals of its own size that must meet a skull-ramming, tusk-slashing charge.
- **Mixed:** leopards and hyena groups; similar-sized herbivores with horns.
- **Bad:** lions, tigers and bears; large bovids, rhinos and hippos with a big mass advantage.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Skull-Ram Charge** — boars charge from 20–30 m and slam foreheads together hard enough to fracture skulls, a clash that can ring out with a loud report.
2. **Flared Tusk Slash** — outward-curving tusks up to about 36 cm slash at an opponent after the head-to-head push.

### Unique traits
1. **Largest Wild Pig** — on average the biggest living member of the pig family, with eastern boars topping 225 kg.
2. **Giant Snout Disc** — a nasal disc up to 16 cm across and big naked swellings under the eyes of boars give it a very distinctive face.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Tropical Africa in a patchy range from Guinea and Côte d'Ivoire in the west, through the Congo Basin, to the highlands of Kenya, including Mount Kenya, and Ethiopia. It uses lowland and montane forest, forest-grassland mosaics and thickets.

### Diet and ecology
Mainly a grazer of grasses that also digs with its tusks for roots and minerals and scavenges carcasses. It is found at low densities of about 0.4–2.6 animals per km², but prime habitat can hold more than 10 per km².

### Social structure
Sounders of up to 20: related females and young, typically with one old boar; boars may also form bachelor groups. Females leave to give birth and return with piglets about a week later, and every adult helps protect the young.

### Reproduction and life history
A mating pair leaves the sounder for a time. Piglets can nurse from any female in the group. Average wild life is about five years, with a recorded maximum of 18.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List, but hunted for bushmeat across most of its range. It is so elusive that some large parks, such as Comoé in Côte d'Ivoire, have never surveyed it systematically. The small West African population has low genetic diversity. As one of Africa's four wild pig genera, it is studied as a possible host in African swine fever cycles.

### Special features
- Largest living wild pig.
- Outward-flaring tusks, up to 35.9 cm in boars.
- Forehead ramming that can fracture skulls.
- Nasal disc up to 16 cm across.
- Naked swellings under the eyes of boars, with scent glands in the cheek pads.

### Human interaction
It is feared more than other African forest pigs because boars can attack without warning. It is hunted for meat and is easily tamed in captivity, but it has never been domesticated. It was only described scientifically in 1904.

### Genuine fun facts
- The giant forest hog is the largest wild pig in the world.
- Boars charge from 20–30 m apart and crash heads like wild sheep.
- A forehead clash can fracture a skull and makes a loud crack when the skull hollows meet squarely.
- Its snout disc can be 16 cm across.
- Piglets can suckle from any female in the sounder.
- Giant forest hogs have been seen driving spotted hyenas away from carcasses.
- Science did not describe the species until 1904.

### Concise site-ready summary
The giant forest hog is the world's largest wild pig, a 210 kg shaggy black boar of African forests that fights by ramming heads hard enough to crack skulls and slashing with flared tusks. It is short-ranged and unarmored, but its temper and willingness to charge without warning make it far more dangerous than its size suggests.

### Rich narrative profile
A giant forest hog boar is a heavy, shaggy black pig of around 210 kg, with a broad snout disc up to 16 cm across, large naked swellings beneath the eyes and tusks that flare out to the sides. It lives in family sounders in the forests and forest edges of tropical Africa, mostly active at night, travelling up to a dozen kilometres a day to graze, root and scavenge.

Its fighting style is brutal. Rival boars line up 20 or 30 metres apart, charge and slam foreheads together; the clash can fracture skulls, and some fights end in death. The same temper is turned on threats: boars have been known to charge people without warning and to push spotted hyenas off carcasses. The whole sounder defends its piglets, but leopards and hyena clans still take forest hogs, and hunters exploit their habit of turning to fight at the edge of cover.

On the ABS scale the giant forest hog is a mid-weight brawler with high ferocity. It is heavier and far more aggressive than most forest herbivores of similar mass, such as the okapi, but lacks the armor, bulk and long weapons of true heavyweights. Its Attack reflects real danger at close range, while its modest Defense and Agility reflect a body built for a short, head-first charge.

- **Mass:** lower bound differs (100 vs 180 kg) because West African animals are much smaller; 210 kg follows the eastern-leaning male median.

- **Aggression claims:** attacks without warning and fatal male fights rest on field-guide summaries rather than systematic studies.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Warthog and Wild Boar:** neither has a research report yet; their live values (Warthog A31.5 D71.9, Wild Boar A75.4 D71.4) are legacy placeholders and are not used as anchors. A 210 kg giant forest hog is two to three times the mass of a typical warthog or wild boar and should end up above both in Size and Raw Power, with the warthog keeping longer tusks for its size.
- **Versus Okapi (275 kg; A53 D44) and Bongo (300 kg; A59 D49):** similar-mass forest herbivores. The forest hog's ramming and tusks and much higher Ferocity (70.0) give slightly higher Attack than the okapi, near the horned bongo, with comparable Defense.
- **Versus Hyena (75 kg; A61 D53):** forest hogs drive single hyenas off carcasses, but hyena clans kill them. The hyena keeps higher Attack for its bite and higher Intelligence and Stamina for its clan hunting.
- **Versus Pygmy Hippopotamus (250 kg; A50 D52):** a similar-sized forest animal; the forest hog is more aggressive and better armed (Attack 56.0 vs 50.0), while the pygmy hippo's thick dermis gives it slightly higher Defense.
