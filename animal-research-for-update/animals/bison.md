# Bison

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Bison / American bison
- **Scientific name:** *Bison bison*
- **Canonical taxon:** American bison, with the Plains bison (*B. b. bison*) as the practical reference form rather than the larger northern wood-bison extreme.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical specimen:** Representative healthy mature adult bull in prime breeding condition, approximately 6–10 years old. Adult males are the primary fighting sex and are roughly twice the mass of cows in Yellowstone references.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Strong. Yellowstone reports bulls up to about 900 kg versus cows around 500 kg. Smithsonian gives mature bulls 816–1,088 kg and 2.7–3.8 m long. Adult bulls also have broader shoulders, heavier forequarters, thicker frontal hair/beard, and slightly longer horns.
- **Population variation:** Plains and wood bison differ. Wood bison are generally larger and differently proportioned. The canonical ABS animal should not silently use a record wood-bison specimen while labeling it simply “Bison.”

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
Authoritative sources vary with population and whether they describe typical or maximum adults. Yellowstone uses approximately 900 kg for a bull and NPS gives roughly 907–998 kg for adult bulls. Smithsonian reports 816–1,088 kg. Texas Tech gives 700–1,000 kg for bulls. A **900.0 kg** canonical bull is therefore defensible, representative of a large mature wild male without selecting a record specimen.

### Height
NPS gives adult males about 5.5–6 ft (roughly 168–183 cm) and Yellowstone about 6 ft at the hump. Smithsonian gives mature bulls up to about 1.9 m. **180.0 cm at the hump** is recommended.

### Length
Smithsonian reports 2.7–3.8 m for mature bulls; Animal Diversity Web gives 3.6–3.8 m for males, while Texas Tech reports males approaching 3.4 m. These definitions likely differ in total-length conventions. **340.0 cm** is recommended as a conservative representative total/body-length field rather than using the upper extreme.

### Speed
NPS sources report 30–40 mph depending on page, with Yellowstone specifically stating up to 35 mph (55 km/h) and emphasizing rapid pivoting. These are agency natural-history values rather than instrumented maximum-speed trials. For the site, **15.3 m/s (55 km/h)** is the best-supported conservative canonical maximum, while the 17.9 m/s / 40 mph NPS claim should remain a documented higher secondary claim rather than the canonical value.

### Lifespan
NPS generally reports **12–20 years** in the wild, with 12–15 common and a few reaching about 20. Smithsonian gives 15–20 years. **15.0 years** is recommended as a representative wild lifespan rather than maximum longevity.

### Bite force
No defensible species-specific bite-pressure measurement was found. Bison are grazing bovids and their combat system is not bite-centered. **`bite_force_psi: 0.0`** should mean no reliable ABS value, not literally zero bite force.

### Horns and other weapons
Both sexes carry permanent, short, sharp, hollow horns. Zoo Idaho reports horn length around **61 cm**, while a University of Idaho paleontological comparison cites a maximum modern *Bison bison* horn-core length of 270 mm, illustrating that horn-core length and complete horn length are not interchangeable. The research file therefore does not promote one horn measurement as a universal canonical field. Combat also uses the enormous head, neck and shoulder complex as a ramming/shoving system.

### Defensive structures
Bison lack armor, but a bull combines very high mass, thick skin, dense winter underfur and guard hairs, fat, a huge skull, powerful neck/shoulder musculature and a robust bovine skeleton. Yellowstone notes thick skin, underfur, long guard hairs and fat as winter insulation. These features provide bulk and toughness, not armor-like immunity.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons and offensive mechanics
A bull's primary system is a mass-backed head-and-horn charge. At roughly 900 kg, even without inventing a collision-force number, its momentum is enormous relative to medium animals. Short pointed horns add penetrating and hooking potential at close range, while the massive head and shoulder complex supports pushing, tossing and ramming. During the rut, bulls compete directly for access to cows, so the canonical male is biologically built for dangerous intraspecific contests.

### Defensive adaptations and durability
The greatest defense is scale. Healthy adults are relatively safe from natural predation, with ADW and Smithsonian noting that predation disproportionately affects calves, weak, old or ill animals. Dense hair, thick skin, fat and heavy musculoskeletal construction add resilience. However, this is not dermal armor: vulnerable soft tissue, limbs and hindquarters remain attackable.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Bison are much faster and more agile than their silhouette suggests. Yellowstone reports roughly 35 mph, rapid pivoting, strong swimming and the ability to jump objects around 1.5 m high. Their turning ability is especially important against predators targeting the hindquarters. Agility should therefore be high for megafauna but not confused with the fine evasive control of small carnivores or aerial animals.

### Endurance and stamina
Bison spend roughly 9–11 hours per day foraging, migrate and move seasonally in free-ranging systems, survive severe winters, clear snow with their heads, and maintain a large thermoregulatory body through harsh conditions. They are not specialized long-distance cursorial pursuit hunters, so Stamina should be strong rather than elite.

### Senses
Yellowstone describes excellent hearing, vision and smell. Broad situational awareness helps detect predators and rival bulls, but there is no unusual sensory mechanism warranting specialist-level scores comparable with echolocators or highly specialized raptors.

### Intelligence and tactics
Bison show social organization, dominance, mate tending, threat displays and adaptive antipredator responses. Bulls during rut use displays and direct competition. This supports functional tactical behavior but not evidence for exceptional problem-solving comparable with primates, cetaceans or corvids.

### Hunting behavior
None. Bison are herbivorous grazers. Offensive ratings derive from defense, rivalry and natural weapon use rather than predation.

### Intraspecific fighting
This is central to the canonical male. Bulls congregate during the breeding season, bellow, display dominance, form temporary tending bonds with cows and compete with other males. Prime male breeding activity often begins around age six, when mass and competitive ability are sufficient.

### Predator defense
Adult size, horns, rapid pivoting, group presence and willingness to charge make a healthy bull an extremely difficult target. Wolves can prey on bison, but healthy adults are much safer than calves or compromised individuals.

### Aggression / ferocity
Bison are not indiscriminately aggressive, but they are dangerous when threatened and bulls become especially combative around the rut. NPS warns of bluff charging, pawing, bellowing, snorting and real charges.

### Environmental strengths
- Open prairie and grassland give room for acceleration and threat detection.
- Cold and snow favor the bison's insulation and snow-clearing adaptations.
- Strong swimming broadens mobility.
- Large body size makes rough contact with smaller opponents disproportionately dangerous.

### Environmental limitations
- Dense obstacles reduce charge lanes.
- Heat increases the cost of the thick forebody coat and huge mass.
- Steep technical terrain favors more sure-footed mountain specialists.
- The animal is terrestrial and cannot meaningfully fight aquatic or aerial specialists outside accessible contact.

### Major weaknesses
- No true armor or shell.
- Hindquarters and legs are less massively protected than the forequarters.
- Horns are short compared with long-horned bovids and do not create a large standoff distance.
- Large turning radius and inertia still constrain fine maneuvering despite excellent agility for its size.
- Herbivore combat repertoire is direct and comparatively predictable.

### Matchup archetypes
**Strong against:** small and medium terrestrial animals that must enter close range; lightly built pursuit predators; opponents unable to evade or withstand a charge; many similarly sized herbivores lacking comparable forequarter mass.

**Weak or less favorable against:** giant animals with substantially greater mass, heavily armored opponents, large predators able to repeatedly attack vulnerable rear/side regions without accepting frontal contact, and opponents whose venom/electric/other special mechanisms bypass bulk.

## 7. Abilities and traits

### Proposed special abilities, exactly 2
1. **Prairie Juggernaut** — A near-tonne bull can accelerate to surprisingly high speed and deliver a horned, head-first charge backed by immense forequarter mass. Matchup value is highest against opponents forced to contest frontal space.
2. **Blizzard Breaker** — The shoulder hump and powerful neck musculature let bison sweep snow aside to reach forage, while thick skin, underfur, guard hairs and fat support severe-winter performance. This is an environmental endurance advantage, not armor.

### Proposed unique traits, exactly 2
1. **Pivoting Bulk** — Despite its size, a bison can pivot quickly, jump substantial obstacles and swim strongly, making the common “slow tank” mental model inaccurate.
2. **Rutting Vanguard** — Prime bulls are strongly selected for direct male-male competition, combining dominance displays, bellowing, tending behavior and willingness to physically contest rivals.

## 8. Expanded profile content

### Habitat
Historically associated especially with North American grasslands and prairies, but the species also used open savannas and habitats from boreal regions to semi-desert where grazing was available. Modern conservation populations are fragmented and occupy only a small fraction of the historical range.

### Geographic range
Historically ranged broadly across North America, from Alaska and Canada south toward northern Mexico and across much of the continental interior. Modern conservation herds occur in scattered protected, tribal and managed landscapes in the United States and Canada.

### Diet
Primarily grasses and sedges, with other vegetation such as sagebrush, forbs and browse used depending on availability. ADW characterizes bison as year-round grazers and reports dry vegetation intake averaging about 1.6% of body mass per day.

### Ecology
Bison are major grazing ecosystem engineers. Grazing changes plant communities, while wallowing creates disturbed patches used by other organisms. Their historical abundance made them a foundational ecological force across the Great Plains.

### Social structure
Cow-calf groups form the core herds, while adult bulls can be solitary or occur in small bull groups outside the breeding season. During the rut, bulls join breeding aggregations, display, compete and temporarily tend individual cows.

### Reproduction and life history
Females generally begin breeding around 2–3 years. Gestation is about 9.5 months and usually produces one calf in spring. Bulls are physiologically mature earlier but often do not become successful active breeders until around age six because competition favors older, heavier males.

### Conservation status
The American Society of Mammalogists reports the IUCN status as **Near Threatened**, emphasizing dependence on conservation programs and the fact that most continental bison are managed in private commercial settings rather than functioning as free-ranging wildlife. Conservation status should not be confused with raw headcount of domestic/commercial herds.

### Major adaptations
- Enormous shoulder hump supported by elongated thoracic processes and powerful musculature.
- Thick winter insulation concentrated strongly on the head and forebody.
- Permanent pointed horns in both sexes.
- High speed and rapid pivoting despite huge body mass.
- Snow-clearing head and neck mechanics.
- Strong swimming ability.

### Human interaction
Bison are culturally and historically central to many Indigenous peoples and became a major symbol of the North American plains. Commercial hunting and government-era destruction drove wild populations close to extinction in the nineteenth century. Conservation, tribal restoration and protected herds have produced a major recovery, although free-ranging conservation herds remain a small part of the total continental population. Bison are also a serious human-safety hazard when approached; NPS mandates substantial viewing distance because apparently calm animals can charge rapidly.

### Strong fun facts
- American bison are the largest living terrestrial mammals in North America.
- A roughly 900 kg bull can run around 55 km/h according to Yellowstone.
- Bison can pivot quickly enough that simply getting behind one is not a safe predator strategy.
- They can jump obstacles around 1.5 m high and are strong swimmers.
- The shoulder hump is not a fat camel-like hump; it reflects elongated vertebral processes and massive supporting musculature.
- Bison sweep their heads side-to-side to clear snow and expose winter forage.
- Wallowing helps shed fur and deter biting insects, and rutting bulls also use wallows in scent/display behavior.
- Yellowstone retains a continuously free-ranging bison lineage that has persisted there since prehistoric times.

### Concise site-ready summary
The American bison is North America's largest land mammal, a near-tonne bull built around enormous forequarter power, permanent horns and explosive speed. Its greatest battle advantages are a devastating frontal charge, high durability and agility that is extraordinary for its mass. Bison are not armored tanks, however: their defenses come from bulk, thick integument and toughness rather than a shell, and their combat style is direct. Prime bulls add a biology of intense rut competition to an animal already adapted to harsh winters, long daily foraging and rapid predator-facing pivots.

### Detailed narrative profile
A mature American bison bull is a misleading combination of dimensions. Its outline looks heavy and front-loaded because it is heavy and front-loaded, with an immense head, a high shoulder hump and a body approaching a metric tonne. Yet Yellowstone reports speeds around 55 km/h and specifically notes rapid pivoting. This changes the battle interpretation: the bison is not merely a stationary wall of flesh. It can actively close distance, turn to keep horns and head toward a threat, jump, and swim.

The forequarters are the key mechanical center. Large shoulder and neck muscles allow the animal to swing its head through snow, and the same architecture supports close physical contests. Both sexes have permanent horns, but the mature bull is the canonical combat animal because males are far larger and direct male-male rivalry is a major component of breeding biology. The horns are short compared with those of some bovids, so the bison does not win through exceptional reach. It wins through the combination of a pointed contact weapon and huge mass behind it.

Its defense is similarly easy to exaggerate. Thick skin, dense winter hair, fat, robust bones and sheer body volume make a healthy bull difficult for predators to bring down. Authoritative natural-history sources accordingly note that adult bison are relatively safe from predators compared with calves and compromised animals. But hair and hide are not armor plating. A large predator that can avoid the head and repeatedly damage legs, flanks or hindquarters still has a route to victory.

The ABS profile should therefore place bison firmly among strong terrestrial megafauna without letting fame or size push every score toward the ceiling. Its Attack and Defense are high because of real absolute mass and weapon delivery. Agility is strong because its acceleration, turning and obstacle ability are genuinely exceptional for an animal this large, but it remains below smaller precision movers. Intelligence is moderate. Special is also moderate because cold-weather and snow-foraging adaptations are biologically impressive but do not bypass normal combat rules in the way venom, electricity or echolocation can.

### Confidence and caveats

- **Identity:** High. Generic “Bison” is standardized here as American bison, *Bison bison*, with a Plains-bison-biased representative bull.
- **Mass:** High. 900 kg is supported by multiple independent authoritative sources and avoids exceptional 1,088+ kg values.
- **Height:** High. 180 cm is central to authoritative adult-male values.
- **Length:** Medium. Sources differ substantially, likely in measurement convention and population. 340 cm is conservative and should be revisited if ABS standardizes total-length methodology.
- **Speed:** Medium. Multiple NPS pages agree that bison are exceptionally fast but disagree between roughly 30, 35 and 40 mph. The Yellowstone 35 mph value is selected as a conservative agency figure, not claimed as laboratory-verified maximum.
- **Lifespan:** High. Multiple authoritative sources converge on roughly 12–20 years.
- **Bite PSI:** High confidence that no value should be promoted from this research pass. No credible species-specific pressure measurement was located.
- **Horn dimensions:** Medium/low for a canonical number. Full horn and horn-core measures must not be conflated.
- **ABS ratings:** Editorial research recommendations with medium confidence pending full-roster normalization.

## 10. Cross-animal calibration notes

- **Versus Bighorn Sheep:** Bison is roughly eight times the canonical mass (900 vs 110 kg) and must be substantially higher in Raw Power, Defense and Attack. Bighorn remains superior in technical steep-rock Agility. This check helps prevent pound-for-pound leakage.
- **Versus Bactrian Camel:** Bison is heavier and more purpose-built for direct head/horn conflict, so Attack 72.0 versus Camel 59.0 is appropriate. Camel's provisional Stamina 90.0 remains far above Bison 74.0 because the camel has stronger evidence for extreme load/dehydration endurance.
- **Versus Beluga Whale:** Similar broad megafaunal scale but incompatible environments. Bison has more dedicated terrestrial natural weapons, while Beluga's Special and Intelligence remain higher due to echolocation and cetacean cognition.
- **Versus African Elephant:** Bison must remain well below elephant in absolute Size/Raw Power and likely Defense because an adult elephant is several times heavier. Bison's speed and pivoting can justify a much smaller gap in Agility than in power.
- **Versus Alligator:** Bison has far more terrestrial mass and locomotor speed, but Alligator's directly measured crushing bite and armored integument justify keeping its Attack/Protection specializations competitive or superior in their relevant dimensions.
- **Normalization watch:** Attack 72.0, Defense 68.0 and Raw Power 78.0 should be revisited alongside Cape Buffalo, Musk Ox, Moose, Elk, Black Rhinoceros and other large ungulates. Do not inflate bison merely because it is iconic or because a 900 kg body makes individual collisions spectacular.
