# Tibetan Mastiff (*Canis familiaris*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Tibetan Mastiff (Do Khyi)
- **Scientific name:** *Canis familiaris* Linnaeus, 1758 (domestic dog; Tibetan Mastiff breed, FCI standard No. 230)
- **Exact animal represented:** the Tibetan Mastiff as a working flock and camp guardian of the Tibetan Plateau and Himalaya, not the heavily coated, oversized "lion-head" show and status dogs bred in China during the 2000s market boom.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (domestic breed).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male working Tibetan Mastiff, **55.0 kg**, **70.0 cm** at the withers.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger. The FCI standard sets a minimum of 66 cm for dogs and 61 cm for bitches; the AKC prefers males at 26–29 in (66–74 cm). Wikipedia, citing AKC, gives males **90–150 lb (41–68 kg)** and females **70–120 lb (32–54 kg)**.
- **Population notes:** there is no single breed-standard weight. Show and status dogs can be far heavier and more heavily coated than herders' working dogs, and abandoned free-ranging mastiffs on the plateau are another distinct population. Domestic dogs were bred from wild wolf stock; plateau dogs, including Tibetan Mastiffs, also carry gene variants acquired through interbreeding with Tibetan gray wolves (section 4).

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapon and offensive mechanics
A single weapon system: a large canid bite on a heavy, broad head and massive neck. The Tibetan Mastiff guards by presence, deep baying barks and charges, and bites intruders that keep coming. It has no grappling forelimbs and no specialized killing technique.

### High-altitude physiology
Genomic studies show Tibetan Mastiffs and other highland dogs carry variants of **EPAS1** (a hypoxia-response transcription factor) and **HBB** (hemoglobin beta) that match those of Tibetan gray wolves and entered the dogs through interbreeding. These variants are associated with functioning in the low-oxygen air of the plateau. In a matchup at altitude, this is a genuine physiological edge over lowland animals; at sea level it offers little.

### Free-ranging mastiffs and wildlife
After the Chinese Tibetan Mastiff market collapsed around 2012, many dogs were abandoned on the plateau. A study in Sanjiangyuan GPS-collared 16 stray mastiffs, analyzed 157 mastiff scats and ran 55 camera traps, estimating densities of 0.34–10.6 dogs per km² (mean 1.6). Local observers reported groups of mastiffs cornering snow leopards to take their blue sheep kills, and snow leopards usually giving up the carcass. This shows real competitive power in groups, not that one mastiff beats a snow leopard in a fight.

### Defense and toughness
The heavy mane and dense coat pad the neck and shoulders, and the frame is heavy-boned. It is still an unarmored dog vulnerable to deep bites and claws.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A heavy, powerful mover built for steady patrol over rough, high ground. Less agile than wild canids or the lighter, more athletic Kangal.

### Endurance
Suited to long cold nights on guard and steady work at altitude. Its hypoxia-related gene variants make sustained activity at 4,000 m and above more practical than for lowland dogs. It is not a long-distance pursuit animal.

### Senses
Canid smell and hearing, used for night vigilance; the breed is known for barking at night when kept outdoors. No breed-specific sensory data were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Historically worked independently with nomadic herders and at monasteries and villages. Independent, territorial and aloof with strangers. Free-ranging mastiffs form groups and use numbers to rob other predators, but in a strict one-on-one matchup it relies on straightforward guarding tactics.

### Intraspecific fighting
Males are territorial and will fight strange dogs; no quantitative data on injuries were located.

### Aggression and ferocity
Calm with its own household, strongly territorial toward intruders. Free-ranging dogs have attacked wildlife, livestock and people, according to news and conservation reports.

### Environmental strengths
- High-altitude steppe, alpine meadows and cold mountain valleys.
- Extreme cold and wind, where the heavy coat matters.
- Night guarding of a fixed camp or flock.

### Weaknesses
- Heavy and relatively slow; poor at pursuit and quick turns.
- One close-range weapon; no armor beneath the coat.
- The heavy coat is a burden in heat.
- A single dog lacks the group advantage that lets free-ranging mastiffs bully other predators.
- Far below bears, big cats and large ungulates in absolute power.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** foxes, jackals, coyotes, lighter canids and small carnivores; lowland animals forced to fight at altitude.
- **Mixed:** a single wolf, a snow leopard, a Kangal or similar large guardian dog.
- **Bad:** bears, large cats, yaks and other large ungulates, crocodilians, armored or venomous opponents.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Thin-Air Engine** — carries EPAS1 and HBB gene variants shared with Tibetan gray wolves, linked to functioning in low-oxygen air. At high altitude it keeps working when lowland opponents tire.
2. **Mane Guard** — the heavy neck and shoulder mane pads the area most canid and felid opponents target, blunting the first bites of a fight.

### Unique traits
1. **Foghorn Bay** — a deep, booming bark used on night guard, a key part of its deterrence display.
2. **Single Yearly Season** — unlike most dog breeds, females come into season only once a year, usually in late autumn, with most pups born in December and January.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Native to the Tibetan Plateau and Himalayan highlands, including Tibet, Qinghai and neighboring mountain regions, where it guarded herders' camps, livestock, villages and monasteries. Now kept worldwide as a guardian and companion.

### Diet
A domestic dog fed by its owners. Free-ranging abandoned mastiffs on the plateau scavenge, take livestock and wildlife, and steal kills from other predators.

### Ecology
In traditional herding, the mastiff guarded livestock and camps against wolves and other threats. Abandoned free-ranging mastiffs have become an ecological problem, competing with snow leopards, attacking wildlife and people, and spreading disease, according to conservation and news reports.

### Social structure
Kept singly or in small numbers as guardians, strongly bonded to its household and aloof with strangers. Free-ranging dogs form groups.

### Reproduction and life history
Females have a single estrus per year, usually in late autumn, even when kept at low altitude in mild climates. Most pups are born in December and January. The breed matures slowly.

### Conservation
Not applicable as a wild species; it is a **Domesticated** breed. Its main conservation relevance is negative: abandoned free-ranging mastiffs are considered a potential threat to snow leopards on the plateau.

### Special features
- Wolf-shared EPAS1 and HBB gene variants linked to low-oxygen tolerance.
- Heavy double coat with a thick neck mane.
- Massive head and heavy bone.
- Single breeding season per year.
- Deep, booming bark.

### Human interaction
Guardian and status dog. In China, a price boom in the 2000s was followed by a market collapse around 2012, leaving many dogs abandoned on the plateau. Free-ranging mastiffs have mauled people and preyed on wildlife. Owners need space, secure fencing and training.

### Genuine fun facts
- Tibetan Mastiffs carry high-altitude gene variants that match those of Tibetan gray wolves.
- Females come into season only once a year, unlike most dogs.
- Groups of abandoned mastiffs have been seen driving snow leopards off their blue sheep kills.
- Researchers GPS-collared 16 stray mastiffs in Sanjiangyuan to study their effect on snow leopards.
- Its bark is often described as a deep, foghorn-like bay.
- Traditionally it guarded nomadic herders' camps and monasteries, often working on its own.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Tibetan Mastiff is a massive, heavily maned guardian dog from the Tibetan Plateau. It fights with a strong bite, heavy bone and a padded neck, and it carries wolf-shared gene variants that help it keep working in thin mountain air. It is slow and unarmored, and it is badly outmatched by bears and big cats.

### Rich narrative profile
The Tibetan Mastiff is the guardian dog of the roof of the world. For generations it watched over herders' camps, flocks, villages and monasteries on the Tibetan Plateau, often working alone and making its own decisions. A fit working male stands about 70 cm at the shoulder and weighs around 55 kg, though show and status dogs bred during China's mastiff boom were often much heavier and more heavily coated.

Its most unusual feature is invisible. Genetic studies show Tibetan Mastiffs and other highland dogs carry versions of the EPAS1 and HBB genes that match those of Tibetan gray wolves and came into the dogs through interbreeding. These variants are linked to coping with the low oxygen of the plateau, so at 4,000 m the mastiff keeps working when a lowland animal would flag. Its heavy mane is the other practical asset, padding the neck that wolves and other dogs aim for.

In a fight it is a straightforward guardian: deep bays, a charge, and a heavy bite. It is less agile than the Kangal and far less athletic than a wild wolf, so it wins by mass and persistence, not speed. Groups of abandoned mastiffs have driven snow leopards off their kills, but that is teamwork against a smaller cat. On its own, it is a strong medium-sized fighter that stays well below bears, lions and tigers.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Gray Wolf (50 kg; A55 D41 Ag66 St89 I78):** similar mass; the mastiff gets higher Defense for mane, bone and slightly greater mass, but lower Attack, much lower Agility and Stamina, and lower Intelligence than a wild pursuit hunter.

- **Versus Dingo (18 kg; A40 D24 Ag82):** the mastiff is three times heavier and far stronger, far less agile.
