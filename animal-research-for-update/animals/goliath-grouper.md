# Goliath Grouper (*Epinephelus itajara*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Goliath grouper (Atlantic goliath grouper; formerly called jewfish)
- **Scientific name:** *Epinephelus itajara* (Lichtenstein, 1822)
- **Exact animal represented:** the Atlantic goliath grouper of the western and eastern Atlantic, the largest grouper in the Atlantic Ocean. Eastern Pacific goliath groupers, often treated as a separate species (*E. quinquefasciatus*), are not the represented form.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult, **200 cm** total length and **150 kg**, a large adult of the kind seen on spawning aggregation sites, rather than the 2.5 m, 363–455 kg maxima.
- **Sex choice:** sex is left unspecified. Males mature at about 110–115 cm and 4–6 years, females at about 120–135 cm and 6–7 years (Florida Museum), but no reliable adult size difference by sex was found.
- **Population notes:** numbers collapsed from fishing, especially at spawning aggregations, and U.S. harvest was banned from 1990. Populations in Florida have recovered in part, while red tides, cold snaps and loss of mangrove nursery habitat remain threats.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- NOAA Fisheries and the Florida Museum: up to **800 lb (363 kg)**; FishBase published weight **455 kg**; Florida hook-and-line record **309 kg** (680 lb).
- Peer-reviewed summaries describe the species as reaching over 2 m and 400 kg (Mar. Environ. Res. 2023; J. Ethnobiol. Ethnomed. 2014).
- FishBase's Bayesian length-weight relationship (W = 0.01072 × TL^3.04) gives about 106 kg at 200 cm, but it underestimates the heaviest fish (about 209 kg at 250 cm versus the 363 kg maximum). Scaling the 2.5 m, 363 kg maximum down to 2.0 m by the cube of length gives about 186 kg.
- **Canonical:** **150.0 kg** for a 200 cm adult, between those two estimates.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate; no weighed series of 2 m wild adults was retrieved.

### Length
- Maximum about **2.5 m** (NOAA "up to 8 feet"; Florida Museum 2.5 m; FishBase 250 cm TL); FishBase common length **150 cm**.
- Juveniles stay in mangroves for 5–6 years until about 1 m long.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 200.0** total length for a large adult.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
- Not meaningful for a fish. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- No measured swimming speed was found. It is an ambusher that catches prey "with a quick rush and snap of the jaws" (Florida Museum).
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0**, meaning no defensible measurement.

### Lifespan
- Oldest verified goliath grouper **37 years**, from a heavily fished population; the Florida Museum suggests they may live to about 50. NOAA gives "more than 30 years".
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 37.0.**
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Bite and dentition
- Three to five rows of teeth in the lower jaw (Florida Museum, FishBase). The Florida Museum notes short, weakly developed canines, while FishBase states that front canines are absent. The teeth seize and hold, but most prey is simply engulfed and swallowed whole.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No measurement exists.

### Defensive structure
- A very robust, thick body whose depth is well over half its length (Florida Museum), covered in scales, with a finely serrated preopercle (FishBase). No armor or venomous spines.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The goliath grouper kills by ambush and engulfment. It waits near a cave, ledge or wreck, then rushes and snaps open its huge mouth; the sudden expansion draws prey in, and rows of small teeth hold it while it is swallowed whole (Florida Museum). Its diet shows what that gulp can handle: spiny lobsters, crabs and shrimps, stingrays, parrotfish, catfish, toadfish, burrfish and cowfish, octopus and young sea turtles (Florida Museum, FishBase, Wikipedia). The Florida Museum records very large goliaths stalking divers and even making unsuccessful ambushes on them.

It has no cutting teeth, so it cannot take chunks out of large animals. Its offense depends on getting the whole target, or a limb of it, into the mouth.

### Defense and durability
Defense comes from sheer bulk. The body is massive and thick, with scales and robust bones and fins. Large adults "likely have very few natural predators" (Florida Museum), although smaller goliaths are taken by barracuda, moray eels, king mackerel, other groupers, sandbar sharks and great hammerheads. There is no armor or venom.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A slow, heavy swimmer built for short, powerful lunges from cover rather than pursuit. It holds position well around wrecks and reefs.

### Endurance
Mostly site-attached, staying around its refuge, but adults travel to seasonal spawning aggregations. Endurance is modest for a fish of its size.

### Senses
Goliaths use each other's booming calls to find one another (Florida Museum), so hearing matters on the reef. Specific sensory measurements were not found.

### Intelligence and tactics
Goliaths defend territories around caves, wrecks and ledges with a clear warning sequence: open mouth, quivering body and a loud, low booming sound made by muscles on the swim bladder, which carries great distances and helps goliaths find each other (Florida Museum). Acoustic monitoring off Florida shows these calls are used both during spawning aggregations and in territorial behavior. Some individuals become familiar with divers, such as "Wilbur" at Boynton Beach, Florida.

### Hunting behavior
A lie-in-wait predator of slow-moving bottom animals, especially crustaceans. Juveniles in mangroves eat mostly shrimp, with crabs and fish (Mar. Environ. Res. 2023).

### Intraspecific fighting and predator defense
Territorial displays and booming are used against intruders; spawning aggregations number fewer than 150 fish (Wikipedia).

### Aggression
Usually docile and curious toward divers, but territorial near its refuge, and very large fish have stalked and made unsuccessful ambushes on divers (Florida Museum).

### Environmental strengths
- Reefs, wrecks, ledges and rocky or muddy bottoms in shallow warm water, usually above 46 m.
- Close-range ambush from cover.
- Murky mangrove and estuary edges for juveniles.

### Weaknesses
- Slow, heavy swimmer with no pursuit speed.
- No cutting teeth; must engulf prey.
- No armor or venom.
- Stays near home sites, making it easy to find and spear.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** crustaceans, small sharks and rays, turtles and fish that come within gulping range.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized fish that are too big to swallow.
- **Bad:** large sharks, fast agile predators and anything too big to engulf.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Gulp Ambush** — a sudden rush and snap from cover; the expanding mouth pulls prey in and rows of small teeth hold it while it is swallowed whole. Spiny lobsters, stingrays and young sea turtles all fit.
2. **Swim-Bladder Boom** — muscles on the swim bladder produce a loud, low rumbling call that travels great distances underwater, used to warn intruders and to find other goliaths.

### Unique traits
1. **Atlantic's Largest Grouper** — reaching about 2.5 m and 363 kg or more, it dwarfs every other Atlantic grouper.
2. **Wreck Guardian** — it defends caves, ledges and shipwrecks with an open-mouth, body-quivering threat display.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Found in the tropical and subtropical Atlantic: from Florida and the Gulf of Mexico through the Caribbean to Brazil in the west, and along the coast of West Africa from Senegal to Cabinda in the east. Adults live on reefs, wrecks, ledges and rocky or muddy bottoms, usually shallower than 46 m; juveniles grow up in mangroves and estuaries.

### Diet and ecology
Feeds largely on crustaceans, especially spiny lobsters, shrimps and crabs, plus fish such as stingrays and parrotfish, octopus and young sea turtles. It prefers slow-moving prey. Juveniles in mangroves eat mostly shrimp. Researchers have suggested that recovering goliaths could help control invasive lionfish.

### Social structure
Solitary and territorial for most of the year, gathering in small spawning aggregations of fewer than 150 fish at traditional sites.

### Reproduction and life history
Males mature at about 110–115 cm and 4–6 years; females at about 120–135 cm and 6–7 years. Spawning happens in aggregations, and there is a short annual larval settlement period, which makes recruitment sensitive to weather. Growth is slow: about 10 cm a year until age six, 3 cm a year at 15 and under 1 cm a year after 25. The oldest verified age is 37 years.

### Conservation
Listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List after earlier assessment as Critically Endangered. U.S. harvest was banned in 1990, and Florida reopened a limited harvest in 2023 after a 32-year moratorium, issuing up to 200 permits a year by lottery. Threats include overfishing of spawning aggregations, red tides, cold snaps, loss of mangrove nurseries and high mercury levels in large fish.

### Special features
- Very robust body, deeper than half its length.
- Huge mouth that engulfs prey whole.
- Three to five rows of teeth in the lower jaw.
- Swim-bladder muscles that produce a far-carrying booming call.
- Mottled camouflage of brown, yellow, grey or green with small black spots.

### Human interaction
Easy to approach, which made it vulnerable to spearfishing. Now a favourite of divers at Florida wrecks, where individuals can become familiar with people. Fishers sometimes blame goliaths for eating lobsters and catches, but an analysis of landing data found the recovering population is not the cause of declining fish and lobster stocks.

### Genuine fun facts
- A goliath grouper can make a booming sound loud enough to travel great distances underwater.
- It swallows most prey whole, including spiny lobsters and young sea turtles.
- Juvenile goliaths spend their first 5–6 years hiding among mangrove roots.
- The oldest verified goliath grouper was 37 years old, and they may live to about 50.
- U.S. waters banned taking goliath groupers for 32 years, from 1990 until Florida reopened a small harvest in 2023.
- A well-known goliath called "Wilbur" at Boynton Beach, Florida, lets divers approach it.
- Very large goliaths have been seen stalking divers.

### Concise site-ready summary
The goliath grouper is the Atlantic's largest grouper, a 2-metre, 150 kg ambusher that guards wrecks and caves with an open-mouth threat display and a booming call. It lunges from cover and swallows lobsters, rays and even young turtles whole, but it is slow and has no cutting teeth.

### Rich narrative profile
The goliath grouper is a heavyweight that prefers to let food come to it. A large adult, about 2 metres long and around 150 kg, spends most of its time near a favourite cave, ledge or shipwreck, its mottled brown body blending into the shadows. When a lobster, stingray or parrotfish passes too close, the grouper rushes forward and snaps open its enormous mouth, drawing the prey in and swallowing it whole.

It defends its home with a clear warning system. An intruder first sees an open mouth and a quivering body, then hears a deep boom produced by muscles on the swim bladder, a sound that carries far through the water and also lets goliaths find each other at spawning time. The largest individuals have been seen stalking divers, although most goliaths are curious and tolerant of people.

In a battle, the goliath grouper is a close-range gulper with a lot of mass. Anything small enough to fit in its mouth is in serious danger, but it has no cutting teeth, no armor and no speed, so larger sharks and fast predators outclass it. Overfished almost to disappearance in the 20th century, it has become a symbol of recovery on Florida's reefs.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 150 kg is an estimate between a length-weight relationship and scaling from the maximum; published maxima range from 363 to 455 kg.
- **Canines:** the Florida Museum describes short, weak canines; FishBase says front canines are absent.
- **Lifespan:** 37 years is the oldest verified from a depleted population; true maximum may be about 50.
- **Speed and bite:** never measured.
- **IUCN date:** sources differ on the year of the Vulnerable assessment; the category itself is consistent.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Wels Catfish (22 kg; A42 D30 Ag50 St55 I57 Sp74):** another big-mouthed engulfing ambusher. The goliath is about seven times heavier and eats lobsters, rays and turtles, so Attack (50.0 vs 42.0) and Defense (48.0 vs 30.0) are higher. The wels keeps higher Agility, Intelligence (learned beaching attacks) and Special (electroreception).
- **Versus Bull Shark (111 kg; A72 D52 Ag73):** similar mass, but the bull shark's forceful cutting bite and agility make it a far more dangerous fighter. Defense is close (48.0 vs 52.0).
- **Versus Arapaima (100 kg; A42 D57 Ag55 Sp66):** a similar-sized giant fish. The goliath's heavier body and larger prey give it higher Attack; the arapaima's armored scales keep its Defense higher.
- **Versus Oceanic Whitetip Shark (150 kg; A71 D53 Ag70):** same mass, but the whitetip is an active, serrated-toothed hunter; the goliath is lower in every physical rating.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "Goliath" and large maximum weights do not raise Attack; a 150 kg representative adult with no cutting teeth stays at 50.0. The booming call is scored in Special, not in Attack.
