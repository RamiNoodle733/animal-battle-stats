# Stork (*Ciconia ciconia*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Stork (white stork)
- **Scientific name:** *Ciconia ciconia* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** the white stork of Europe, western Asia and Africa, the species in the site's photo; the smaller Asian population (*C. c. asiatica*, about 1,450 birds) is not used for the measurements.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male, **3.5 kg**, about **110 cm** from bill tip to tail and **115 cm** tall when standing, with a wingspan near the middle of the 155–215 cm range.
- **Sex choice:** the sexes look alike, but males are larger on average; in captive birds males weighed 11% more than females (Mata et al. 2010). Males also arrive first in spring and claim and defend the nest, so a male is used.
- **Population notes:** most European birds migrate to Africa by eastern (Bosphorus–Nile) or western (Gibraltar) routes, but some Iberian birds now stay all year, feeding at landfills. Bill colour ranges from dull in young birds to bright red in adults.

- No armor. Feathers and a light, hollow-boned bird skeleton. Defense is height, reach, flight and nesting high on buildings, trees and poles.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The white stork's weapon is its long, straight, pointed bill, driven by a long neck. It swallows small prey whole but kills and breaks apart larger prey first (Wikipedia). Prey includes insects, earthworms, frogs, snakes and lizards, voles, moles and shrews, and sometimes eggs, young birds and fish. The bill can deliver sharp jabs, but a 3.5 kg bird has little force behind it and no talons or hooked tip for holding struggling animals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
On the ground it walks at a slow, steady pace. In the air it flaps with slow, regular wingbeats or, far more often, soars on broad wings in rising thermals, circling up to 1,200–1,500 m and gliding between them. It is a capable, efficient flyer rather than an agile one; flapping flight burns about 23 times more fat per distance than soaring.

### Endurance
Endurance is its standout feature. European birds fly thousands of kilometres to Africa and back each year; the spring return averages 49 days and the autumn trip about 26 days. About 530,000 white storks use the eastern route each year. Juveniles flap 23% more than adults and spend 14% more energy in flight (Rotics et al. 2016). Resident Iberian storks travelled up to 48.2 km to visit landfills outside the breeding season (Gilbert et al. 2016).

### Senses
A daytime visual hunter. Storks returning to Latvia in spring locate moor frogs by homing in on the males' mating calls, so hearing also guides foraging.

### Intelligence and tactics
Storks improve with experience: young birds explore new places during migration, while older birds fly faster and more directly and invent new shortcuts, suggesting learned spatial memory (Aikens et al. 2024). They readily exploit new food sources such as landfills and follow blackbuck or farm machinery to catch disturbed insects.

### Sociality and hunting
Gregarious outside the breeding season, gathering in flocks of thousands on migration and in Africa. Breeding pairs nest in loose colonies and forage mostly within about 5 km of the nest.

### Fighting and aggression
Breeding pairs are territorial and defend the nest with the "up-down" display, in which the head is thrown back onto the back and brought forward again, together with crouching and wing-spreading. Adults communicate mainly by loud bill-clattering amplified by the throat pouch, likened to distant machine-gun fire; they are otherwise almost voiceless. In food shortages, parents sometimes kill weak or small chicks to reduce the brood.

### Environmental strengths
- Open grassland, wet meadows, farmland and shallow wetlands.
- Warm, dry skies with thermals for soaring.
- High nest sites on roofs, churches, poles and trees.

### Weaknesses
- Low absolute mass and a fragile, light skeleton.
- No talons, armor or hooked bill.
- Slow on the ground and not built for tight aerial manoeuvres.
- Needs thermals for efficient long flights; long sea crossings are costly.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** frogs, snakes, lizards, rodents, large insects and other small prey.
- **Mixed:** birds of similar size and small mammals that bite back.
- **Bad:** eagles, large carnivores, crocodilians, and anything heavy enough to catch it on the ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Thermal Rider** — it spirals up in rising warm air to 1,200–1,500 m and glides onward, using a fraction of the energy of flapping, which lets it cover thousands of kilometres and outlast most opponents in a long pursuit.
2. **Dagger Bill** — a long, straight, pointed bill on a long neck lets it strike at a distance, stab small animals and break larger prey apart before swallowing.

### Unique traits
1. **Machine-Gun Clatter** — almost voiceless as an adult, it communicates by rapidly clattering its bill, amplified by its throat pouch into a sound likened to distant machine-gun fire.
2. **Rooftop Giant Nest** — pairs build stick nests 1–2 m deep and up to 250 kg on roofs, church towers, poles and trees, often reusing them for years.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Breeds across much of Europe (about a quarter of the world population in Poland), north-west Africa and western Asia, and winters mainly in sub-Saharan Africa, with some birds in India. It prefers open farmland, wet meadows, grassland and shallow wetlands and avoids areas overgrown with tall grass and shrubs.

### Diet and ecology
A carnivore eating insects (especially beetles, grasshoppers, locusts and crickets), earthworms, frogs, snakes, lizards and small mammals such as voles, moles and shrews, with occasional eggs, young birds, fish, molluscs, crustaceans and scorpions. Its nests host many other species, from house sparrows and starlings to kestrels and little owls.

### Social structure
Gregarious: flocks of thousands gather on migration and in winter, and non-breeders form groups of 40–50. Breeding pairs are monogamous for the season and territorial around the nest, nesting in loose colonies.

### Reproduction and life history
The male arrives first and claims a nest. The female usually lays four eggs (one to seven), incubated for 33–34 days; chicks reach about 3.4 kg within 45 days and fledge at 58–64 days. First breeding is usually at about four years.

### Conservation
Listed as **Least Concern** by the IUCN. It declined and disappeared from parts of western Europe in the 19th and 20th centuries because of wetland drainage and farming changes, then recovered through reintroduction and protection; a reintroduction project in Sussex, England, produced the first wild nests there in 2020, the first documented British breeding since 1416. Power-line collisions and changing migration habits linked to landfills are current concerns.

### Special features
- Long, straight, pointed red bill and long red legs.
- Broad wings suited to thermal soaring.
- Bill-clattering communication with a resonating throat pouch.
- Urohidrosis: droppings on the legs cool the bird by evaporation.
- Huge, long-used stick nests.

### Human interaction
Welcomed as a bird of good luck in much of Europe, where it nests on houses and churches. Folklore that storks deliver babies is widespread, especially in Slavic cultures. A stork found in Klütz, Germany, in 1822 with an ornate African arrow embedded in its body (the Rostocker Pfeilstorch, now a mounted specimen at the University of Rostock) was early proof of long-distance bird migration.

### Genuine fun facts
- A stork found in Germany in 1822 with an African arrow embedded in its body helped prove that birds migrate to Africa.
- Flapping flight burns about 23 times more fat per distance than soaring.
- Storks returning to Latvia find frogs by listening to their mating calls.
- A single stork nest can weigh up to 250 kg.
- Storks cool off by directing their droppings onto their legs.
- Older storks invent new shortcuts on their spring migration.
- The oldest known wild white stork lived 39 years after being ringed.

### Concise site-ready summary
A tall black-and-white wader with long red legs and a dagger-like red bill, the white stork stalks meadows for frogs, rodents and insects and soars thousands of kilometres between Europe and Africa each year. It is a superb long-distance traveller, but at about 3.5 kg it is a light and fragile fighter.

### Rich narrative profile
The white stork is one of Europe's best-loved birds, a metre-tall wader with white plumage, black flight feathers and brilliant red legs and bill. Pairs return each spring to huge stick nests on rooftops, church towers and poles, greeting each other by throwing their heads back and clattering their bills in a rattle like distant gunfire.

Its real talent is travel. Rather than flapping across the Mediterranean, storks ride rising columns of warm air, circling up over a kilometre and gliding on towards the next thermal, funnelling through Gibraltar or the Bosphorus by the hundreds of thousands. GPS tags show that young birds explore and waste energy, while experienced adults fly faster, straighter and even invent shortcuts.

On the ground it hunts by walking slowly and stabbing, taking insects, frogs, snakes and small mammals. In an Animal Battle Stats matchup, the stork has reach and a sharp bill that can hurt small opponents, and remarkable stamina, but its light bones, lack of talons and modest weight make it vulnerable to any serious predator.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** ADW's 5–10 kg is far above Wikipedia's 2.3–4.5 kg, BTO's 3.3 kg and the 2.9 kg measured average of rescued birds; ADW's figure is rejected.
- **Lifespan:** ADW's typical 20–25 years sits awkwardly with its own 21% annual adult mortality estimate; 20.0 is used with low-moderate confidence, and the 39-year ringing record is treated as exceptional.
- **Speed:** 15.0 m/s is a GPS-measured mean airspeed during sea crossings, not a sprint maximum.
- **Bill:** no standard adult bill length or force measurement was found.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Shoebill (5.4 kg; A34 D21 Ag62 St56 I55 Sp67):** the heavier shoebill with its huge hooked bill stays well above in Attack and Defense and keeps a higher Special for its ambush-and-collapse strike; the stork far outclasses it in Stamina and is more agile in flight.
- **Versus Flamingo (3.5 kg; A19 D14 Ag72 St68 I57 Sp67):** the same mass. The stork's pointed stabbing bill earns a higher Attack than the flamingo's filter bill, and its long migrations a higher Stamina; the flamingo keeps higher Special for its filter-feeding specialization.
- **Versus Pelican (6.9 kg; A28 D22 Ag72 St84 I64 Sp73):** twice the stork's mass, so it stays above in Attack and Defense, and is rated slightly higher in Stamina and Intelligence.
- **Versus Bald Eagle (5.3 kg; A36 D22 Ag79):** a raptor with talons and a hooked bill, far above in Attack and Agility despite a similar size.
- **Anti-inflation check:** migration distance feeds Stamina and Special only; it does not raise Attack or Defense.
