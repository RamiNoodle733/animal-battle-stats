# Common Death Adder (*Acanthophis antarcticus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Common Death Adder (southern death adder)
- **Scientific name:** *Acanthophis antarcticus* (Shaw & Nodder, 1802), family Elapidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the common death adder of eastern and southern Australia. Other death adders (northern *A. praelongus*, desert *A. pyrrhus*, the Papuan forms and others) are separate species and are not used here. Despite its viper-like look and name, it is an elapid, related to cobras and taipans, not a true adder.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult female, **0.4 kg** and **70 cm** total length. Females grow about 20% longer than males in mean adult snout-vent length (Shine et al. 2014), so a female is used as the representative larger adult.
- **Population notes:** mainland Australian death adders average about 50 cm snout-vent length as adults. South-eastern *A. antarcticus* is exceptionally wide-bodied and has the relatively largest head of the group. Diet differs regionally: south-western animals eat mostly lizards, while south-eastern animals commonly take mammals. The Victorian population is historically recorded but now uncertain.

### Bite, fangs and venom
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-pressure measurement; the weapon is venom through front fangs.
- **Fangs:** longer and more mobile than in most elapids, though far shorter than in large vipers. No precise measurement was located.
- **Yield:** death adders inject on average **40–100 mg** of venom per bite (genus-level figure).
- **Potency:** LD50 about **0.4–0.5 mg/kg** subcutaneous in mice.
- **Composition:** the 2025 proteome found three-finger toxins make up 59.8% of the venom, almost all one long-chain postsynaptic neurotoxin (alpha-elapitoxin-Aa2b, 59% of the venom), plus phospholipase A2s at 22.5%, including a presynaptic neurotoxin complex. It is the first Australian elapid shown to have a cobra-like, three-finger-toxin-dominated venom.
- **Action:** paralysis that starts with drooping eyelids and double vision and can progress to breathing failure. In the Australian Snakebite Project, 12 of 14 envenomed patients developed neurotoxicity, with a median onset of 4 hours; two needed mechanical ventilation, and no patient developed clotting problems. Before antivenom, about half or more of death adder bites were fatal (50% per Wikipedia; about 60% per the Australian Museum).

### Defensive structure
- A short, thick body with smooth scales and a broad head. No armor; camouflage is its main defense.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The primary weapon is a fast strike through relatively long, mobile front fangs, delivering a neurotoxic venom dominated by a single potent receptor-blocking toxin, backed by presynaptic neurotoxins. The secondary tool is the caudal lure: a thin, pale, grub-like tail tip that it twitches close to its head to draw prey within range.

### Offensive mechanics
It hides under leaf litter, coiled, and may wait for days. When a lizard, frog, bird or small mammal comes to investigate the wriggling tail, it strikes, injects venom and waits for the prey to die before eating. Doses of 40–100 mg are large for a snake of this size.

### Defensive features and durability
A 0.4 kg snake with no armor. Rather than flee, it tends to stay put and rely on camouflage, which makes it dangerous to people who step or reach near it. It is fragile against any large animal.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Slow-moving and stout; its strength is the short, quick strike from a coil, not movement across ground.

### Endurance
Very low energy use: it can wait for long periods between meals, and replacing used venom costs little energy compared with digestion or shedding (Pintor et al. 2010). Not built for sustained exertion.

### Senses
Normal elapid vision and chemoreception; detects prey that approaches its ambush site. It is commonly thought to be deaf because it does not flee, but this is a misconception about its behavior.

### Intelligence and tactics
Lure-and-ambush hunting is a genuine behavioral specialization, and it takes a wider range of prey, including fast lizards and warm-blooded animals, than most Australian elapids of similar size. Beyond that, its behavior is stereotyped.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Diet across death adders is mostly lizards (55%), with frogs and mammals about 21% each; larger snakes take more warm-blooded prey, and south-eastern common death adders often eat mammals.

### Fighting and aggression
Not aggressive, but it holds its ground instead of fleeing and bites if touched or trodden on.

### Environmental strengths
- Leaf litter in forest, woodland, heath and grassland, where its banded coloring hides it.
- Ambushing prey that must approach its tail lure.
- Close range, especially against animals that cannot see it.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny, unarmored body and very slow movement.
- Venom takes hours to paralyze large animals.
- Poisoned by eating cane toads; toads also eat young death adders.
- Poor ability to escape once found.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** lizards, frogs, small birds and small mammals that approach the lure or step close without seeing it.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized snakes.
- **Bad:** large animals that can crush or trample it, raptors, varanid lizards, armored animals, and anything that can wait out the slow-acting venom.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Grub-Tail Lure** — it lies hidden under leaves and wriggles its thin, pale tail tip like a grub or worm near its head. When prey comes to investigate, it strikes, injects venom and waits for the prey to die.
2. **Nerve-Block Venom** — a single long-chain three-finger neurotoxin makes up about 59% of the venom, blocking nerve-to-muscle signals, backed by presynaptic neurotoxins. In people, paralysis starts with drooping eyelids and can stop breathing hours after the bite.

### Unique traits
1. **Viper-Shaped Elapid** — with its broad triangular head, short thick body and ambush habits it looks and behaves like a viper, yet it is an elapid whose venom resembles a cobra's.
2. **Stand-Its-Ground Ambusher** — instead of fleeing, it relies on camouflage and stays put, which makes it more likely to be stepped on and bitten than faster, more nervous snakes.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The common death adder occurs over much of eastern and coastal southern Australia, mainly Queensland, New South Wales and South Australia, and more sparsely in the Northern Territory and Western Australia. It is the only death adder in the Sydney region. Its status in Victoria is uncertain. It lives in forest, woodland, grassland and heath, hiding in loose leaf litter and debris.

### Diet and ecology
It eats lizards, frogs, birds and small mammals, using a sit-and-wait strategy with its tail lure. It takes a broader range of prey, including fast skinks and warm-blooded animals, than most Australian elapids of similar size.

### Social structure
Solitary.

### Reproduction and life history
It gives birth to live young in late summer. Litters usually number about 3–20 (mean litter sizes of 8–14 across death adders), with over 30 recorded. Newborns are about 12–18 cm long and fully independent. Facultative parthenogenesis (virgin birth) has been confirmed in captive southern death adders by DNA testing, the first record for any elapid.

### Conservation
Listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (2018 assessment, as reported by Wikipedia). Habitat loss and the spread of cane toads are concerns: toads eat young death adders, and adults that eat toads are poisoned. Field data from northern Australia show the picture is complex; one population of the related northern death adder increased after toads arrived.

### Special features
- Grub-like tail lure used for caudal luring.
- Long, mobile front fangs for an elapid.
- Cobra-like three-finger-toxin venom.
- Broad triangular head and short, wide body.
- Banded camouflage for leaf litter.

### Human interaction
Death adders are not aggressive, but because they stay still rather than flee, people walking in bushland may step close to them. Before antivenom, about half or more of bites were fatal. Today deaths are rare because antivenom is widely available and paralysis develops slowly, although antivenom may not reverse paralysis that is already established. Anticholinesterase drugs can also reverse the venom's nerve block.

### Genuine fun facts
- It wiggles a grub-like tail tip to lure prey within striking distance.
- It is not a true adder or viper but a relative of cobras and taipans.
- About 59% of its venom is a single neurotoxin.
- Before antivenom, about half or more of death adder bites were fatal.
- It may wait in the same spot for days until a meal passes.
- A litter can contain more than 30 young.
- It was once thought to be deaf because it does not flee from footsteps.
- Captive females have produced young without a father, the first virgin births confirmed in the cobra family.

### Concise site-ready summary
The common death adder is a short, thick Australian elapid that hides in leaf litter and twitches a grub-like tail tip to lure prey. Its fast strike delivers a potent cobra-like neurotoxin that can paralyze a person over several hours. At about 0.4 kg and 70 cm it is tiny, slow and fragile, so it wins only when prey comes to it.

### Rich narrative profile
The common death adder looks like a viper and hunts like one, yet it belongs to the cobra family. It is short and wide, with a broad triangular head and a thin tail ending in a pale, grub-like tip. Hidden under leaves, it coils and waits, sometimes for days, twitching that tail tip near its head. When a lizard, frog, bird or mouse comes to investigate, the adder strikes, injects its venom and waits for its prey to die.

The venom is unusual among Australian snakes. Most Australian elapid venoms are rich in clotting toxins, but the death adder's is dominated by a single cobra-type neurotoxin that blocks the signal from nerve to muscle, backed by presynaptic neurotoxins. In people, the first signs are drooping eyelids and double vision, typically about four hours after the bite, and severe cases need breathing support. Before antivenom, about half or more of bites were fatal.

For battle ratings, the death adder is a pure ambush specialist. Its lure, camouflage and venom give it one of the roster's highest Special scores, but it is tiny, very slow and unarmored, and its venom takes hours to disable a large animal. It is dangerous to whatever walks up to it and nearly helpless against anything that sees it first.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Fatality before antivenom:** 50% (Wikipedia, genus) versus about 60% (Australian Museum).
- **Victoria:** the Australian Museum lists Victoria in the range, while Wikipedia says it is no longer found there; museum records describe the Victorian population as uncertain.
- **Strike speed:** "fastest strike of any snake" is a popular claim that has not been properly measured.
- **Venom yield:** 40–100 mg is a genus figure and was not confirmed for *A. antarcticus* alone.
- **Mass and lifespan:** single aggregated figures; the lifespan record comes from very few animals.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Eastern Brown Snake (1 kg):** the brown snake is heavier, faster and its venom acts quickly on blood clotting, so it keeps higher Attack, Agility and Stamina. The death adder's lure and camouflage raise its Special and Tactics above the brown snake's.
- **Versus Inland Taipan (1.8 kg):** the taipan's venom is far more potent and it bites repeatedly, so its Attack is clearly higher; the death adder's unique luring gives it a Special close to the taipan's.
- **Versus Gaboon Viper (7 kg):** the closest ambush-and-camouflage counterpart. The Gaboon viper is much larger with far longer fangs, so it keeps higher Weaponry, Defense and Special; the death adder's neurotoxic venom and quicker strike give it similar Attack and slightly higher Agility.
- **Versus Indian Cobra (2 kg):** the death adder's venom is cobra-like in composition, but the cobra is five times heavier, far more mobile and more active, so it keeps higher Agility, Stamina and Attack.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the high pre-antivenom death rate reflects potency and people stepping near a motionless snake, not combat power; physical ratings stay near the floor for a 0.4 kg snake.
