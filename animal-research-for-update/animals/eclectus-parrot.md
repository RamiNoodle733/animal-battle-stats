# Eclectus Parrot (*Eclectus roratus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Eclectus Parrot
- **Scientific name:** *Eclectus roratus* (Statius Müller, 1776), used here in the broad sense of the pet trade and older literature.
- **Exact animal represented:** the living eclectus parrots of New Guinea, the Moluccas, the Solomon Islands and Cape York, Australia. Until 2023 all living forms were treated as one species, *E. roratus*. The IOC, IUCN and BirdLife now split them into four species: Moluccan eclectus (*E. roratus* in the narrow sense), Papuan eclectus (*E. polychloros*, including the Solomon Island and Australian birds), Sumba eclectus (*E. cornelia*) and Tanimbar eclectus (*E. riedeli*). Most field research, including the long-term Cape York study used here, is on birds now placed in *E. polychloros*. Pet "eclectus parrots" come from several of these forms.
- **Status:** Living; the Moluccan and Papuan eclectus are both Least Concern (IUCN).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **0.45 kg**, **38.0 cm** total length. Females are the fighting sex: they monopolize scarce nest hollows and fight other females, sometimes to the death, to hold them. ADW states the sexes are similar in size, with males tending to be larger, so the female is used without a mass penalty that matters on the roster scale.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** the most extreme colour difference of any parrot. Males are bright green with red flanks and underwings, blue primaries and an orange-yellow upper bill; females are red with blue or purple underparts and an all-black bill. Early naturalists thought they were different species.
- **Population notes:** size varies between island forms; the Australian eclectus is described as the largest subspecies (Wikipedia), and Aru Island birds are often around 100 g heavier than nominate Papuan birds. The Cape York population has a strongly male-biased adult sex ratio (Legge et al. 2004).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The bill is the only weapon. No force measurement exists, but eclectus parrots are mid-sized parrots with strongly hooked bills that open fruit, nuts and seeds. The key combat evidence is behavioral: females that own a good nest hollow stay in or near it for up to 11 months a year and fight off other females, sometimes to the death (Heinsohn 2008 as summarized on Wikipedia). This shows a willingness to fight hard over a resource, not an ability to kill larger animals.

### Offensive mechanics
Fights between females happen at nest hollows high in emergent rainforest trees. A female displays from the hollow entrance, showing her red underwings (ADW). Bites and grappling with the feet at the hollow are the likely means of combat; no detailed description of fighting technique was located.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A stocky, short-tailed canopy flier and climber. Males travel widely to find food; they have been observed foraging over a range of about 30 km² and flying up to 7.2 km to mate with females (Wikipedia, citing Heinsohn). Eclectus parrots rarely cross wide stretches of open sea. Agility is scored good but below longer-tailed, more acrobatic parrots.

### Endurance
Males feed the female and chicks on a regular morning and afternoon schedule for months, which requires repeated foraging flights. No tracking of daily distance was located.

### Senses
Normal parrot vision, including ultraviolet sensitivity. The male's plumage reflects ultraviolet light, making him conspicuous to females that can see it while staying camouflaged to predators such as peregrine falcons that are less sensitive to it (Wikipedia, summarizing Heinsohn et al. 2005).

### Intelligence and tactics
No controlled cognition study of eclectus parrots was located. Their breeding behavior is complex: females mate with several males (polyandry), groups can include several males and females (polygynandry), and several males can cooperate to feed one female's brood (cooperative polyandry). Females can bias the sex of their offspring, and in poorer hollows mothers often remove male nestlings within three days of hatching (Heinsohn et al. 2011). Intelligence is set as a typical capable parrot, below the tested cockatoos and macaws.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Female-female competition for hollows is intense and can be lethal. Males share females because good hollows are so rare. Nest hollows are high, 14–30 m above the ground, because lower ones flood in the rainforest wet season.

### Predator defense
Males rely on green camouflage in the canopy and flight. Females rely on staying inside a high, deep hollow (ADW describes entrances 25–30 cm and hollows 60–300 cm deep).

### Environmental strengths
- Lowland rainforest canopy with tall emergent trees.
- Deep, high nest hollows that are hard for predators to reach.

### Weaknesses
- About 0.45 kg in absolute mass.
- No armor.
- Depends on rare large nest hollows.
- Low breeding success; females often fledge only one chick.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small animals that try to enter its hollow; smaller birds.
- **Mixed:** other parrots of similar size competing for cavities.
- **Bad:** falcons and other raptors, tree-climbing snakes, cats and any larger predator.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Hollow Duelist** — a female that owns a good nest hollow stays with it for most of the year and fights rival females, sometimes to the death, to keep it.
2. **Canopy Cloak** — the male's emerald green plumage hides him from predators while he forages in the canopy, yet its ultraviolet sheen makes him stand out to eclectus females.

### Unique traits
1. **Reversed Colour Pair** — the most extreme colour difference of any parrot: red-and-blue females and green males, so different that they were once named as separate species.
2. **Sex-Choosing Mothers** — eclectus mothers can bias the sex of their chicks, and in poorer hollows they often remove male nestlings within three days of hatching.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Eclectus parrots live in lowland rainforest from the Moluccas and New Guinea to the Bismarck Archipelago, the Solomon Islands and the tip of Cape York Peninsula, Australia, where they are restricted to rainforest in the Iron and McIlwraith ranges (Legge et al. 2004). They stay mostly in the canopy and nest in emergent trees. They have been introduced to Palau, Singapore and the Goram Islands (ADW).

### Diet and ecology
They feed mainly on fruit and fruit pulp, plus seeds, leaf buds, blossoms, nectar, figs and nuts (Wikipedia, citing Birds of the World). Their oesophagus and proventriculus are wide and stretchy, letting them take in large amounts of food quickly.

### Social structure
The breeding system is unusual for a parrot. Females mate with several males, and groups can include several males and several females. Multiple males cooperate to feed one female and her chicks. At Iron Range, Cape York, an estimated 538–596 breeding females shared the forest with 1,059–1,173 males (Legge et al. 2004).

### Reproduction and life history
Females lay two eggs per clutch in hollows 14–30 m up, incubate for about 28 days, and often fledge only one chick. Chicks fledge at about 13 weeks and become independent at about 6 months; both sexes mature at about 3 years (ADW). Females can bias offspring sex and in poorer hollows often remove male nestlings shortly after hatching (Heinsohn et al. 2011).

### Conservation
The Moluccan and Papuan eclectus are both listed as **Least Concern** by the IUCN, though some small island populations are rarer. The Australian population is small (around 540–600 breeding females at Iron Range) and depends on scarce large nest trees, so it is considered vulnerable to habitat loss (Legge et al. 2004).

### Special features
- Most extreme male-female colour difference of any parrot.
- Ultraviolet-reflecting male plumage.
- Cooperative polyandry, unique among parrots.
- Offspring sex control and sex-specific removal of chicks.
- Dependence on high, deep rainforest nest hollows.

### Human interaction
Eclectus parrots are popular pets and do well in captivity, though they were not bred in large numbers until the 1980s. Their feathers are used as decorations by people in New Guinea, and in some areas they are considered crop pests. Pet eclectus are often of Vosmaer's, Solomon Island or red-sided (Papuan) forms.

### Genuine fun facts
- Male and female eclectus look so different that scientists once thought they were two species.
- Female eclectus parrots may fight to the death over a good nest hollow.
- A female can stay at her nest hollow for up to 11 months a year while males bring her food.
- Several males may cooperate to feed one female's chicks, something no other parrot is known to do.
- The male's green feathers reflect ultraviolet light that females can see.
- Eclectus mothers often remove newly hatched sons from poorer nest hollows.
- At Iron Range in Australia, males outnumber breeding females about two to one.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Eclectus Parrot is a stocky rainforest parrot from New Guinea, nearby islands and Cape York, famous because green males and red females look like different species. Females own the scarce nest hollows and will fight rivals to the death over them. In an absolute fight, though, it is a 0.45 kg bird with a hooked bill and no armor.

### Rich narrative profile
The eclectus parrot turns the usual bird pattern upside down. The female is the brilliant one, red with blue or purple, and she is the one who fights. Good nest hollows high in emergent rainforest trees are rare, so a female that finds one stays with it for most of the year, displaying from the entrance and driving off rival females, sometimes fatally. Males, emerald green and camouflaged in the canopy, spend their time foraging and bringing food, often several of them to one female.

Long-term field work on Cape York showed how unusual this system is. Females mate with several males, groups of males cooperate to feed one brood, and mothers can control the sex of their chicks, often removing young sons from poorer hollows within days of hatching. The male's green plumage also reflects ultraviolet light, which makes him bright to females but hard for predators to see.

In an absolute matchup the eclectus is a mid-sized parrot of about 0.45 kg. The female's readiness to fight over a hollow gives it more ferocity than most parrots, but it has no measured bite, no armor and no talons. It is dangerous only to small rivals at its nest.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Taxonomy:** the catalogue name *Eclectus roratus* now strictly means only the Moluccan eclectus; the report covers the pet-trade group in the broad sense.
- **Mass:** 440 g (AnAge) versus 500–600 g (ADW); canonical 0.45 kg for a female is moderate confidence.
- **Lethal fights:** reported in Heinsohn's review as summarized on Wikipedia; the PubMed abstract read does not itself give details.
- **Lifespan:** captive records only; canonical 20 years is low confidence.
- **Bite:** no measurement for the genus; Amazona values are context only.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Cockatoo (0.8 kg; A21.0 D13.0 Ag86.0 St59.0 I94.0 Sp76.0):** lighter, with no measured bite, so lower Attack and Defense; the cockatoo's field-tested innovation keeps it far above on Intelligence and Special.
- **Versus Macaw (1.2 kg; A24.0 D17.0 Ag86.0 I86.0 Sp64.0):** far lighter and less acrobatic; lower in every category.
- **Versus Yellow-naped Amazon (0.5 kg; A18.0 D12.0 I84.0):** similar size; the amazon has a calculated 93.6 N bite and strong vocal-learning evidence, so it sits slightly above on Attack and clearly above on Intelligence. The eclectus gets higher Ferocity because of lethal female fights.
- **Versus Crow (0.5 kg; A14.0 D9.0 I96.0) and Kookaburra (0.5 kg; A18.0):** similar mass; Attack sits between them.
- **Anti-inflation check:** fights to the death between females raise Ferocity, not absolute damage; Attack stays low because the weapon and body are small.
