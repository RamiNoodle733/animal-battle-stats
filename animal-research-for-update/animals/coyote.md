# Coyote (*Canis latrans*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Coyote
- **Scientific name:** *Canis latrans* Say, 1823
- **Exact animal represented:** the North American coyote species *Canis latrans*, not an eastern coyote/wolf hybrid population treated as a separate combat form.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **15.0 kg**, representing a robust western/central adult rather than an unusually large eastern hybrid-influenced animal.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger than females in the species. ADW gives a species mass range of 7–21 kg and explicitly records male-larger sexual dimorphism. Yellowstone and Grand Canyon NPS pages give 11–16 kg / 25–35 lb as a common western range.
- **Population variation:** body size is geographically variable. Some eastern coyotes are larger and can carry wolf/dog ancestry, so they should not be used to inflate the species-wide representative baseline. Pelage varies substantially from gray-brown through reddish tones.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- ADW: **7–21 kg** species range, males larger.
- Yellowstone NPS: **25–35 lb (11.3–15.9 kg)**.
- **Canonical:** **15.0 kg** mature male. This is near the upper end of the common Yellowstone range but well below the 21 kg species extreme.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Height
- Yellowstone / Grand Canyon NPS: **16–20 in (40.6–50.8 cm) at the shoulder**.
- **Canonical:** **50.0 cm shoulder height** for a robust male.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Length
- ADW reports **75–100 cm** body length and describes the tail as about half the body length. NPS Golden Gate gives roughly 3–4+ ft total length including a 16–20 in tail for a local California population.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 100.0 cm** as head-body length, with tail recorded separately rather than silently mixing measurement conventions.
- **Tail:** commonly about 40–50 cm, population dependent.
- **Confidence:** Moderate because institutions differ in whether their published length includes the tail.

### Speed
- NPS states coyotes can run **up to 45 mph**, equivalent to **20.1 m/s**. This is an institutional maximum, not an instrumented biomechanical trial.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 20.1 m/s**, retained with **moderate confidence** and explicitly not treated as evidence of sustained pace.

### Lifespan
- Yellowstone NPS: **average about 6 years**, up to 13 years in the park.
- ADW: maximum reported wild lifespan 10 years in its species account; NPS sources elsewhere give potential 10–14 year longevity.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 6.0 years**, because the field should represent a typical wild life rather than maximum longevity.
- **Confidence:** Moderate; survival varies greatly with human pressure and region.

### Bite and dentition
- Coyotes have 42 teeth, with relatively long slender canines and crushing molars described by ADW.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** The live site’s legacy 153 PSI is not accepted. This research pass did not locate a defensible species-specific pressure measurement with clear methodology and contact area. Do not convert force estimates or generic canid claims into PSI.
- **Weapon dimensions:** no standardized representative canine-length measurement suitable for a canonical field was located in this run.

### Defensive structure
- No armor, shell, heavy hide, or unusually protective tissue. Defense is based mainly on mobility, awareness, fur, avoidance, and moderate canid toughness.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The coyote’s main weapon is a canid bite delivered through long canines and a jaw/dentition system capable of seizing vertebrate prey and processing flesh and bone. Forelimbs can brace and paw, but they are not specialized grappling weapons. A lone 15 kg coyote is optimized for killing rodents, rabbits and other smaller prey, not for overpowering similarly sized robust felids or large ungulates head-on.

Coyotes can take larger prey under favorable conditions, particularly vulnerable juveniles or when multiple coyotes cooperate. Grand Canyon NPS notes predation on elk, deer and pronghorn calves. This demonstrates ecological versatility, but does not justify rating an individual coyote as though it has wolf-level prey-subduing power.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Coyotes are digitigrade, light-bodied canids with long limbs and relatively small feet. Their useful combat movement is a combination of rapid acceleration, running speed, turning, retreat-and-reengage behavior, and terrain flexibility. The NPS 45 mph maximum supports high speed, while movement studies show substantial daily travel. Agility is high but below the most specialized small felids and the cheetah’s instrumented acceleration/turning extreme.

### Endurance
A two-year telemetry study of 14 adult coyotes in Durango, Mexico found males traveled **16.47 km/day** on average versus **12.51 km/day** for females. A Georgia GPS study found lower context-specific daily movement, with resident breeder males around **6.1 km/day** and transient males around **8.0 km/day** during the reproductive period. These differences show that daily travel is habitat and social-state dependent. The evidence supports strong general locomotor endurance, not a near-ceiling 97.5 endurance score.

### Senses
Coyotes use acute olfaction and hearing to detect prey and threats, plus broad visual vigilance. Precise sensory thresholds were not verified in this run, so the score is based on well-established predatory function rather than fabricated detection distances.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavioral flexibility is one of the coyote’s strongest advantages. It persists in wilderness, agricultural, suburban and dense urban landscapes, changes food use with local resource availability, can hunt alone or with conspecifics, and uses family social structure and vocal communication. Recent dietary studies show substantial individual and territory-level flexibility in cities. This supports high tactical intelligence for a carnivore, but not primate-level cognition.

### Sociality and hunting
Coyotes are often solitary hunters even when socially organized in family groups. NPS notes that they may hunt alone or in loose pairs. Reports of coyote-badger cooperative hunting should not be generalized: a 2026 three-year camera-trap study in western Kansas found no evidence of broad-scale coordinated/facilitative hunting in that system, emphasizing regional context.

### Fighting and aggression
Intraspecific disputes involve threat displays, chasing and biting. Coyotes generally benefit from avoiding unnecessary injury. Their ecological success should not be confused with extreme willingness to press a losing fight.

### Environmental strengths
- Open grassland and broken scrub favor detection, pursuit and route flexibility.
- Forest edges and heterogeneous terrain favor opportunistic stalking and escape routes.
- Urban environments demonstrate exceptional behavioral adaptability and resource switching.
- Snow and cold are manageable, but coyotes do not have the large snowshoe-like feet of some northern specialists.

### Weaknesses
- Low absolute mass and limited stopping power against large animals.
- No armor and modest structural protection.
- Bite is the primary damaging weapon, requiring dangerous close contact.
- Less grappling specialization than similarly sized felids.
- Group hunting benefits disappear in a strict one-animal matchup.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** smaller mammals, lightly defended birds, many similarly agile animals that cannot match its reach/endurance, fragile opponents that can be chased down.
- **Mixed:** similar-mass generalist carnivores where bite placement, footing and stamina decide the encounter.
- **Bad:** robust larger canids, similarly sized felids with superior grappling, armored animals, large ungulates, megafauna, and opponents with potent venom/chemical defenses that punish a bite-first attack.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Adaptive Hunt Switch** — shifts between solitary foraging, paired hunting and family-associated behavior, while changing prey and resource use with habitat and season. This increases tactical options rather than raw force.
2. **Long-Range Chorus** — howls, yips and other vocal signals support territory advertisement, mate/family contact and coordination. In a one-on-one battle this is mainly a situational awareness/communication specialization, not a damage multiplier.

### Unique traits
1. **Urban Frontier Generalist** — one of North America’s most successful large-mammal urban colonizers, able to exploit heavily modified landscapes while retaining natural prey use.
2. **Lean Canid Frame** — a narrow, digitigrade, long-legged build emphasizes economical travel, speed and evasive movement over wolf-like mass and overpowering strength.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Coyotes occupy an exceptionally broad range of North and Central American environments, including grasslands, deserts, forests, mountains, agricultural land, suburbs and major cities. Their geographic expansion has been aided by landscape change and historical removal of larger carnivores in some regions.

### Diet and ecology
They are opportunistic omnivores. Rodents, rabbits and other small mammals are major prey, with birds, reptiles, amphibians, insects, carrion, fruit and other plant matter also consumed. Deer and other ungulates may appear as carrion or vulnerable prey, especially calves/fawns. Modern urban diet studies show that anthropogenic food can contribute substantially in some territories, but natural prey often remains important.

### Social structure
Coyotes can live in family groups while frequently foraging alone. Pair bonds can persist for years. Both parents provision and protect young, and juveniles can remain associated with parents long enough for an extended learning period.

### Reproduction and life history
ADW reports seasonal breeding, roughly 60–63 day gestation and an average litter near six pups, with considerable litter-size variation. Adult size and sexual maturity are reached around the first year. Pup survival can be low, contributing to a much shorter average field lifespan than maximum longevity records imply.

### Conservation
The species is widely distributed and not globally threatened. Local mortality commonly includes vehicles, persecution/control, disease and other human-associated causes. Its continued range expansion is a major example of carnivore resilience to landscape modification.

### Adaptations
- Digitigrade, long-legged locomotion for economical travel and rapid pursuit.
- Generalist dentition capable of carnivory plus broader omnivorous processing.
- Acute canid sensory suite.
- Flexible activity timing and habitat use around people.
- Broad diet and social flexibility.

### Human interaction
Coyotes often live near people while avoiding direct contact. Food conditioning can increase conflict. Management agencies commonly recommend securing attractants, protecting small pets and avoiding intentional feeding rather than treating every sighting as aggressive behavior.

### Genuine fun facts
- A Durango telemetry study found adult males averaged about **16.5 km of travel per day**.
- Coyotes can persist in dense metropolitan landscapes while still consuming substantial natural prey.
- Their tail is roughly half the head-body length in ADW’s morphological description and is typically carried below the back while running.
- Both parents help feed and protect pups.
- Coyotes have expanded across much of North America despite intensive historical eradication efforts.
- Badger-coyote hunting associations are real observations in some places, but a 2026 Kansas study found they are not a universal regional pattern.

### Concise site-ready summary
A fast, lean and exceptionally adaptable North American canid, the coyote wins through mobility, endurance, sharp senses and tactical flexibility rather than brute force. Its bite is dangerous to smaller prey, but its modest mass and lack of armor make direct fights with larger predators unfavorable.

### Rich narrative profile
The coyote is a survivor built around options. At roughly 15 kg in the canonical male, it lacks the mass to wrestle with the roster’s heavy predators, yet it combines fast digitigrade movement, strong travel endurance, keen sensory awareness and one of the most flexible behavioral repertoires among medium carnivores. It can hunt alone, pair opportunistically, maintain a family territory, shift diet across seasons, and navigate landscapes ranging from desert to downtown.

That flexibility matters in a matchup because the coyote does not need to solve every problem by charging. It can circle, disengage, test distance and exploit fatigue. Its weakness is the moment the fight becomes a committed grapple. Unlike a cat of comparable size it lacks specialized forelimb grappling weapons, and unlike a wolf it lacks the mass and jaw apparatus of a much larger canid. Its best battles are mobile contests against smaller or similarly light opponents, while armor, large size, heavy grappling and punishing counterattacks sharply reduce its options.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Length:** sources mix head-body and total length. Canonical 100 cm is explicitly head-body; tail should be separate.
- **Lifespan:** potential maxima of 10–14+ years do not describe typical survival. Canonical 6 years follows Yellowstone’s field average.
- **Speed:** 45 mph comes from NPS but lacks instrumented-method detail. Keep 20.1 m/s at moderate confidence and do not use it as a proxy for endurance or maneuverability.
- **Bite:** the legacy 153 PSI is rejected pending a traceable measurement. `0.0` means no reliable PSI established, not “no bite.”
- **Eastern animals:** hybrid ancestry and larger body size in some eastern populations should not silently redefine the representative *C. latrans* combat specimen.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Cougar (70 kg):** Coyote must be far lower in Attack, Defense and raw power. It can retain strong Stamina/Intelligence and high Agility without approaching the cougar’s grappling and killing power.
- **Versus Cheetah (46 kg):** Coyote’s NPS maximum is high, but cheetah retains the elite speed/agility tier because its acceleration, braking and high-speed maneuverability are instrumented in wild hunts. Coyote Stamina is much higher.
- **Versus Caracal (17 kg):** similar mass scale, but caracal receives superior close-combat weapon delivery and feline grappling; coyote receives better sustained travel and broader social/ecological flexibility.
- **Versus Chameleon/Cockatoo:** the coyote’s physical combat ratings remain vastly higher in absolute terms despite those smaller animals having higher Special or cognition-oriented scores.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live Defense 62.1 and Endurance 97.5 are not retained. Ordinary fur does not justify high physical defense, and multi-kilometer daily travel does not place the coyote at the full-roster endurance ceiling.
