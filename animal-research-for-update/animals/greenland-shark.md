# Greenland Shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Greenland shark
- **Scientific name:** *Somniosus microcephalus* (Bloch & Schneider, 1801)
- **Exact animal represented:** the North Atlantic and Arctic sleeper shark *Somniosus microcephalus*, not the Pacific sleeper shark (*Somniosus pacificus*).
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **400 kg** and **4.0 m** total length, a large adult below the 5–6 m, 1,000+ kg exceptional animals.
- **Sex choice:** males are typically smaller than females (species accounts), and the longevity study by Nielsen et al. (2016) sampled 28 females from 81 to 502 cm. Large sharks, over about 2 m, are the ones that eat seals and other high-level prey. A large mature female is the representative combat body.
- **Population notes:** most Greenland sharks observed are about 2.4–4.8 m long and weigh up to about 400 kg; the species can exceed 5–6 m and has been reported up to about 1,400 kg. Six free-swimming sharks tagged in Svalbard by Watanabe et al. (2012) weighed 204–343 kg.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Watanabe et al. 2012: six tagged sharks of **204–343 kg**.
- Species accounts: most observed animals up to about **400 kg**; exceptional animals up to about 1,400 kg.
- **Canonical:** **400.0 kg** for a 4 m mature female.
- **Confidence:** Moderate. No length-weight equation was retrieved; the value sits at the top of the commonly observed range and well below the maximum.

### Length
- Nielsen et al. 2016: sampled females 81–502 cm total length; the species reaches over 5 m and exceptionally over 6 m.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 400.0** total length.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
- Not applicable to a shark. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- Watanabe et al. (2012, Journal of Experimental Marine Biology and Ecology) measured a mean cruising speed of **0.34 m/s** with a tail-beat frequency of **0.15 Hz**, the slowest for its size of any fish measured. Maximum burst speed was **0.74 m/s**, with very low acceleration, much lower than the seals it eats. The authors link this to cold water of about 2 °C.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.7**, the measured burst maximum.
- **Confidence:** High for the tagged sample.

### Lifespan
- Nielsen et al. (2016, Science) dated eye-lens nuclei with radiocarbon and found a lifespan of **at least 272 years**; the largest shark was estimated at **392 ± 120 years**. Maturity is estimated at about **150 years** of age.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 272.0**, the conservative lower bound for the oldest animals, not the central estimate.
- **Confidence:** High that lifespans exceed two centuries; the exact figure is uncertain.

### Bite and dentition
- Upper jaw: about **48–52** thin, pointed, unserrated teeth that hold prey like anchors.
- Lower jaw: about **48–52** broad, square, interlocking teeth with short smooth cusps pointing outward, forming a cutting blade.
- Feeding mechanics: the shark holds prey with the upper teeth and rolls its head in a circle so the lower blade cuts out a round chunk of flesh.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite measurement exists.

### Defensive structure
- Large cartilaginous body with denticle-covered skin; no armor.
- Tissues carry high levels of trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), which stabilizes proteins against cold and pressure. Raw flesh is toxic to mammals that eat it (Anthoni et al. 1991), but this does not protect a living shark from being bitten.
- Most adults carry the parasitic copepod *Ommatokoita elongata* attached to the cornea.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The Greenland shark's weapon is its jaw: anchoring upper teeth and a lower row of broad, interlocking blade-like teeth. Holding prey with the upper jaw and rolling its head, it carves out round plugs of flesh. Large Greenland sharks eat ringed, bearded and hooded seals, as well as whale tissue and large fish, and they scavenge carcasses including polar bears and reindeer that end up in the sea. The problem is delivery: a shark whose top measured burst is 0.74 m/s cannot run down a healthy seal. Watanabe et al. suggested it may instead catch seals while they sleep or rest in the water, approaching slowly and quietly. Its offense is therefore powerful on contact but hard to bring to bear on an alert, mobile opponent.

### Defense and durability
Defense rests on mass, a thick cartilaginous body, and a slow, cold metabolism. There is no armor and no ability to escape by speed. Toxic flesh only matters to an animal that eats it. The shark's long life and slow growth mean injuries are slow to be replaced.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
This is the slowest fish measured for its size. Cruising at 0.34 m/s with a tail beat every seven seconds or so, it has very little acceleration. Agility in any active sense is minimal.

### Endurance
The Greenland shark keeps moving slowly for long periods and ranges from the surface to more than 2,000 m, but its sustained output is very low. It is built for economy, not for prolonged hard exertion.

### Senses
A 2025 Nature Communications study showed that the Greenland shark retains a working visual system suited to very dim light: its retina contains only rod photoreceptors, the genes for rod vision are intact and active, and DNA-repair activity appears to keep the retina healthy over centuries. The corneal parasites do not stop light reaching the retina. Like other sharks it also relies on smell (it is readily drawn to bait and carcasses), the lateral line and electroreception.

### Intelligence and tactics
Evidence points to an opportunistic scavenger and stealth predator. Possible ambush of sleeping seals and a size-related shift in diet from squid in small sharks to seals and fish in large sharks show flexible feeding, but there is no evidence of complex tactics.

### Hunting behavior
Small sharks under about 2 m eat mainly squid; larger sharks mainly eat seals and fish. Stomach studies from Svalbard and Greenland found seal tissue in about a third of sharks and whale tissue in fewer, alongside fish.

### Intraspecific fighting and predator defense
No fighting between Greenland sharks is documented. Adults have few natural predators; orcas are possible but poorly documented.

### Aggression
Generally sluggish and non-aggressive toward divers, though it is a genuine predator of seals.

### Environmental strengths
- Near-freezing, dark and deep water, where its cold-tolerant tissues and dim-light vision work and many opponents cannot.
- Ambush or stealth scenarios where the opponent is resting.
- Encounters where the opponent must stay in contact with its cutting jaw.

### Weaknesses
- Extremely slow and low in acceleration.
- No armor.
- Cannot chase or escape.
- Poor in warm water, which it avoids.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** slow, resting or already injured animals; opponents that come to it in cold, dark water.
- **Mixed:** similarly sized sharks and large fish, which can bite it but also risk its roll-bite.
- **Bad:** fast predators that can hit and retreat at will (Orca, Great White Shark), and any fight in warm, bright shallow water.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Roll-Cut Bite** — thin upper teeth pin the prey while the shark rolls its head so the interlocking lower blade cuts out a round chunk of flesh. It can remove large pieces from seals and whale carcasses once contact is made.
2. **Silent Sleeper Approach** — cruising at about 0.34 m/s with a slow tail beat makes very little disturbance, and researchers suggest this lets it reach seals that are asleep or resting. In a matchup it favors surprise over pursuit.

### Unique traits
1. **Centuries-Old Survivor** — eye-lens radiocarbon dating shows a lifespan of at least 272 years, the longest known of any vertebrate, with maturity at about 150 years.
2. **Rod-Only Night Vision** — its retina holds only rod cells tuned for dim light and stays working for centuries, even though most adults carry parasitic copepods on their corneas.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Greenland sharks live in the cold North Atlantic and Arctic, from the surface in the far north to more than 2,000 m deep further south. They are usually found in near-freezing water and are commonest at several hundred metres depth.

### Diet and ecology
They are generalist predators and scavengers. Small sharks mainly eat squid; larger sharks mainly eat seals and fish. They also scavenge whale carcasses and the remains of land animals, including polar bears and reindeer, that end up in the sea. They are among the largest predators in Arctic seas.

### Social structure
Solitary. Individuals gather at carcasses and baited sites but are not known to form groups.

### Reproduction and life history
The Greenland shark gives birth to live young. It grows extremely slowly and is thought to reach maturity only at around 150 years of age. Very little is known about mating or pupping.

### Conservation
The Greenland shark is listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List (2020 assessment, raised from Near Threatened). The main threat is bycatch in Arctic and North Atlantic fisheries; with maturity at about 150 years, populations recover extremely slowly. The Northwest Atlantic Fisheries Organization adopted protections in 2022.

### Special features
- Slowest fish for its size, cruising at 0.34 m/s.
- Lifespan of at least 272 years, the longest known for a vertebrate.
- Rod-only retina that remains functional for centuries.
- Anchoring upper teeth and a blade-like lower jaw used in a rolling bite.
- TMAO-rich tissues that are toxic when eaten raw.

### Human interaction
Greenland sharks are caught as bycatch and were historically fished for liver oil. The flesh is poisonous raw but is fermented and dried to make the Icelandic dish kæstur hákarl. Attacks on people are essentially unknown.

### Genuine fun facts
- At least one Greenland shark was estimated to be about 392 years old.
- A Greenland shark's tail beats only about once every seven seconds when cruising.
- The fastest burst ever measured was 0.74 m/s, slower than a brisk human walk.
- Most adults carry a parasite attached to their eyes, yet their vision still works.
- Their retina contains only rod cells, suited to near-darkness.
- Their raw flesh is poisonous, but in Iceland it is fermented into kæstur hákarl.
- Remains of polar bears and reindeer have been found in their stomachs.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Greenland shark is a 4-metre, 400 kg Arctic sleeper shark that can live for centuries and cuts round chunks of flesh from seals with a rolling bite. It is the slowest fish measured for its size, so it wins by stealth and patience in cold, dark water rather than by speed or agility.

### Rich narrative profile
The Greenland shark lives slowly. It cruises through near-freezing water at about a third of a metre per second, its tail sweeping once every several seconds, and it grows extremely slowly. Radiocarbon trapped in its eye lenses shows that some individuals are well over two centuries old, and the species may not breed until it is around 150.

It is still a real predator. Large Greenland sharks eat seals, and their jaws are made for it: thin upper teeth pin the prey while a row of interlocking lower teeth works like a blade as the shark rolls its head. Since it could never chase down an alert seal, scientists think it approaches resting or sleeping animals so slowly and quietly that they never wake. Its eyes, though often colonized by parasites, contain a rod-only retina that still works in near-darkness.

In a battle, the Greenland shark is a slow heavyweight with a dangerous bite and almost no mobility. Against a fast predator such as an orca or great white it cannot dodge, chase or flee, so it must rely on mass and toughness. Against anything that blunders into its jaws in cold, dark water, that rolling bite can do serious damage.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** no length-weight equation retrieved; 400 kg is an editorial placement.
- **Seal hunting:** ambush of sleeping seals is a hypothesis from speed data, not a direct observation; scavenging also explains seal remains.
- **Lifespan:** 272 years is a lower bound for the oldest animals, not a typical lifespan.
- **Vision:** older accounts describe parasite-infected sharks as effectively blind; the 2025 study shows the retina remains functional.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Great White Shark (900 kg; A88 D68 Ag73 St91 I70 Sp87):** the great white is more than twice as heavy, far faster and armed with serrated cutting teeth. Every Greenland rating is lower; the largest gap is Agility (12.0 vs 73.0).
- **Versus Tiger Shark (legacy, not yet researched; A87.9 D63.6 Ag85.1):** the live tiger shark values are legacy placeholders. A researched tiger shark of similar or greater mass would still sit far above the Greenland shark in Attack and Agility because it actively attacks large prey at speed.
- **Versus Bull Shark (111 kg; A72 D52 Ag73):** the Greenland shark is nearly four times heavier and edges it in Defense (54.0 vs 52.0), but the bull shark's fast, forceful bite keeps its Attack well above 55.0.
- **Versus Nile Crocodile (400 kg; A89 D80) and Gharial (180 kg; A56):** at the same mass as the Nile Crocodile, the Greenland shark is far below in Attack and Defense because it lacks armor and a fast strike. Attack is close to the Gharial's 56.0, another large animal whose weapon is limited by delivery and prey specialization.
- **Versus Galapagos Tortoise (Ag17) and Sloth (Ag34):** Agility 12.0 is deliberately the lowest among large vertebrates here because the Greenland shark is the slowest fish measured for its size and has very low acceleration.
