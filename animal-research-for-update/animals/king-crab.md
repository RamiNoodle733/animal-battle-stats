# King Crab — Red King Crab (*Paralithodes camtschaticus*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Roster name:** King Crab
- **Resolved taxon:** Red king crab, *Paralithodes camtschaticus* (Tilesius, 1815), family Lithodidae.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy, hard-shelled, fully mature adult male, represented at **3.1 kg** and about **17.4 cm carapace length**. This is deliberately representative rather than record-sized: a 2023 Barents Sea sample of adult males averaged 3.09 kg and 174.4 mm carapace length, while Alaska records include exceptional males to 24 lb (10.9 kg) and nearly 5 ft leg span.
- **Dimorphism/population note:** large males develop proportionally greater carapace width and walking-leg merus length than large females. Adult right chela is usually the larger claw. Growth, legal-size structure and maximum size vary by population and age, so the Alaska record is not used as the canonical animal.

## 4. Combat biology
### Weapons and offense
The primary weapons are the paired chelae. The adult right claw is normally larger and is suited to crushing and manipulating hard prey. NOAA reports that larger red king crabs consume clams, mussels, barnacles, crabs, fish, sea stars, sand dollars and brittle stars, demonstrating useful crushing/manipulation against shelled and structurally resistant prey. The smaller opposite chela provides a second gripping/manipulating tool. The animal can seize, brace and crush at close range, but it lacks long-range offense and is vastly weaker in absolute force than large vertebrate predators.

### Defense and durability
A hard, calcified, heavily spined exoskeleton is the crab's main defensive system. Mature large red king crabs have relatively few predators, according to NOAA, but that protection is state-dependent. After molting the shell is soft and the animal becomes markedly vulnerable until it hardens. The staging fighter is therefore a healthy hard-shelled adult, not a freshly molted individual.

### Locomotion, endurance and environment
Adults undertake annual nearshore-to-offshore migrations. Kodiak males have been reported moving up to 100 miles round trip annually, at times about a mile per day. That supports sustained benthic travel but not fast tactical movement. The crab is built for cold marine benthos and performs poorly in hypothetical terrestrial or warm/dry settings where respiration, support and desiccation become decisive limitations.

### Senses and tactics
Like other decapods, red king crabs use antennal and contact chemosensory/mechanosensory information in benthic foraging, but this run found no strong species-specific basis for exceptional sensory or cognitive scoring. Adults segregate by sex away from mating/molting grounds, while juveniles can form enormous defensive pods. Those population behaviors should not be treated as a multiplier on a single adult male's combat power.

### Fighting, predation and aggression
Red king crabs are opportunistic benthic omnivores/predators rather than pursuit hunters. Their combat-relevant pattern is close-range gripping, crushing and armored persistence. Cannibalism occurs, and large crabs consume other crabs. No evidence located here supports portraying the species as unusually aggressive toward arbitrary large opponents.

### Strengths
- Hard, spined exoskeleton is difficult for small soft-bodied opponents to penetrate.
- Two chelae provide gripping and crushing, with a typically enlarged right claw.
- Broad stance and multiple walking legs give stable benthic footing.
- Long-lived adults can sustain seasonal migrations.

### Weaknesses
- Very slow tactical relocation compared with vertebrate and many smaller mobile roster animals.
- Vulnerable joints and ventral surfaces remain less armored than the dorsal carapace.
- Post-molt soft-shell state is an extreme temporary weakness.
- Marine specialization makes dry-land matchups physiologically unfavorable.
- Absolute mass and force remain modest on the 225-animal scale despite impressive size among crustaceans.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favorable:** small soft-bodied or lightly protected benthic opponents that must enter claw range; similarly sized animals unable to bypass the shell.

**Unfavorable:** fast attackers that can exploit joints, large crushing predators, strong shell specialists such as octopuses/sea otters, and terrestrial opponents in a dry arena.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits
### Special abilities
1. **Crusher Chela** — the adult's typically enlarged right claw provides a specialized close-range crushing and gripping tool capable of processing hard benthic prey.
2. **Spined Carapace** — a calcified, spine-bearing exoskeleton protects much of the dorsal body and makes attacks by small opponents difficult.

### Unique traits
1. **Lithodid Asymmetry** — adult claw asymmetry concentrates the strongest manipulation/crushing role in the usually larger right chela.
2. **Seasonal Seafloor March** — mature crabs perform recurring nearshore/offshore migrations, with Kodiak males documented traveling up to roughly 100 miles round trip annually.

## 8. Expanded profile
### Habitat and range
Native range extends from British Columbia north through Alaska/Bering Sea and across the northern Pacific toward Japan. It has also become established in the Barents Sea after introduction. Mature animals generally occupy deeper continental-shelf habitat, while early life stages use shallower complex habitat.

### Diet and ecology
Large adults are broad benthic omnivores/predators. NOAA lists worms, clams, mussels, barnacles, crabs, fish, sea stars, sand dollars and brittle stars. They can also be cannibalistic. Juveniles face many fish and crab predators; large hard-shelled adults have fewer predators, though octopuses and sea otters remain important threats.

### Social structure and life history
Sexes tend to segregate in deeper water outside mating/molting areas. Females must molt to mate, while adult males can skip molts and retain a shell for one or two years. Sexual maturity occurs around four to five years according to ADF&G. Juveniles can form very large pods, likely as anti-predator aggregation.

### Conservation and human interaction
The species supports major commercial, personal-use and subsistence fisheries. NOAA currently manages multiple Alaska stocks separately; stock condition differs geographically. It is also an ecologically consequential introduced species in the Barents Sea.

### Adaptations
The combination of calcified armor, spines, robust walking legs and asymmetric chelae suits cold benthic life. Molting solves the growth constraint imposed by rigid armor but temporarily reverses the defensive advantage.

### Fun facts
- Exceptional Alaska males can approach a **five-foot leg span**.
- The record male cited by ADF&G weighed **24 lb**, but this is deliberately not used as the canonical specimen.
- The fifth pair of legs is small and normally tucked under the rear carapace; males use these specialized limbs in sperm transfer.
- Adult males can sometimes retain the same shell for **one or two years** by skipping a molt.
- Kodiak adult males have been reported migrating as much as **100 miles round trip annually**.
- Juvenile pods can contain **tens of thousands** of crabs according to NOAA.

### Concise site summary
A giant cold-water lithodid armed with asymmetric crushing claws and a spiny calcified shell. The red king crab is a slow but well-protected close-range grappler whose armor and claw leverage matter far more than speed.

### Narrative profile
The red king crab wins by making close contact unpleasant. A hard, thorny shell shields a compact central body while long walking legs hold a broad stance and paired chelae manipulate whatever enters reach. The right claw is normally the larger adult crusher, a useful tool against the clams, mussels, barnacles and other armored benthic prey in its diet. Yet its reputation as a giant crab must stay in scale: a representative mature male is only a few kilograms, and the spectacular 24-pound, five-foot-span animals are exceptional old specimens rather than the correct default fighter. Against similarly sized soft-bodied opponents the shell-and-claw combination is formidable. Against a large vertebrate predator, the crab's modest absolute force, exposed joints and slow relocation become severe constraints. Its armor is also conditional: after molting, the normally durable shell becomes temporarily soft and vulnerable.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Lifespan:** ADW gives expected 15–20 years, while ADF&G estimates exceptional large animals at 20–30 years. Canonical 20.0 years sits at the overlap and avoids presenting maximum age as typical.
- **Size:** ADF&G maxima are much larger than the adult-male mean in the Barents Sea sample. Canonical mass therefore uses the measured adult-male mean, not the record.
- **Speed:** one mile/day is a travel rate, not top speed. No conversion into a false maximum is made.
- **Claw force:** functional crushing is well supported, but no defensible species-specific Newton or pressure measurement was located. No number is invented.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes
- At **3.1 kg**, this animal must remain far below Honey Badger (14 kg), Japanese Macaque (11.3 kg), Jackal (8.1 kg) and larger vertebrates in absolute Raw Power and Attack despite impressive crustacean leverage.
- Protection can exceed those small vertebrates in local shell resistance without implying superior whole-body survivability against much larger attackers.
- Its Agility must stay well below fast terrestrial vertebrates and agile arthropods such as the Huntsman Spider; slow migration is not tactical agility.
- Stamina is moderate because long migrations show persistence, but low travel rate is not evidence for sustained high-output exertion.
- Special is meaningful because armor and asymmetric crushing tools alter matchups, but neither is rare enough to approach venom/electricity specialists such as King Cobra.
- The old live values, including 11 kg, 36 km/h and 100 PSI, were treated as unverified placeholders and did not anchor these recommendations.
