# Sperm Whale (*Physeter macrocephalus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Sperm whale (also cachalot)
- **Scientific name:** *Physeter macrocephalus* Linnaeus, 1758 (some accounts, including Animal Diversity Web, file it under the synonym *Physeter catodon*)
- **Exact animal represented:** the single living sperm whale species, the largest toothed whale. Not the pygmy or dwarf sperm whales (*Kogia*), which are much smaller separate species.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **35,000 kg** and **15.0 m** long, representing a large bull below NOAA's stated male maximum of 52 ft (15.9 m) and 45 tons (40,823 kg).
- **Sex choice:** sexual dimorphism is extreme. NOAA Fisheries gives males up to 52 ft and 45 tons, females up to 36 ft (11 m) and 15 tons (13,607 kg). Males are also the sex that fights other males, and the ramming-combat hypothesis for the forehead applies to male-male competition. A mature male is therefore the correct combat specimen.
- **Population notes:** females and young live in stable social units in warmer waters, while mature males range alone or in loose groups into high latitudes. NOAA notes that males reach physical maturity at around 50 years of age at roughly 52 ft, so younger adult males are materially smaller than the canonical bull.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- NOAA Fisheries: males **up to 45 tons (40,823 kg)**; females up to 15 tons (13,607 kg).
- **Canonical:** **35,000 kg** for a large mature male. This stays below the NOAA upper figure so a maximum is not silently used as the representative animal.
- **Confidence:** Moderate. No representative (mean) adult-male mass from a primary morphometric study was retrieved in this run; the canonical value is an editorial placement inside the agency range.

### Length
- NOAA Fisheries: males up to **52 ft (15.9 m)**, females up to 36 ft (11 m). Physical maturity in males at about 52 ft.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 1500.0** total length.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Height
- Standing height is not a meaningful measurement for a fully aquatic whale. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- Aoki et al. (2007) tagged 11 sperm whales and obtained accurate speed data from 8 (136 h, 137 dives). Mean speed during dives was **1.77 ± 0.39 m/s**; travelling speed is commonly 1.5–3 knots.
- Aoki et al. (active-hunting study) found mean swim speed near **1.8 ± 0.4 m/s** with bursts of speed at depth; 87% of bursts were turning ("inflectional") bursts and 13% straight-line bursts, and whales accelerated to **more than 3 m/s** during rapid orientation changes.
- Popular claims of 40–45 km/h appear in secondary summaries without an instrumented method, so they are not adopted.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0**, meaning no defensible instrumented maximum, not immobility. Tag data prove routine speeds near 1.8 m/s and hunting bursts above 3 m/s.

### Lifespan
- NOAA-linked summaries give females 70+ years and males commonly around 60 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 60.0** for a male.
- **Confidence:** Moderate; whale age estimation is indirect.

### Bite and dentition
- The lower jaw carries **18–28 functional conical teeth per side**; upper teeth are few, weak and nonfunctional, and the lower teeth fit into sockets in the upper jaw (Animal Diversity Web / species accounts).
- The lower jaw is long and narrow. It is a grasping tool for squid and fish, not a wide cutting apparatus.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No measured or defensible modelled bite pressure exists for this species.

### Defensive structure and head
- The forehead contains two large oil-filled compartments, the **spermaceti organ** and the **junk**, which together make up **up to one quarter of body mass** (Panagiotopoulou et al. 2016).
- Finite-element modelling in that study found that connective-tissue partitions inside the junk reduce bone stresses during an impact, consistent with the head working as a battering ram in male-male contests.
- Thick blubber and huge tissue depth provide passive protection; there is no hard armor.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The sperm whale has two distinct offensive systems. The first is its **mass and forehead**: a 35-tonne bull whose head holds up to a quarter of its body mass in the spermaceti organ and junk. Panagiotopoulou et al. (2016) modelled the forehead and found that its internal connective-tissue partitions reduce skull stress on impact, supporting the long-standing idea that males ram each other. The second is the **lower jaw**, a long narrow bar with 18–28 conical teeth per side that closes into sockets in the toothless upper jaw. That jaw is built to seize squid, fish, sharks and skates, not to shear chunks from large animals the way an orca or great white shark does.

In practice this makes the sperm whale devastating in collision and dangerous at the jaw, but less efficient at killing a large, mobile opponent than the roster's specialist macropredators. Its weapons are powerful but slow to deliver.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Routine swimming is slow and economical: tag studies give mean dive speeds of about 1.8 m/s. At depth, however, sperm whales perform genuine pursuit, with bursts above 3 m/s and sharp turning bursts making up most of the high-effort swimming. Fais et al. (2016) showed that prey capture involves chasing and buzzing rather than the old "acoustic stunning" idea. The whale is therefore more agile than its bulk suggests, but a 15 m body still has a wide turning radius and cannot match dolphin- or orca-grade repositioning.

### Endurance
Endurance is the sperm whale's greatest physical asset. Watwood et al. (2006) tagged 37 whales across the Atlantic, Gulf of Mexico and Ligurian Sea. An average dive cycle was a **45-minute dive followed by a 9-minute surface interval**, individual mean maximum dive depths were **985 m, 644 m and 827 m** by region, and whales spent **more than 72% of their time** in foraging dive cycles. That is sustained, repeated, deep, active hunting day after day.

### Senses
The sperm whale's biosonar is the strongest sensory weapon on the roster. Møhl et al. (2003) recorded on-axis clicks with source levels up to **236 dB re 1 µPa (rms)**, very short (about 100 µs), highly directional (directionality index 27 dB), with centroid frequencies near 15 kHz, and described them as by far the loudest sounds recorded from any biological source. Watwood et al. found that whales click for about 81% of each dive, scanning the dark water continuously.

### Intelligence and tactics
Sperm whales carry the largest brain of any animal by mass (about 9 kg in species accounts). Their social life is organised into vocal clans with distinct coda dialects. Hersh et al. (2022) showed that "identity codas" work as symbolic markers of clan membership across 23 Pacific locations, and that clans exaggerate their dialect differences where they overlap. Against orcas, family units form a coordinated defensive rosette. This is real cooperative, learned, culturally structured behavior, although it is below the orca's documented cooperative-hunting flexibility.

### Sociality and hunting
Females and young live in stable units; mature bulls are usually solitary. Hunting is individual and acoustic: each whale dives, clicks to search, then shifts into a rapid "buzz" to close on a target. Diet centers on deep-water squid, including giant and colossal squid, along with sharks, skates and fish (NOAA). Sperm whales eat about 3–3.5% of body mass per day.

### Fighting and aggression
Male-male combat is supported mainly by the forehead mechanics study; direct observation of full ramming fights is rare. Groups defend themselves against killer whales: Pitman et al. (2001) watched about 35 killer whales attack nine sperm whales off California for four hours. The sperm whales formed a "marguerite" (heads together, tails out), and some individuals left the formation to help pulled-away group members. The killer whales eventually killed a seriously wounded, isolated whale, which shows that the sperm whale is not invulnerable to a coordinated pack.

### Environmental strengths
- Deep open ocean, where its diving and biosonar are unmatched.
- Darkness and low visibility, where most opponents lose their main sense and it loses nothing.
- Long engagements, where its dive endurance outlasts most animals.

### Weaknesses
- Must surface to breathe; a long engagement at the surface removes its depth advantage.
- Slow routine swimming and a very large turning radius.
- Narrow lower-jaw bite is poor for cutting large opponents.
- Coordinated pack attack can isolate and wear down an individual, as orcas have shown.
- Useless in any shallow-water or land setting; strandings are often fatal.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** squid, sharks and other animals of lower mass in open or deep water; any opponent that relies on vision in darkness; animals that cannot survive a full-body ram.
- **Mixed:** a single large toothed predator (orca, great white shark) in open water, where the sperm whale's mass and ram face a faster, better-cutting attacker.
- **Bad:** coordinated packs, and any shallow or terrestrial setting.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Abyssal Sonar Click** — clicks of up to 236 dB re 1 µPa (rms), the loudest biological sound recorded, scan the dark water through about 81% of every dive and shift into a rapid buzz to lock onto prey. In a battle it finds and tracks opponents in complete darkness at long range.
2. **Battering-Ram Forehead** — the spermaceti organ and junk make up to a quarter of body mass, and internal partitions reduce skull stress on impact. A charging bull turns tens of tonnes of momentum into a head strike built to absorb its own recoil.

### Unique traits
1. **Hour-Long Deep Diver** — average 45-minute foraging dives to several hundred metres to about a kilometre, with only 9-minute breaks, repeated through most of the day.
2. **Clan Coda Culture** — sperm whales belong to vocal clans marked by distinct click-pattern dialects that act as symbolic identity signals, and family units close ranks into a defensive rosette under attack.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Sperm whales live in deep water in every ocean, typically beyond the continental shelf and along slopes and canyons where deep squid concentrate. Females and young stay mainly in warmer tropical and subtropical waters; mature males range much farther toward polar seas and return to lower latitudes to breed.

### Diet and ecology
The diet is dominated by deep-water squid, including giant and colossal squid, with sharks, skates and fish also taken (NOAA). An adult eats about 3–3.5% of its body mass per day. Sperm whales are major predators of the deep sea, and their stomach contents have been one of the main sources of knowledge about giant squid, whose beaks accumulate inside them.

### Social structure
Females and young form long-lasting social units that babysit calves at the surface while mothers dive. Units belong to larger vocal clans defined by shared coda dialects, a form of culture documented across the Pacific (Hersh et al. 2022). Mature males leave the family units and live alone or in small bachelor groups.

### Reproduction and life history
Males keep growing for decades and reach physical maturity at around 50 years and about 52 ft (NOAA). Females are much smaller. Long lives, slow growth and long calf dependence make populations slow to recover from losses.

### Conservation
The sperm whale is listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List (Taylor et al. 2019 assessment). Commercial whaling removed very large numbers historically; current threats include ship strikes, entanglement, ocean noise, pollution and ingestion of marine debris. It is protected under the US Endangered Species Act and Marine Mammal Protection Act (NOAA).

### Special features
- Loudest biological sound recorded, used as long-range directional biosonar.
- Forehead of spermaceti organ and junk, up to a quarter of body mass, with impact-reducing internal partitions.
- Largest brain of any animal by mass.
- Lower jaw with 18–28 functional teeth per side and a nearly toothless upper jaw.
- Deep-dive physiology sustaining repeated 45-minute dives.

### Human interaction
Sperm whales were hunted intensively for spermaceti oil and ambergris. Today they are a major whale-watching species in places such as the Azores, Dominica, Norway and New Zealand. Ship strikes and fishing-gear entanglement remain real risks.

### Genuine fun facts
- Their clicks reach up to 236 dB re 1 µPa, the loudest sound measured from any animal.
- The forehead oil compartments can make up about a quarter of the whole whale's mass.
- A typical foraging dive lasts about 45 minutes, with only about 9 minutes at the surface in between.
- Sperm whales spend more than 72% of their time in foraging dive cycles.
- Different clans use different click-pattern "dialects", and clans exaggerate the differences where they meet.
- Males are about three times heavier than females: up to 45 tons against 15 tons.
- When orcas attack, family groups can form a "marguerite" with heads together and tails out.

### Concise site-ready summary
The largest toothed predator alive, a bull sperm whale combines a 35-tonne body, a battering-ram forehead and the loudest biosonar on Earth. It hunts squid in the dark a kilometre down on hour-long dives, but its slow routine swimming and narrow grasping jaw make it more of an unstoppable heavyweight than a fast killer.

### Rich narrative profile
The sperm whale is built around the deep. A mature bull weighs tens of tonnes and a large part of that mass sits in its enormous square head, which holds the spermaceti organ and junk. Those oil-filled compartments shape and focus the whale's clicks into a beam of sound so intense that no recorded animal matches it. With that beam the whale searches black water hundreds of metres down, then accelerates into a rapid buzz as it closes on a squid.

In a battle, those same traits make it a heavyweight with strange strengths. The head is not only an acoustic lens: modelling shows its internal partitions reduce the stress of an impact, fitting reports and theory that males ram one another. A charging bull brings a mass several times greater than any orca and a skull built to take its own hit. Its jaw, lined with heavy conical teeth, can seize and hold but is not designed to carve up large prey, and its routine swimming is slow. Against a small, fast opponent it may struggle to land its strike; against anything that has to absorb one, the outcome is grim.

Its deepest advantage is endurance. Where most air-breathing hunters manage a few minutes underwater, the sperm whale repeats 45-minute dives through most of the day. Add large-brained clan cultures, coordinated defense against orcas and a sensory system that works in total darkness, and it becomes one of the most complete giants in the ocean: formidable in almost any open-water matchup, vulnerable mainly to coordinated packs and to any setting that removes the deep.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** NOAA gives a maximum, not a mean. Canonical 35,000 kg is an editorial representative bull below that maximum.
- **Speed:** secondary sources quote 40–45 km/h, but the only instrumented data retrieved give routine ~1.8 m/s and bursts above 3 m/s. `speed_mps` stays 0.0 rather than adopting an untraceable maximum.
- **Ramming:** the forehead model supports the ramming idea, but routine head-to-head fights are rarely observed directly.
- **Lifespan:** age estimates for large whales are indirect.
- **Brain mass:** the ~9 kg value comes from a species account, not a primary measurement retrieved here.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Blue Whale (120 t; A78 D91 Ag44 St87 I69 Sp76):** the blue whale is heavier and keeps the Size ceiling, but the sperm whale is an active predator with teeth, a ramming head and far stronger biosonar. Sperm whale Attack 90.0 sits well above the blue whale's 78.0; Defense is close (90.0 vs 91.0); Agility slightly higher (47.0 vs 44.0); Stamina, Intelligence and Special are higher because of deep diving, clan culture and biosonar.
- **Versus Orca (5 t; A94 D78 Ag84 St88 I99 Sp94):** the orca stays higher in Attack because its wide toothed jaw cuts large prey and it delivers strikes far faster; the sperm whale is seven times heavier, so it takes Defense (90.0 vs 78.0), Stamina (94.0 vs 88.0) and Raw Power (95.0 vs 94.0). Intelligence 86.0 remains well below the orca's 99.0. Special is tied at 94.0 because the sperm whale's record biosonar and dive physiology match the orca's echolocation-plus-culture package; this tie was checked and kept deliberately.
- **Versus Megalodon (30 t; A100 D96 Ag58 St92 I58 Sp88):** similar mass, but Megalodon keeps the Attack ceiling with its huge serrated cutting teeth. The sperm whale is below it in Defense and Agility, above it in Stamina, Intelligence and Special.
- **Versus Colossal Squid (470 kg; A67 D43) and Giant Squid (150 kg; A58 D36):** both are documented sperm whale prey and are far lower in every physical rating, matching their reports' "very poor" matchup against sperm whales.
- **Versus Great White Shark (900 kg; A88 D68):** the sperm whale's Attack of 90.0 sits just above the shark's 88.0 because of its vastly greater mass, even though the shark has a better cutting weapon.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Speed 45.0 and Agility 47.0 avoid rewarding popular but untraceable top-speed claims. Size stays below the Blue Whale.
