# Striped Hyena (*Hyaena hyaena*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Striped hyena (also spelled striped hyaena)
- **Scientific name:** *Hyaena hyaena* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** the living striped hyena of North and East Africa, the Middle East, the Caucasus, Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent. This is a different species from the spotted hyena already on the roster (*Crocuta crocuta*) and from the brown hyena.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Status:** Living; Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male of about **38.0 kg**, head-body about **110 cm**, shoulder height about **72 cm**. This sits in the upper part of the normal male range (26–41 kg) without using the heaviest reported individuals.
- **Sex choice:** the sexes are close in size. The IUCN/SSC Hyaena Action Plan and San Diego Zoo give males 26–41 kg and females 26–34 kg, and ADW notes that males tend to be slightly heavier. A mature male is therefore the appropriate combat specimen.
- **Population variation:** body size differs across the range. The IUCN action plan records the view that the larger populations of North Africa, the Middle East, Central Asia and India kill larger prey and are more often seen in small groups, while the smaller East African and Arabian forms are more solitary and are not known to kill large herbivores. Some secondary summaries give a wider 22–55 kg adult range; the upper end is not used as the representative value.

### Bite and dentition
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No species-specific measured bite pressure was found. Comparative bite-force quotient tables list the spotted and brown hyenas but not a verified striped hyena value, and quotients cannot be turned into PSI.
- **Weapons:** a skull with a very high sagittal crest, shortened face and massive cheek teeth. The IUCN action plan notes that the cheek teeth and supporting musculature easily permit gnawing and breaking of bones and tortoise carapaces. The skull is smaller and slightly less massive than those of the brown and spotted hyenas. A dental study of hyaenids found that solitary scavengers such as the striped and brown hyenas have relatively large canines and shorter carnassials than the hunting spotted hyena.
- **Claws:** four toes per foot with short, blunt, non-retractable claws, used for digging rather than fighting.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Defense comes from a robust neck and forequarters, a coarse coat with an erectile dorsal mane (mid-back hairs up to about 20 cm in Arabian animals), mass, a pungent anal-gland secretion and a habit of feigning death when overpowered.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The striped hyena's weapon is its bite. A high sagittal crest anchors large jaw muscles, and massive premolars crack bones, carapaces and dried carcass parts that other carnivores leave behind. It is mainly a scavenger of kills made by wolves, big cats and people, plus livestock carrion, but it also kills smaller animals. Observed hunts were a simple chase and grab, and larger individuals kill prey by seizing the flank or groin. Livestock kills are mostly goats, sheep, dogs and poultry. In a Balochistan survey of 876 livestock killed by carnivores in a year, striped hyenas accounted for only 0.6%, against 66.3% for gray wolves, which illustrates how much less of an active killer it is than a wolf.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The body slopes from high shoulders to lower hindquarters. It is built for long, economical walking rather than sprinting or quick turns: foraging speed is 2–4 km/h, rising to about 8 km/h when trotting. It digs well and shelters in caves, rock fissures and burrows. Agility is modest and clearly below the spotted hyena.

### Endurance
Kruuk's Serengeti work, summarised in the IUCN action plan, found striped hyenas covering **7–27 km per night (mean 19 km)** while searching for food. Seasonal influxes that follow migrating herds in Turkmenistan suggest long foraging trips. This supports good walking endurance, not long high-speed pursuit.

### Senses
Large ears, good night vision and a keen sense of smell. It locates tortoises in their hiding places during aestivation and hibernation; one animal dug out three tortoises in two and a half hours. No quantitative sensory thresholds were found.

### Intelligence and tactics
It memorises the locations of fruiting trees, garbage dumps and other feeding sites, caches food in dense vegetation, and switches between scavenging, fruit, insects and small prey. It may shadow wolf packs, and has occasionally been recorded travelling with them. Its best-known tactic is defensive: when attacked by dogs or dug out by people it may sham death, even when repeatedly bitten.

### Sociality and hunting
Usually seen alone or in pairs, although groups of up to seven have been reported and animals may gather at a carcass. Serengeti home ranges were 44 km² for a female and 72 km² for a male. Daughters from a previous litter often help raise younger cubs, doing most maternal duties except suckling.

### Fighting and aggression
When striped hyenas fight each other they bite at the throat and legs, not the mane, which serves as a signal. The black mid-back patch and mane are raised in aggressive encounters. Against other species it is bolder than its reputation suggests: in India it usually wins one-on-one disputes over carcasses with leopards, tiger cubs and dogs, though adult tigers dominate it, and in Israel it dominates single wolves while wolf packs can displace it. In East Africa it is dominated by the spotted hyena. It can kill a dog with a single bite.

### Environmental strengths
- Open, arid and semi-arid country, rocky hills and ravines with caves for daytime cover.
- Night conditions, when it does almost all of its foraging.
- Places with carcasses, where bone-cracking jaws give it access to food others cannot use.

### Weaknesses
- Only about 38 kg; outmatched by lions, tigers, spotted hyenas and wolf packs.
- Slow, with no sprinting or fast turning ability.
- No armor; bite is the only serious weapon.
- Tends to feign death or retreat rather than press a fight it is losing.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** dogs, jackals, foxes, small predators and animals of its own mass that cannot withstand a bone-cracking bite; lone leopards contesting a carcass.
- **Mixed:** single gray wolves and similar-sized canids, where endurance and bite placement decide it.
- **Bad:** big cats, spotted hyenas, wolf packs, large herbivores and armored animals.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Bone-Splitter Jaws** — a high sagittal crest, massive premolars and strong jaw muscles let it crack bones and tortoise shells. In a fight this makes a sustained bite on a limb unusually damaging for an animal of its size.
2. **Dead-Hyena Bluff** — when overpowered by dogs or dug out by people it can sham death for long periods, even when repeatedly bitten, then recover and slip away. It can also evert its anal pouch and release a pungent secretion.

### Unique traits
1. **Mane of Warning** — a long dorsal mane and black mid-back patch are raised in aggressive encounters; ADW notes the raised mane makes it appear about 38% larger. Rivals bite the throat and legs rather than the mane.
2. **Wolf Follower** — across the Middle East and Central Asia much of its food comes from wolf kills, and on rare occasions striped hyenas have been recorded travelling with wolf packs.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Found from North and East Africa through the Middle East, Turkey and the Caucasus to Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent. It prefers open habitat, thorn scrub, semi-desert, rocky hills and ravines with caves, and avoids dense forest, true sand desert centres and high cold mountains; it has been recorded to about 3,300 m in Pakistan. It tolerates people well and has been recorded close to dense human settlements in Israel.

### Diet and ecology
Mainly a scavenger of carrion, bones and the kills of wolves, big cats and other predators. It also eats insects, fruit such as dates, melons and Balanites fruit, human refuse and small vertebrates, and in Turkmenistan it digs out tortoises. In 2024 a camera trap in Kenya photographed an adult carrying a large honeycomb, the first documented case of honey eating by a hyena. It can drink fresh, soda or salt water, or meet its water needs from melons.

### Social structure
Generally described as solitary, but with more social structure than that label suggests. Pairs and small groups occur, especially in the larger populations, and several animals may meet at a carcass. Scent marking with anal-gland paste ("hyena butter") on grass stalks, stones and tree trunks communicates presence.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding is not seasonal. Litters are usually two to four cubs, born with eyes and ears closed and without the adult mane. Cubs stay with their mother until they forage with her at about 10–12 months, and older daughters may act as helpers. Sexual maturity comes at two to three years.

### Conservation
Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List. The global population is estimated at under 10,000 mature individuals. Threats include deliberate and incidental persecution (in Arabia and North Africa it is baited, tracked and trapped as a supposed grave robber), poisoning (it readily takes strychnine bait laid for wolves or jackals, and was wiped out along Israel's Mediterranean coast by a 1918–1948 rabies poisoning campaign), a shrinking prey base, and a falling supply of carcasses as the large carnivores it scavenges from decline. Camera-trap work in southern Tanzania found it at low density (about 1.4 per 100 km²).

### Special features
- Bone-cracking cheek teeth and a high sagittal crest.
- Erectile dorsal mane and black mid-back patch.
- Death-feigning when overpowered.
- Eversible anal pouch with a pungent secretion.
- Blunt, non-retractable digging claws.

### Human interaction
Usually timid around people. It has long been persecuted as a grave robber and livestock killer, and it carries a heavy load of folklore across its range. Rare attacks on people, mostly on sleeping children, have been reported historically in the Caucasus, Central Asia and India, and a 2024 case report described facial injuries from a striped hyena bite in northern India. Its actual share of livestock losses is small in most surveys.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its raised mane can make it look about 38% bigger.
- When cornered by dogs it may play dead, even while being bitten.
- One striped hyena was seen digging out three hibernating tortoises in two and a half hours.
- A Kenyan camera trap caught one carrying a honeycomb, the first record of a hyena eating honey.
- Serengeti animals walked 7–27 km a night looking for food.
- In India it usually beats a lone leopard in a dispute over a carcass.
- Older daughters help their mothers raise the next litter.

### Concise site-ready summary
The striped hyena is a shaggy, 38 kg night scavenger of Africa and Asia whose bone-cracking jaws let it feed where others cannot. It walks long distances, bluffs with a raised mane and can play dead when overpowered. It will face down a lone leopard over a carcass, but it is slow and lacks the power of big cats or spotted hyenas.

### Rich narrative profile
The striped hyena is the most widespread and least understood of the bone-cracking hyenas. A mature male weighs around 38 kg, stands about 72 cm at the shoulder and has the family's sloping back, big pointed ears and a coat of grey or beige barred with black stripes. Along its spine runs a long mane that it raises when threatened, suddenly looking far larger than it is.

It spends the night walking. Serengeti animals covered up to 27 km in a night at a steady 2–4 km/h, checking bushes, holes and tree bases, remembering where fruit trees and rubbish dumps are and caching what they cannot eat. It follows wolves and big cats to their kills, and its massive premolars and powerful jaw muscles let it finish bones, hooves and tortoise shells that the killers leave behind.

In a fight it relies on that bite and on its nerve. It can kill a dog with a single bite and usually wins a one-on-one dispute over a carcass with a leopard, yet it is slow, lacks claws built for fighting, and will often choose to sham death rather than keep fighting a losing battle. On the absolute roster scale it sits below the heavier spotted hyena and the gray wolf in Attack, but its toughness, endurance and odd defensive tricks make it a stubborn opponent for medium-sized animals.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** authoritative sources give 26–41 kg for males; some summaries give 22–55 kg. The canonical 38 kg stays inside the authoritative male range.
- **Lifespan:** ADW's 24 years reflects captivity. The 11-year canonical value follows the San Diego Zoo wild estimate and is low confidence.
- **Predation on large animals:** reports of kills of horses and donkeys may include misidentified scavenging, as the IUCN action plan warns.
- **Bite:** no striped hyena bite-force value could be verified; the field stays at 0.0.
- **Speed:** only foraging and trotting speeds are published; no sprint value is adopted.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hyena (spotted, 75 kg female; A61 D53 Ag61 St83 I78 Sp72):** the striped hyena is about half the mass, has a smaller, less massive skull and lacks clan cooperation, so it sits below the spotted hyena in every headline rating.
- **Versus Gray Wolf (50 kg; A55 D41 Ag66 St89 I78):** lighter and a much less active killer, so Attack is 5 points lower. Defense is set one point higher because of its robust neck and forequarters and proven tolerance of heavy biting; Israeli observations of striped hyenas dominating single wolves at carcasses support keeping the two close. The wolf keeps far higher Agility, Stamina and Intelligence.
- **Versus Jackal (8.1 kg; A34 D27 Ag74):** the striped hyena is several times heavier with a bone-cracking bite, so it is well above the jackal in Attack and Defense and well below it in Agility.
- **Versus Brown Hyena (43 kg; A53 D45):** the brown hyena is heavier, with a larger skull and more robust teeth, so it sits a few points above the striped hyena in Attack and Defense.
- **Versus Aardvark (65 kg; A41 D40):** despite being lighter, the striped hyena is a carnivore with a damaging bite, so its Attack is higher; Defense is similar.
- **Anti-inflation check:** a reputation for grave robbing and man-eating folklore does not raise its ratings; it stays in the moderate band well below big cats.
