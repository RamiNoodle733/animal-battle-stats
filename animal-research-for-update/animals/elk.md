# Elk

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Common name:** Elk / wapiti
- **Scientific name:** *Cervus canadensis*
- **Living/extinct:** Living
- **Canonical specimen:** healthy mature Rocky Mountain-type bull, about 5+ years old, in hard-antler breeding condition.
- **Sex/population context:** Bulls are substantially larger and are the primary fighting sex. Yellowstone reports bulls around 700 lb (318 kg) and ~5 ft (152 cm) at the shoulder versus cows around 500 lb. Roosevelt elk can be substantially heavier, while tule elk are smaller. The canonical animal therefore avoids both small and giant subspecies extremes.

## 2. Physical measurements
### Mass, height and length
Yellowstone NPS reports bulls around 700 lb and 5 ft at the shoulder. Bryce Canyon gives Rocky Mountain bulls about 700 lb, 8 ft nose-to-tail and 5 ft at the shoulder. Great Smoky Mountains reports adults 7-10 ft long and bulls up to 1,000 lb, illustrating geographic/population variation rather than a representative universal bull.

Recommended representative bull: **320.0 kg**, **152.0 cm shoulder height**, **240.0 cm nose-to-tail length**.

### Antlers and weapons
Yellowstone reports a typical healthy mature bull's antlers at **55-60 in (140-152 cm) long**, just under **6 ft (~183 cm) wide**, about **30 lb (13.6 kg) per pair**, commonly six tines per side. Grand Canyon reports antlers up to ~18 kg. Bulls wrestle with antlers during the rut; when antlerless they can use the front hooves, which Yellowstone notes is more injury-prone.

### Speed
A defensible instrumented species-specific maximum was not located in this run. The live site's 65 km/h is therefore not retained as established fact. **`speed_mps: 0.0`** means unresolved reliable maximum, not immobility.

### Lifespan
Great Smoky Mountains NPS reports an average lifespan around **15 years**. Older sources sometimes cite 20+ year maxima. Canonical **15.0 years** is used as representative rather than maximum longevity.

### Bite force
Elk combat is not bite-centered and no defensible standardized species-specific PSI measurement was found. **`bite_force_psi: 0.0`**.

### Defenses
No armor. Defense comes from ~320 kg mass, robust cervid skeleton and musculature, dense seasonal coat, mobility, vigilance, herd behavior, antlers when present, and dangerous hooves. Limbs, abdomen and other soft tissues remain vulnerable.

## 4. Combat biology
**Primary weapons:** hardened branching antlers in season, backed by neck/shoulder musculature and body mass. **Secondary weapons:** front-hoof strikes and trampling/kicking. Rutting bulls assess, parallel-walk, threaten and then lock antlers, pushing and twisting for dominance. The antler rack gives exceptional reach and multiple contact points but can entangle and is seasonal.

**Locomotion:** elk are mobile large ungulates capable of seasonal elevational migration and rapid terrestrial escape. Their long limbs provide useful acceleration and obstacle negotiation, but top speed is not used as a proxy for agility.

**Endurance:** seasonal migrations, extensive foraging and movement across mountain landscapes support strong sustained capacity. This does not justify the legacy 94.7 Stamina, which lacked direct physiological evidence for near-ceiling performance.

**Senses:** Great Smoky Mountains NPS describes acute smell and exceptional eyesight. Hearing and vigilance are also important for predator detection and rut communication.

**Tactics/intelligence:** bulls use assessment, display, bugling, rival tracking and escalation before dangerous physical contests. This is meaningful combat decision-making but not evidence for primate/corvid-level flexible cognition.

**Predator defense:** adults face wolves, cougars and bears in parts of the range. A healthy bull can deter or injure predators with antlers/hooves and size, but large carnivores can exploit flanks, hindquarters, exhaustion, snow and group attacks.

**Environment:** open meadows favor awareness and antler maneuvering; forests offer cover but can constrain the rack. Deep snow raises movement cost. Steep terrain is usable but elk are not technical cliff specialists.

**Weaknesses:** no armor, vulnerable legs and abdomen, seasonal loss of antlers, direct linear fighting style, and high energetic costs during rut. Large racks can be awkward in dense cover.

**Matchups:** strong against smaller terrestrial opponents forced into frontal range; mixed against similarly sized horned ungulates; poor against much heavier megafauna, armored opponents, or large predators/specialists able to avoid the frontal rack and repeatedly attack vulnerable zones.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits
### Special abilities
1. **Rutting Rack** - A mature bull's ~55-60 inch multi-tined antlers create long frontal reach for wrestling, hooking, shoving and threat display.
2. **Hoof Counterstrike** - When close contact bypasses the rack, powerful forelegs can strike and stamp; Yellowstone notes antlerless bulls use front hooves in disputes and can cause injury.

### Unique traits
1. **Annual Arsenal** - Bulls grow, harden, wield and then shed a massive bony weapon every year; mature antlers can grow roughly two-thirds of an inch per day late in growth.
2. **Bugle-and-Assess Rivalry** - Bulls use bugling and displays to locate and assess rivals before escalating to direct antler wrestling, reducing unnecessary contact while coordinating rut competition.

## 8. Expanded profile
**Habitat/range:** forests, mountain valleys, shrublands and open meadows across western and locally restored portions of North America, with seasonal elevational movements in many populations. **Diet:** grasses, sedges, forbs, shrubs, bark, conifer needles and other browse. **Ecology:** major grazer/browser and prey base for large carnivores; Yellowstone notes elk dominate winter wolf kills and materially affect vegetation and nutrient dynamics. **Social:** gregarious; cows and young form herds, bulls often segregate outside the rut, and dominant bulls gather harems during breeding season. **Life history:** rut mainly September-October in Yellowstone; usually one calf in late spring/early summer. **Conservation:** globally abundant relative to many large mammals, but local management, hunting, predator restoration, disease and habitat connectivity strongly shape populations. **Human interaction:** important game and cultural species; rutting bulls and protective cows are dangerous at close range.

### Fun facts
- Yellowstone reports a typical healthy mature bull's antlers just under six feet wide.
- A mature rack can weigh about 30 lb per pair and is regrown annually.
- Roughly 70% of mature-bull antler growth occurs in the latter half of the growth period.
- Bulls commonly retain antlers through winter, and NPS notes this can reduce injuries because antlerless hoof fighting is more dangerous.
- Yellowstone elk are a major food source for wolves, bears, cougars and scavengers.

### Concise site summary
The elk is a 300-plus-kilogram North American cervid whose mature bulls carry one of the most imposing seasonal weapons among living deer. Its battle profile centers on long multi-tined antlers, strong neck and shoulder musculature, dangerous hooves, good large-ungulate mobility and strong migratory endurance. It is formidable head-on during the rut but lacks armor and remains vulnerable to larger megafauna and predators that can bypass the rack.

### Rich narrative profile
A mature bull elk is built for ritualized but genuinely dangerous frontal competition. The rack changes the geometry of a fight before bodies touch: long beams and multiple tines create reach, catch opposing antlers and transmit force into a heavily muscled neck. Bulls advertise and assess through bugling and displays, then escalate to wrestling when neither yields. That makes the elk more tactically structured than a simple charging herbivore.

Absolute scaling matters. At roughly 320 kg the bull is powerful, but it is not a bison, rhinoceros or elephant. Its hide is not armor and its long legs remain vulnerable. Against a smaller terrestrial opponent the rack can dominate access to the head and chest; against a larger or more durable opponent, the elk's lack of mass and protection becomes decisive. Seasonal antler shedding also means the canonical hard-antler rut bull represents the species' strongest normal fighting state rather than a year-round weapon configuration.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
Older taxonomy sometimes places North American elk under *Cervus elaphus*; this report follows current North American usage *Cervus canadensis*. Body size differs strongly among subspecies and populations. Maximum speed remains unresolved rather than copying common 40-45 mph claims. Antler measurements describe representative mature bulls and should not be treated as universal or permanent year-round dimensions.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization
- **Bison:** Elk is roughly one-third the mass of the 900 kg canonical bison, so Raw Power, Attack and Defense remain clearly lower despite longer antler reach.
- **Donkey:** Elk is over twice the canonical donkey's mass and has a large purpose-built rut weapon, justifying higher Attack, but both remain unarmored ungulates.
- **Cougar:** Elk has far greater mass and frontal reach, while cougar remains much more agile and has a dedicated predatory grapple/bite system.
- **Bighorn Sheep:** Elk is much larger and has broader weapon reach, but bighorn's cranial collision specialization should not be erased by mass alone.
- **Inflation check:** no elk headline score exceeds the elite 85+ band. The legacy 94.7 Stamina is specifically rejected as unsupported near-ceiling placement.
