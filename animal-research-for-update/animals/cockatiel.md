# Cockatiel (*Nymphicus hollandicus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Cockatiel (quarrion, weero)
- **Scientific name:** *Nymphicus hollandicus* (Kerr, 1792)
- **Exact animal represented:** the cockatiel as a species: the grey, crested, nomadic cockatoo of inland Australia, which is also the wild stock that one of the world's most common companion parrots was bred from. Colour mutations (lutino, pied, pearl and others) are pet varieties of the same species and are not treated separately.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult wild-type male, **90 g (0.09 kg)** and **32 cm** long including the tail, matching ADW's 80–90 g average and AnAge's 90 g, and inside Wikipedia's 30–33 cm length.
- **Sex choice:** males and females are about the same size. Adult males have a bright yellow face and crest and a vivid orange cheek patch; females and juveniles are greyer with barred undertails (Wikipedia; ADW). A male is used for roster consistency, not because it is a stronger fighter.
- **Population notes:** the smallest member of the cockatoo family (Cacatuidae), sharing the family's erectile crest, powder down and facial feathering (Wikipedia). Northern birds are more nomadic; southern birds make more predictable seasonal movements (ADW).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
A small hooked cockatoo bill, used to dehusk *Acacia* and grass seeds and to bite in defense, is the cockatiel's only weapon. It has no measured bite force and no talons. Against anything larger than itself it can deliver only nips; its whole response to danger is to fly.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Cockatiels are fast, direct fliers with long pointed wings. The long tail fans out in flight and its angle is adjusted to control altitude and stability; the crest is laid flat during flight to cut drag (ADW). They have been studied across a wide range of wind-tunnel speeds, making them a model species for bird flight mechanics (Hedrick et al. 2004; Morris and Askew 2010). Small flocks fly in tight, well-coordinated formations (BirdLife Australia). They also climb with zygodactyl feet and walk well on the ground.

### Endurance
Cockatiels are nomadic and follow food and water; flocks can travel great distances from nest sites while foraging, "achieved relatively quickly, thanks to their high flight speeds" (ADW). Their habitat swings from about 4.5 °C on winter nights to over 43 °C in summer (ADW). Wind-tunnel respirometry shows a U-shaped cost-of-flight curve, with an energy-efficient cruising range in the middle speeds (Bundle et al. 2007; Morris, Nelson and Askew 2010). No telemetry of daily distances was found.

### Senses
Good vision and loud, far-carrying calls; the pitch and duration of alarm calls signal how serious a threat is (ADW). Cheek feathers are shaped to protect the ear during flight. No unusual sensory system is documented.

### Intelligence and tactics
Cockatiels learn whistled tunes and some words; ADW notes they mimic melodies far better than speech. A cockatiel study found birds spontaneously singing in synchrony with a melody of human music (Seki 2023). In a comparison of nine parrot species imitating R2-D2 sounds, budgerigars and cockatiels were more accurate at monophonic sounds than larger-brained grey and amazon parrots (Dam et al. 2025). Crest position communicates alarm, calm and aggression (Wikipedia; ADW). Problem-solving research on this species is thin.

### Sociality and hunting
Seed-eaters that never hunt. Gregarious all year, even when breeding; flocks usually hold up to 30 birds but can reach hundreds or thousands (BirdLife Australia; ADW). Pairs stay together year-round.

### Fighting and aggression
ADW describes cockatiels as timid and without natural defense mechanisms, always choosing flight. Males are more vocally assertive in courtship, and crests flatten when a bird is angry or defensive, but damaging fights are not described.

### Environmental strengths
- Open arid and semi-arid country near water, where fast direct flight and flock vigilance work best.
- Wide temperature tolerance, from near-freezing nights to over 43 °C days.
- Ground-feeding camouflage in dappled shade.

### Weaknesses
- 90 g body with a weak bill.
- No protection; timid temperament.
- Depends on fresh water.
- Flock protection disappears in a one-on-one matchup.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** insects and small animals that cannot follow it into the air.
- **Mixed:** other small birds, where speed and vigilance decide who escapes.
- **Bad:** raptors, snakes, cats and any medium predator that can seize it.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Fan-Tail Sprint** — a long tail that fans out to control altitude and stability, a crest folded flat to cut drag, and wind-tunnel flight up to 16 m/s make the cockatiel's first and only move a fast, controlled escape.
2. **Melody Sync** — a vocal learner that copies whistled tunes and has been recorded singing in time with human music; in a matchup this helps flock contact and alarm signalling, not damage.

### Unique traits
1. **Mood Crest** — the erectile crest stands vertical when startled, sits at an angle when relaxed and lies flat when angry or defensive, a readable signal shared with the larger cockatoos.
2. **Smallest Cockatoo** — at about 30–33 cm and 90 g it is the smallest member of the cockatoo family, with the family's powder down and crest on a slim, long-tailed frame.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Cockatiels live across mainland Australia, mostly in arid and semi-arid open country but always near water: open woodland, savanna around waterholes, shrubland and farmland (Wikipedia; ADW). They are absent from the fertile south-west and south-east corners, the deepest Western Australian deserts and Cape York (Wikipedia). BirdLife Australia lists woodland, desert and grassland habitats.

### Diet and ecology
Wild cockatiels eat mainly seeds, especially *Acacia*, as well as wheat, sunflower and sorghum, and often feed on crops, to the dismay of farmers (Wikipedia; ADW). They forage on the ground, where their grey plumage makes them hard for aerial predators to spot.

### Social structure
Gregarious throughout the year. Flocks usually hold up to 30 birds but may reach hundreds or thousands; small flocks fly in tight formation and flocks roost communally at night (BirdLife Australia). Pair bonds form early and persist all year (ADW).

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding is triggered mainly by rainfall. Pairs nest in tree hollows, usually in dead eucalypts near water, about 1–2 m above the ground. Males sing, strut and inspect the hollow before the female enters; both parents care for the young (ADW).

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (BirdLife International assessment, as reported by Wikipedia and BirdLife Australia). The species is widespread and common.

### Special features
- Erectile crest that signals mood and folds flat in flight.
- Long, pointed tail that fans out to control altitude and stability.
- Powder down that waterproofs the plumage and rises as dust when the bird shakes.
- Fast, direct flight studied across 0–16 m/s in wind tunnels.
- Loud, graded alarm calls and learned whistled songs.

### Human interaction
One of the five most popular pet bird species; an estimated 4.8 million US households keep cockatiels (Wikipedia). Pet birds are valued for their crests, whistling and tameness. Wild flocks raid cereal and sunflower crops. Cockatiels are also a standard laboratory species for flight mechanics.

### Genuine fun facts
- The cockatiel is the smallest cockatoo in the world.
- Its crest is a mood meter: upright when startled, angled when relaxed, flat when angry.
- Cockatiels have been flown in wind tunnels at up to 16 m/s so scientists could measure how their flight muscle works.
- A cockatiel study found birds spontaneously singing along in time with a melody of human music.
- In a citizen-science test, budgerigars and cockatiels imitated simple R2-D2 beeps more accurately than grey parrots.
- Cockatiels shed so much powder down that a cloud of dust rises when they shake after preening.
- Their tail makes up about half of their total length.

### Concise site-ready summary
The cockatiel is the smallest cockatoo, a 90-gram grey nomad of inland Australia that answers every threat by flying. Fast, direct flight and tight flocks keep it safe in open country, but its small bill and timid nature make it harmless in a real fight.

### Rich narrative profile
The cockatiel is a cockatoo in miniature: the same erectile crest, powder down and loud voice as the larger cockatoos, packed into a slim 90-gram body with a tail as long as the rest of the bird. Wild flocks roam the dry interior of Australia in search of seeding *Acacia* and grasses, never straying far from water, and pairs breed in eucalypt hollows whenever rain brings food.

Everything about the cockatiel points toward escape. Animal Diversity Web describes it as timid, with no natural defense beyond flight, and its long pointed wings and fanned tail make it one of the faster small parrots. Researchers use it as a model for bird flight, flying cockatiels in wind tunnels at up to 16 metres per second. Its crest works as a signal to flockmates, and its alarm calls change in pitch with the size of the threat.

As a pet it is known for whistled tunes rather than speech, and it has been recorded singing in time with human music. None of that helps in a fight. A 90-gram bird with a small bill and no protection cannot hurt a vertebrate predator; its best outcome in any matchup is a fast, clean getaway.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Speed:** the 16 m/s value is the top of an experimental wind-tunnel range; ADW's "over 40 mph" is plausible but unsourced. Neither is a verified field maximum.
- **Lifespan:** wild 10–14 years (ADW) versus Wikipedia's "average 12–15 years"; captive records of 35–36 years are exceptional.
- **Bite:** no cockatiel measurement exists; no PSI or newton value is assigned.
- **Mimicry:** ADW says cockatiels cannot accurately imitate human speech, while Wikipedia says they learn many words; both agree whistled melodies are their strength.
