# Bonobo (*Pan paniscus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Bonobo (formerly "pygmy chimpanzee")
- **Scientific name:** *Pan paniscus* Schwarz, 1929
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild bonobo of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the second species in the genus *Pan* alongside the chimpanzee. Captive language-trained individuals are used only as evidence of cognitive capacity, not as the representative specimen.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Status:** Living; found only south of the Congo River in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature wild adult male of about **42.0 kg**, head-body about **80 cm**, able to stand about **115 cm** tall.
- **Sex choice:** males are about 30% heavier than females and have somewhat larger canines, although bonobos show the smallest canine size difference between the sexes of any living ape. Females are socially dominant through coalitions, but the heavier male is used for the physical combat baseline.
- **Population variation:** reported male masses range widely (about 34–60 kg), with typical wild adult males in the high 30s to mid 40s. Heavy captive animals are not used as the baseline.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
A bonobo fights with its hands, arms and teeth: it grabs, pulls, hits and bites. Its canines are smaller than a chimpanzee's and it is more lightly built, so its damage output is lower. Male bonobos are nonetheless physically aggressive: a 2024 study found male bonobos engaged in about 2.8 times as many aggressive encounters and about three times as many physical attacks as male chimpanzees, although these fights were rarely severe. Bonobos also hunt: at LuiKotale and other sites they catch forest antelope (duikers) and occasionally monkeys.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Bonobos are strong climbers and move easily between ground and trees. Their long legs make them more comfortable walking upright than chimpanzees, especially when carrying food. Grasping hands and feet give excellent control in three dimensions.

### Endurance
Daily travel is on foot and through trees while foraging for fruit; bonobos are not built for long runs. Endurance is moderate, similar to the chimpanzee.

### Senses
Good colour vision and depth perception, keen hearing and a rich vocal and gestural communication system. No specialized sense.

### Intelligence and tactics
Bonobos are among the most cognitively capable animals. The captive bonobo Kanzi learned hundreds of lexigram symbols and, with his sister Panbanisha, made stone flakes by direct percussion and used them for specific tasks. In the wild, the key tactical skill is social: females form coalitions, target males in most of them, and in some communities outrank most males.

### Sociality and hunting
Bonobos live in fission-fusion communities of several dozen animals. Meat eating is opportunistic and usually involves duikers, with food sharing common and occasionally even shared across community boundaries.

### Fighting and aggression
Across four studied bonobo communities, a 2014 review of five decades of data found only one suspected killing, against 152 killings by chimpanzees in 15 communities. Bonobos are not harmless, though: in 2026 researchers reported the first documented case of a bonobo infant dying after an attack by a neighbouring group at Kokolopori. Conflict is frequent but usually low in severity, and tension is often settled through social and sexual contact rather than injury.

### Environmental strengths
- Rainforest with dense canopy and many climbing routes.
- Group settings where coalition support can change the outcome.
- Close-range scrambles where grasping hands help.

### Weaknesses
- Light build and small canines for an ape.
- No armor and low body fat.
- Tends to de-escalate rather than press a lethal fight.
- Much less powerful than gorillas or large predators.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small mammals and primates, duiker-sized prey, lightly armed animals that cannot follow it into trees.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized primates such as baboons and mandrills, where canines and aggression matter.
- **Bad:** big cats, bears, large herbivores and venomous snakes.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Sisterhood Coalition** — females team up against males in the large majority of their coalitions, allowing them to outrank most males in some communities; in a fight this means an attacker can face several allies at once.
2. **Stone-Flake Craft** — captive bonobos Kanzi and Panbanisha struck flint cores to make sharp flakes and used them for specific tasks such as cutting and digging, showing a capacity for making and using tools.

### Unique traits
1. **Peacemaking Ape** — conflict is common but usually low in severity, and tension is often defused through social and sexual contact; across decades of study only a single suspected killing was recorded.
2. **Upright Long-Legged Build** — a slim upper body, narrow shoulders and long legs make the bonobo more at ease walking upright than the chimpanzee.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Lowland rainforest and swamp forest south of the Congo River in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, with some forest-savanna mosaic at the range edge. The river separates bonobos from chimpanzees.

### Diet and ecology
Mainly ripe fruit, plus leaves, stems, seeds, flowers, mushrooms, invertebrates and occasional meat. Hunting of duikers and small primates is more frequent at some newer study sites than once believed, and meat is often shared.

### Social structure
Fission-fusion communities of several dozen animals that split into smaller parties during the day. Females move to a new community at maturity and build alliances there. Female coalitions give females high social rank relative to males.

### Reproduction and life history
Females usually give birth to a single infant, and infants depend on their mothers for years. Long intervals between births mean populations recover slowly from losses.

### Conservation
Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Population estimates range roughly from 5,000 to 17,000 animals but are very uncertain because conflict has limited survey work. Main threats are hunting for bushmeat, habitat loss and civil unrest.

### Special features
- Slim build with long legs suited to upright walking.
- Grasping hands and feet.
- Small canines and wide face compared with the chimpanzee.
- Rich vocal and gestural communication.
- Female-dominated coalition social system.

### Human interaction
Bonobos are threatened by hunting and habitat loss in the Congo Basin. Sanctuaries such as Lola ya Bonobo care for orphans from the bushmeat trade. Research with captive bonobos, including Kanzi, has been central to studies of ape language and tool making.

### Genuine fun facts
- Bonobos live only south of the Congo River; the river keeps them apart from chimpanzees.
- In a comparison of killings across the genus *Pan*, chimpanzees accounted for 152 while bonobos had just one suspected case.
- Male bonobos were found to be about three times more physically aggressive than male chimpanzees, though fights were rarely serious.
- Female bonobos outranked about 70% of males in their communities in one study.
- Kanzi the bonobo learned hundreds of lexigram symbols and made stone tools.
- Bonobos have been seen sharing meat with members of a different community.

### Concise site-ready summary
The bonobo is a slim, long-legged great ape of the Congo rainforest, nearly as clever as the chimpanzee but built lighter and armed with smaller canines. It relies on climbing skill, grasping hands and powerful female coalitions rather than brute force, and its fights are frequent but seldom deadly.

### Rich narrative profile
Bonobos look like slender chimpanzees with long legs, a small head and a dark face framed by hair parted down the middle. They live only south of the Congo River, in swampy and lowland rainforest, and spend their days feeding on fruit in trees and moving between patches on the ground, often walking upright to carry food.

Their social life is what makes them famous. Females band together and use coalitions to keep males in check, so in many communities females hold the top ranks. Tension is often defused with social and sexual contact, and across decades of study only one bonobo killing was even suspected. Recent work has added nuance: male bonobos squabble more often than male chimpanzees, and in 2026 researchers documented an infant dying after an intergroup attack. The bonobo is peaceful by chimpanzee standards, not harmless.

In ABS terms the bonobo sits just below the chimpanzee on physical ratings, because it is lighter, more gracile and has smaller canines, and just below it on Intelligence and Special because wild bonobos use fewer tools. Its cognitive capacity is still elite: Kanzi's symbol use and stone-flake making show what a bonobo mind can do. In a matchup, its best assets are climbing, dexterity and allies, not raw damage.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** reported male ranges run from 34 to 60 kg; 42 kg is a representative wild male, not a heavy captive animal.
- **Aggression:** bonobos are both more frequently aggressive (males) and far less lethal than chimpanzees; both findings are kept rather than one erasing the other.
- **Lifespan:** only longevity cases were found; 40 years is low confidence.
- **Cognition:** captive language and tool results show capacity, not typical wild behavior.
