# Koala research report

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Common name:** Koala
- **Scientific name:** *Phascolarctos cinereus*
- **Taxon represented:** the living koala species across eastern and southern mainland Australia. Regional body size varies strongly and older proposed north/south subspecies boundaries should not be treated as clean combat taxa here.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy fully mature **southern adult male**, represented at **12.0 kg** and **78.0 cm head-body length**. This is deliberately a large-population representative adult, not a record animal. Adult males are the larger sex and the primary intrasexual competitors.
- **Dimorphism/population variation:** Australian Museum reports Victorian males averaging 12.0 kg versus Victorian females 8.5 kg, while Queensland males average about 6.5 kg and females about 5.1 kg. ADW gives southern male mean head-body length about 78 cm versus 72 cm in females. The geographic gradient is real and large enough that a northern male should not be silently substituted for the canonical southern fighter.

## 2. Physical measurements
### Mass
- Australian Government DCCEEW: species range broadly **4-15 kg**, with Victorian males averaging **12 kg** and Queensland males **6.5 kg**.
- Australian Museum fact sheet: Victorian males average **12.0 kg**, females **8.5 kg**; Queensland males **6.5 kg**, females **5.1 kg**.
- ADW: southern males average **11.8 kg**, northern males **6.5 kg**.
- **Canonical:** 12.0 kg adult southern male. **Confidence: high.**

### Length and height
- Australian Museum gives species size range **68-82 cm**.
- ADW reports southern males averaging **78 cm head-body length** and females 72 cm. Koalas have only a vestigial tail, so head-body length is the biologically useful length.
- Historical anatomical descriptions place all-fours height around 10-11 in (25-28 cm), but this is not strong enough or standardized enough for a modern canonical site height.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 78.0.**
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0**, pending a defensible standardized adult-male standing/shoulder-height dataset.

### Speed and locomotion
- Queensland Government states that disturbed koalas can break into a bounding gallop at **up to 30 km/h**, equivalent to **8.3 m/s**. The same source stresses that they are poorly adapted to terrestrial walking.
- They are powerful climbers. Strong arms, powerful legs, sharp claws, two opposed foredigits and rough gripping pads support vertical climbing and branch control.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 8.3**, as an official-government reported terrestrial maximum, not as a measure of agility or sustainable pace. **Confidence: moderate.**

### Lifespan
- ADW demographic summaries include wild values around **13-17 years** and captive values up to 20 years.
- Billabong Zoo gives wild averages of **15-18 years for females and 10-15 years for males**.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 15.0**, a reasonable upper-normal mature male figure rather than a captive maximum. **Confidence: moderate.**

### Bite force and dentition
- No defensible species-specific bite pressure in PSI was found. Comparative finite-element work confirms meaningful herbivorous cranial biomechanics but does not justify converting modelled bite performance into PSI.
- Koalas possess forward lower incisors, upper canines and specialized selenodont cheek teeth for cutting and grinding foliage.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0** because reliable PSI is unavailable, not because the animal cannot bite.

### Natural weapons and defensive structures
- **Fore/hind claws:** Queensland Government describes sharp claws, strong arms and powerful legs; San Diego Zoo describes sharp bark-gripping claws and strong arm/shoulder musculature. Exact claw length was not located in a sufficiently authoritative morphometric source, so no dimension is invented.
- **Teeth/jaws:** capable of defensive biting, but the skull and dentition are primarily adapted to browsing rather than predation.
- **Body/fur:** stocky body and dense woolly fur offer modest superficial protection but no armor.
- **Grip:** two opposed digits on each forepaw plus rough pads make the forelimbs effective grappling/clinging structures in trees.

## 4. Combat biology
### Primary and secondary weapons
The koala's most practical weapons are its **sharp claws and powerful gripping forelimbs**, followed by a defensive bite. A mature male is substantially heavier than northern or female animals and can use that mass in close grappling. The claws are optimized for bark penetration and grip rather than slicing prey, but they can scratch an attacker severely. The bite is real and relevant at contact distance, although no credible PSI value exists.

### Offensive mechanics
Combat is close-range. A koala can cling, pull, scratch and bite while maintaining a strong hold. Arboreal structure makes this more effective on trunks and branches where the animal can anchor itself. It lacks long reach, horns, venom, crushing jaws or a dedicated killing weapon, so Attack must remain low on the full-roster scale.

### Intraspecific fighting and aggression
Koalas are generally solitary, but breeding males compete spatially. A playback experiment on free-ranging animals found adult males approaching male bellows, especially calls from smaller males, supporting active intra-male competition. Male bellows advertise size and space occupancy. Wild koalas can bite and scratch when threatened, and mating interactions can be physically aggressive.

### Defense and toughness
Dense fur, a compact build and strong gripping ability provide limited protection, but there is no armor, thick hide or major anti-predator weapon. Ground travel exposes koalas to dogs and vehicles, both recognized conservation threats. Their best defense is positional: remain high in a tree, cling securely and climb away.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
In trees, koalas are capable, deliberate climbers with opposed digits, rough pads and strong shoulders. They can rapidly ascend trunks and negotiate branches. On the ground, their normal gait is slow and awkward, though a disturbed animal can bound at up to 30 km/h. Thus their maneuverability is strongly environment-dependent and should not be equated with the sprint figure.

### Endurance and recovery
Koalas are **low-throughput energy specialists**. Eucalypt foliage is fibrous, low in usable energy and chemically defended. Government and zoo sources report roughly 18-20 hours per day resting/sleeping. This is excellent energy conservation, not high combat endurance. Sustained intense fighting or pursuit should fatigue a koala comparatively quickly.

### Senses and communication
Male bellows are exceptional acoustic signals. Research found inhaled bellow fundamental frequency averaging **27.1 Hz**, extraordinarily low for an approximately 8 kg animal, produced using a specialized vocal structure. Chemical scent marking and vocal communication organize breeding-space interactions. These systems aid social assessment but are not direct damage mechanisms.

### Intelligence and tactics
Koalas navigate arboreal home ranges, select food trees, discriminate among chemically variable leaves and respond strategically to conspecific calls. This supports functional spatial and dietary decision-making, but there is no basis for primate-level problem solving or coordinated pack tactics. Their combat repertoire is comparatively simple.

### Predation / predator defense
Koalas are folivores and do not hunt. Defense is avoidance, climbing, clinging, scratching and biting. Their terrestrial phase is especially vulnerable; current Australian conservation guidance identifies dog attacks and vehicle collisions as major mortality sources.

### Environmental advantages
- **Trees/woodland:** major advantage. Grip, claws and climbing competence allow vertical escape and stable fighting positions.
- **Cool southern habitat:** dense fur and larger body size are advantageous.
- **Eucalypt habitat:** nutritional specialization gives access to a food niche many mammals cannot exploit.

### Environmental disadvantages
- **Open ground:** poor normal terrestrial locomotion and limited escape options.
- **Prolonged heat/drought/fire:** major physiological/ecological pressure, reflected in national threat assessments.
- **Long pursuit or repeated high-output combat:** low-energy diet and long daily rest requirement are disadvantages.

### Major weaknesses
1. No dedicated high-lethality weapon.
2. Low sustained energy budget.
3. Ground vulnerability.
4. No armor and modest body mass on the roster-wide scale.
5. Highly specialized diet and habitat dependence.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Best against:** similarly small, poorly armed opponents when the koala can grapple from a trunk or branch; opponents unable to climb effectively.
- **Worst against:** medium/large terrestrial carnivores, long-reach or armored opponents, and anything that can force prolonged exertion or remove the arboreal escape advantage.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings
- **Attack: 28.0** - a 12 kg male can claw, grapple and bite, but lacks a dedicated lethal weapon and remains far below serious medium/large predators in absolute damage potential.
- **Defense: 23.0** - compact build, fur, grip and arboreal escape help, but there is no armor and ground vulnerability is pronounced.
- **Agility: 54.0** - skilled arboreal positioning and rapid climbing raise it well above its slow stereotype, while poor ground maneuverability prevents a high roster score.
- **Stamina: 32.0** - energy conservation is excellent, sustained exertional output is not. Long resting time and a low-energy diet are decisive constraints.
- **Intelligence: 49.0** - competent spatial, dietary and social assessment, but not evidence for advanced tactical problem-solving comparable with corvids, apes or dolphins.
- **Special: 62.0** - unusual Eucalyptus detoxification physiology/microbiome and extremely low-frequency bellow apparatus are distinctive, though neither is a direct high-damage combat system.

## 7. Abilities and traits
### Special abilities, exactly two
1. **Eucalypt Detox Engine** - specialized gastrointestinal physiology and microbiome permit exploitation of chemically defended, fibrous Eucalyptus foliage. This is primarily a survival specialization rather than an offensive buff, so its value is concentrated in Special rather than Attack or Stamina.
2. **Subsonic-Scale Bellow** - adult males use a specialized vocal structure to produce exceptionally low-pitched bellows, with published inhaled fundamental frequencies averaging 27.1 Hz. It supports size advertisement and spacing among rivals rather than physically stunning opponents.

### Unique traits, exactly two
1. **Double-Thumb Tree Grip** - two opposed foredigits, rough pads and sharp claws create an unusually secure arboreal grip and improve trunk/branch control.
2. **Southern Giant Gradient** - body size changes dramatically across the range; Victorian males average about 12 kg versus about 6.5 kg in Queensland, making locality unusually important to combat-scale representation.

## 8. Expanded profile content
### Habitat and range
Koalas occupy eucalypt-dominated forests and woodlands across eastern and southern mainland Australia, from Queensland through NSW/ACT and Victoria into South Australia. Distribution is patchy because suitable food trees and connected habitat are not continuous.

### Diet and ecology
Koalas are specialist folivores feeding overwhelmingly on selected eucalypt foliage, with some flowers and stems. More than 400 tree species may be used for food/habitat across the range, but local individuals are selective. Their gut microbial community includes pathways associated with lignocellulose degradation, urea recycling and metabolism of Eucalyptus secondary compounds. Diet specialization reduces food competition but imposes a low-energy lifestyle.

### Social structure
They are predominantly solitary. Home ranges overlap and communication uses scent and vocal signals. During breeding, adult males bellow, mark trees and compete for space/access to females. Playback work shows adult males actively approaching some rival male calls.

### Reproduction and life history
Females can mature around two years; males are physiologically fertile young but generally do not become successful competitors until larger and older. Gestation is about one month, normally producing one tiny joey. The young develops in the pouch, later rides on the mother's back, and acquires digestive microbes during the transition to foliage.

### Conservation
The combined Queensland, NSW and ACT populations have been listed **Endangered under Australia's EPBC Act since 12 February 2022**. Major threats include habitat loss/fragmentation, drought and heat, bushfire, climate change, disease including chlamydia and koala retrovirus, dog attacks and vehicle strikes. Victoria and South Australia are outside that federal listed population.

### Major adaptations
- Opposed foredigits, rough pads, sharp claws and strong shoulders for climbing.
- Dense fur and geographic body-size/fur variation.
- Highly specialized hindgut fermentation and microbial community for Eucalyptus foliage.
- Energy-conserving low-activity lifestyle.
- Specialized male vocal anatomy for extremely low-frequency bellows.

### Human interaction
Koalas are a major Australian conservation flagship. Urban expansion and roads increase habitat fragmentation and direct mortality. Handling a wild koala is hazardous because defensive bites and scratches can be serious despite the species' popular docile image.

### Genuine fun facts
- A southern adult male can be nearly twice the mass of a Queensland male population average.
- Male bellows can average only about 27 Hz in fundamental frequency, far lower than expected for an animal of their body size.
- Koala forepaws have **two opposed digits**, not the single opposed thumb arrangement familiar in humans.
- They can gallop at up to about **30 km/h** when disturbed despite spending most of their lives in trees.
- Their gut microbiome is measurably tied to which Eucalyptus species individuals can use effectively.
- Koalas have only a vestigial external tail.

### Concise site-ready summary
A stocky Australian tree-dwelling marsupial specialized for Eucalyptus life, the koala combines a powerful double-thumb grip, sharp climbing claws and surprising short-burst ground speed with a very low-energy lifestyle. Mature southern males are the largest fighters, but the species remains a modest absolute combatant whose best defense is vertical terrain rather than raw damage or armor.

### Rich narrative profile
The koala is often misread as simply slow. Its biology is better understood as extreme specialization. A mature southern male is a muscular 12 kg arboreal grappler with sharp claws, opposed foredigits and enough short-burst speed to bound at roughly 30 km/h when forced onto the ground. In a tree, the same anatomy provides secure purchase and rapid vertical escape. In a close encounter it can bite and scratch, and breeding males do compete for space and access to females.

That does not make the koala a high-tier fighter. Its teeth are primarily a browsing system, its claws evolved for climbing, and it lacks armor, venom, horns or a large-carnivore killing bite. More importantly, its entire energy economy is constrained by a fibrous, chemically defended diet. The koala succeeds by extracting value from food that excludes many competitors and by conserving energy for most of the day, not by sustaining prolonged high-output activity.

Its most extraordinary adaptations therefore belong in Special rather than Attack. The gastrointestinal system and microbiome help process Eucalyptus secondary compounds, while males possess a specialized vocal apparatus capable of producing remarkably low bellows used in reproductive competition. These features make the koala biologically unusual without pretending that unusual biology equals heavyweight combat power.

## 11. Confidence and conflicts
- **Mass:** high confidence, but population variation is unusually large. A single species-wide mean would erase meaningful combat variation. The 12 kg canonical value intentionally represents the larger southern adult male fighting sex.
- **Length:** high-moderate confidence. 78 cm is an adult southern male mean, while the museum's broader species range is 68-82 cm.
- **Height:** unresolved, kept at 0.0 rather than mixing historical all-fours measurements with modern standardized fields.
- **Speed:** moderate confidence. Queensland Government supplies 30 km/h for a disturbed bounding gallop, but this is not a laboratory performance study and should not be used to inflate Agility or Stamina.
- **Bite force:** no defensible PSI. Finite-element cranial work is not a license to fabricate pressure.
- **Lifespan:** variable by sex, population and captivity. 15 years is a defensible canonical male figure, not a maximum.
- **Conservation:** federal Endangered status applies specifically to the combined QLD/NSW/ACT populations, not automatically to every koala in Victoria or South Australia.

## 12. Cross-animal normalization notes
- **Versus Japanese macaque (11.3 kg):** similar mass, but the macaque has far greater manipulative/tactical flexibility. Koala should remain substantially below it in Intelligence and combat versatility. Koala's claws/grip justify comparable or slightly stronger static grappling hardware, not a higher overall Attack.
- **Versus green iguana (7.0 kg):** koala is heavier and has stronger mammalian forelimb grappling, so Attack/Raw Power can sit slightly higher, while iguana retains more unusual defensive/locomotor Special mechanisms such as tail autotomy/regeneration.
- **Versus black-backed jackal (8.1 kg):** despite being heavier, koala lacks a carnivore's predatory bite, pursuit behavior and combat specialization, so it must remain below jackal Attack and practical terrestrial fighting capability.
- **Versus kiwi (3.1 kg):** koala has materially greater absolute mass, claws and raw power, hence higher Attack/Defense, but kiwi's exceptional sensory specialization justifies a higher Special score.
- **Versus kangaroo (80 kg):** the gap must remain enormous in Raw Power, Attack and Stamina. Koala's proportional climbing strength must not leak into heavyweight scores.
- **Anti-inflation check:** 30 km/h is a short disturbed gallop, not evidence for elite Agility; long sleep and low metabolism are survival efficiency, not endurance; Eucalyptus detoxification is Special, not toughness or poison resistance against unrelated toxins.
