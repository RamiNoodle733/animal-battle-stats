# Kea (*Nestor notabilis*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Kea (also mountain parrot or New Zealand mountain parrot)
- **Scientific name:** *Nestor notabilis* Gould, 1856
- **Exact animal represented:** the living New Zealand parrot *Nestor notabilis*, endemic to the forests, subalpine scrub and alpine zones of the South Island.
- **Status:** Living; Endangered (IUCN), Threatened–Nationally Endangered in New Zealand.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **0.96 kg**, **48.0 cm** total length. Males are heavier and have a longer upper bill.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** males average **956 g** and females **779 g**; female body mass is about 20% lower (NZ Birds Online). The male's upper bill is 12–14% longer than the female's.
- **Population notes:** adults weigh 750–1,000 g, with a mean of 922 g. Juveniles have yellow eye-rings, cere and lower bill. Juveniles gather in wandering flocks and have an unusually long juvenile period for a parrot.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The bill is the main weapon, helped by the claws. The kea's long, curved upper bill tears and pries. The clearest evidence of its offensive use is the long-disputed attacks on sheep. In 1992, nocturnal attacks were filmed, proving that at least some kea land on healthy sheep and use bill and claws to rip through the wool and eat fat from the back. The kea does not directly kill the sheep, but sheep can die from infection or from injuries while trying to escape. A few kea also prey on Hutton's shearwater chicks in the Seaward Kaikōura Range, and kea take mice and rabbits and scavenge deer, chamois, tahr and sheep carcasses.

### Offensive mechanics
Attacks on sheep happen mostly at night, often in winter, and snow-bound sheep with heavy fleece were historically the most vulnerable. The bird attacks from the sheep's back, tearing at the wool to reach fat. For other food it digs in soil for grubs and tubers, rips open rotten logs for huhu beetle larvae, and pulls apart plants.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It is a large, strong-flying parrot, at home in the gusty wind of mountain ridges and saddles, and it also walks, hops and climbs well on rock and ground. Agility is scored high for a heavy parrot.

### Endurance
Kea range across about four million hectares of mountains, from coastal dunes to high alpine peaks, and live through cold alpine winters, breeding even in winter. No measured daily flight distance was located. Stamina is scored moderate-strong.

### Senses
No sensory measurement was located. Kea find hidden food by digging and probing and quickly notice new objects, but no special sense is documented.

### Intelligence and tactics
This is the kea's standout category, with controlled experiments and some mixed results:
- Kea showed three signatures of statistical inference: they predicted sampling outcomes from relative quantities and combined that with physical information (a barrier) and social information (a biased experimenter) (*Nature Communications*, 2020).
- In a loose-string cooperation task, pairs that got along attempted and succeeded more often, but kea did not spontaneously understand their partner's role (*Animal Cognition*, 2016).
- A disabled kea named Bruce, missing his upper bill, chose pebbles of a particular size and used them as tools to preen himself (*Scientific Reports*, 2021).
- Kea did not infer object weight from movement in a breeze, unlike New Caledonian crows (*Biology Letters*, 2024).
- In the wild, kea solve locks and puzzles by trial and error and by watching others. DOC records kea turning on taps, using tools to get food, moving road cones and setting off traps to steal bait.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Adults form long-term monogamous pairs. Juvenile flocks develop flexible social hierarchies, and adults tolerate unrelated juveniles, which helps the young learn foraging skills. Kea play a great deal, and a special play call makes other kea start playing.

### Environmental strengths
- Alpine and subalpine terrain, windy ridges and cold weather.
- Mountain beech forest and scrub.
- Places with human objects, carcasses or sheep to exploit.

### Weaknesses
- Under 1 kg in absolute mass.
- No armor; small, light body.
- Bill and claws are tearing tools, not killing weapons for large animals.
- Curiosity leads to lead poisoning, traps, vehicles and other human hazards.
- Ground nests are vulnerable to stoats, possums and cats.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** grubs, small birds and chicks, very small mammals, puzzles and anything that can be outthought rather than outfought.
- **Mixed:** other parrots and corvids of similar size, where skill and persistence matter.
- **Bad:** raptors, carnivores of any real size, and large animals that can shake it off or strike back.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Wool-Ripping Beak** — the long, curved upper bill and gripping claws tear through thick fleece to reach fat on a live sheep's back, and dig out grubs and roots from soil and logs.
2. **Mountain Puzzle Solver** — kea combine quantities, physical barriers and social cues to make statistical predictions, innovate tools and open locks, letting them get into places and food that other animals cannot.

### Unique traits
1. **Alpine Parrot** — one of very few parrots living above the treeline, it forages across mountain beech forest, subalpine scrub and alpine herb-fields, nests on the ground and breeds in winter.
2. **Contagious Play Call** — kea have a special play call; playing recordings of it to wild kea made both juveniles and adults play more, evidence of positive emotional contagion in a bird.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The kea lives only on the South Island of New Zealand, over about four million hectares along the main mountain ranges from Farewell Spit to Waitutu and in the Kaikōura Ranges. It occurs from coastal dunes and lowland valleys on the West Coast to high alpine peaks, but is most common in montane forest and the subalpine and alpine zones above it. Bones show it once lived through lowland forest on the North Island as well.

### Diet and ecology
It is omnivorous. Kea eat shoots, fruits, leaves, nectar and seeds of more than 40 plant species; dig for insect larvae, grass grubs and plant tubers; rip open rotten logs for huhu grubs; and eat land snails. Animal food includes Hutton's shearwater chicks, mice, rabbits and carcasses of deer, chamois, tahr and sheep. Kea also spread the seeds of alpine plants.

### Social structure
Adults form long-term monogamous pairs. Juveniles gather in loose, wandering flocks, which breeding adults may join as they pass through. Flocks have flexible hierarchies, and unrelated adults tolerate juveniles, which helps young kea learn.

### Reproduction and life history
Kea nest on the ground in natural cavities, such as rock crevices, hollow tree bases or holes under roots, mostly in native forest. Usually four eggs are laid between July and January, with a peak in August–October. The female incubates and feeds the chicks while the male brings food to her. Nests fledge on average 1.6 young.

### Conservation
The kea is listed as **Endangered** by the IUCN and as Threatened–Nationally Endangered in New Zealand, with an estimated 1,000–5,000 birds (DOC). About 150,000 were killed for bounties between 1860 and 1970; protection came in stages, with full protection in 1986. Today's main threats are introduced predators (stoats, possums and cats) at nests, lead poisoning from old buildings, and conflict and accidents with people. At Aoraki / Mount Cook, all 38 live kea tested had detectable blood lead and 26 had dangerously high levels. Predator control greatly improves nest survival.

### Special features
- Long, narrow, strongly curved upper bill, longer in males.
- Olive-green plumage with bright orange-red underwings.
- Ground nesting and winter breeding.
- High curiosity, play and problem-solving ability.
- Long juvenile learning period.

### Human interaction
Kea are famous for investigating people and their gear at ski-fields, huts and car parks, pulling rubber from cars and carrying off small items, which has earned the nickname "clown of the mountains". Feeding kea is harmful and discouraged. They were once killed as sheep pests; today some attacks on sheep are tolerated because the birds are protected. The kea was voted New Zealand Bird of the Year in 2017.

### Genuine fun facts
- Kea have passed tests of statistical inference once thought to be limited to great apes among animals.
- A kea named Bruce, who lost his upper bill, uses pebbles as tools to preen.
- Hearing a recorded kea play call makes wild kea start playing.
- In 1992 kea were filmed attacking sheep at night and tearing through the wool to eat fat.
- DOC records kea turning on taps, moving road cones and setting off traps to steal bait.
- Kea nest on the ground and lay eggs in winter.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Kea is a large, clever mountain parrot from New Zealand's South Island. The canonical male weighs just under 1 kg. Its long, curved bill can rip through sheep wool, and it is among the best-tested bird problem solvers, showing statistical reasoning and tool innovation. In an absolute fight, its small size and lack of armor leave it far below real predators.

### Rich narrative profile
The kea is a parrot that lives where parrots usually do not: on windy ridges and alpine herb-fields of the Southern Alps, nesting on the ground and breeding through the mountain winter. It survives there by being curious. Kea dig, pry, rip and test almost everything they meet, from grubs in rotten logs to rubber on cars, traps set for pests and gear left outside huts.

That curiosity is backed by measured ability. In controlled studies, kea predicted sampling outcomes from ratios of objects while taking into account physical barriers and an experimenter's bias, a combination previously shown only in great apes. A disabled kea invented his own pebble tool for preening. At the same time, experiments show limits: kea did not grasp their partner's role in a cooperation task and did not infer weight from objects moving in a breeze. The picture is of a highly capable, exploratory problem solver rather than an all-purpose genius.

In an absolute matchup, the kea's bill is a tearing tool on a bird under 1 kg. It can wound a sheep that cannot shake it off and kill a seabird chick, but it has no armor and little mass. It wins by working out what an opponent or obstacle will do, not by overpowering anything that can fight back.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Population:** DOC gives 1,000–5,000; NZ Birds Online calls any estimate little more than a guess and discusses 4,000–6,000 as possible extremes.
- **Lifespan:** the 5-year wild median comes from one population's resighting data; some wild kea live over 20 years, and captive kea have reached 50.
- **Sheep attacks:** real and filmed, but only some kea do it and sheep usually die of secondary causes, so this is not scored as a killing attack on large animals.
- **Cognition:** most controlled results come from captive birds and include failures as well as successes; Intelligence is kept just below the top corvids and cockatoos.
- **Speed:** no measured speed; field left at 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Cockatoo (0.8 kg; A21.0 Ag86.0 I94.0 Sp76.0):** similar size. The kea's bill is used on live sheep, so Attack is slightly higher (22.0). Its cognitive evidence is strong but mixed, so Intelligence sits just below at 92.0.
- **Versus Macaw (1.2 kg; A24.0 I86.0):** the macaw's heavier crushing bill keeps it slightly above on Attack; the kea's controlled evidence of statistical inference and tool innovation puts it above on Intelligence.
- **Versus Raven (1.3 kg; A17.0 St87.0 I95.0):** the kea is more willing to use its bill on large animals (Attack 22.0 vs 17.0); the raven keeps higher Stamina and Intelligence.
- **Versus Golden Eagle (5.0 kg; A40.0):** raptors are among the kea's natural threats and stay far above it on every physical category.
- **Anti-inflation check:** sheep attacks are counted as proof of bill use, not as the ability to kill large animals, and intelligence does not raise Attack or Defense.
