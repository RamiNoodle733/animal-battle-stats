# Leopard Seal (*Hydrurga leptonyx*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Leopard Seal
- **Scientific name:** *Hydrurga leptonyx* (de Blainville, 1820), family Phocidae (true seals)
- **Exact animal represented:** the leopard seal of the Antarctic pack ice and Southern Ocean, the only species in its genus.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **420.0 kg** and **300.0 cm standard length**, representing a large, fighting-fit cow rather than a record animal.
- **Sex choice:** in this species the females are the larger sex. The Australian Antarctic Division gives average adult males about 2.8 m and 320 kg and females about 3.0 m and 370 kg, with large females above 3.5 m and 500 kg. At Cape Shirreff, adult females averaged **454 ± 59 kg** and **302 ± 11 cm**, against **302 ± 22 kg** and **276 ± 11 cm** for adult males; the smallest adult female weighed 9 kg more than the largest adult male. The larger sex is used as the combat specimen, as the report spec requires.
- **Population variation:** Cape Shirreff females, which feed heavily on fur seal pups and penguins, are heavier than the circumpolar averages. Canonical 420 kg sits between the Australian Antarctic Division average and the Cape Shirreff mean.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The leopard seal is the Southern Ocean's top predator of warm-blooded prey after the killer whale. Its weapon is a large head with long canine-like teeth on a flexible, powerful neck. It seizes penguins and seals, then shakes and smashes them against the water surface to break them apart. At Cape Shirreff, about 20 leopard seals, mostly a few large females, caused the loss of roughly 70% of Antarctic fur seal pups to predation. Its bite is serious against penguins, pups and similarly sized seals, but it lacks claws or tusks as secondary weapons.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A long, streamlined body and large fore-flippers make it a fast, agile swimmer for its size, able to intercept penguins in open water and at the ice edge. On land or ice it moves like other true seals, by humping along on its belly, and is slow and clumsy.

### Endurance
Tagged leopard seals at Livingston Island made short, shallow dives, averaging about **29 m** and **3 minutes**. Longer and deeper dives occur, with one reported at about **1,256 m** and 25 minutes. Males sing underwater for up to 13 hours a day in two-minute cycles, surfacing between phrases. This supports strong sustained aquatic activity, though it is not in the same class as deep-diving elephant seals.

### Senses
Leopard seals hunt by sight at the ice edge and in open water, and like other true seals they carry whiskers that detect water movement. Species-specific sensory tests were not found, so the score relies on general phocid capability and hunting success.

### Intelligence and tactics
Individual leopard seals develop their own hunting specialties. At Cape Shirreff, a few large females accounted for most fur seal pup kills, and one tagged female used repeated ambush attacks. Seals wait just below the water line where penguins must enter or leave the sea, striking when prey is most exposed. They also switch between hunting large prey and filter-feeding krill as conditions change. Males produce patterned songs built from five call types. These points support strong tactical flexibility for a solitary predator.

### Sociality and hunting
Leopard seals are solitary. They haul out alone on ice floes and hunt alone. Genomic work shows they return to the same haul-out sites across generations.

### Fighting and aggression
A bold predator that attacks penguins and seals close to people and research stations. In 2003 a leopard seal killed British marine biologist Kirsty Brown while she was snorkelling near Rothera Research Station, holding her underwater for about six minutes; it is the only recorded human death caused by the species. A British Antarctic Survey review found that leopard seal responses to people were strongest at the ice edge, where they hunt.

### Environmental strengths
- Open water and ice edges where prey must enter the sea.
- Cold, deep water where its size and blubber are advantages.
- Fights that start underwater, away from the opponent's footing.

### Weaknesses
- Slow and clumsy on land and ice.
- No armor; exposed to deep bites from larger predators such as killer whales.
- Single main weapon (the bite).
- Short routine dives compared with deep-diving seals.
- Far outmatched by killer whales and very large sharks.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** penguins, smaller seals and pups, and medium animals that fight in water.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized pinnipeds and large carnivores at the water's edge.
- **Bad:** killer whales, very large sharks, and any fight on land or ice.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Ice-Edge Ambush** — waits just below the water line where penguins must jump in or climb out, then seizes and shakes them apart against the surface. In a battle it strikes when an opponent is entering or leaving the water.
2. **Krill Sieve** — sucks krill into the mouth and pushes water out through interlocking three-pointed cheek teeth, the same jaws that kill penguins and seals. This lets it switch between tiny prey and large prey, so it rarely runs out of food.

### Unique traits
1. **Heavier Females** — adult females outweigh males, averaging about 454 kg against 302 kg at Cape Shirreff, where the smallest adult female was heavier than the largest adult male.
2. **Antarctic Songster** — males sing underwater for up to 13 hours a day, with loud, patterned songs built from five call types and structured as predictably as nursery rhymes.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Leopard seals live around Antarctica, mainly in the pack ice zone, and reach sub-Antarctic islands such as Kerguelen; wandering animals reach the coasts of southern continents, and the species appears on South Africa's national Red List. They haul out on ice floes and, at some sites, on beaches.

### Diet and ecology
They feed at both the top and near the bottom of the Southern Ocean food web. Krill can make up anywhere from about half to most of the diet depending on place and season, and one examined seal held more than 10,000 freshly caught krill. They also kill penguins, Antarctic fur seal pups and other seals. Studies at Cape Shirreff showed that a small number of leopard seals can drive local declines of fur seals.

### Social structure
Solitary. Adults haul out and hunt alone. Genomic analyses indicate that leopard seals return to the same haul-out sites across generations.

### Reproduction and life history
Females give birth and nurse on the pack ice. During the breeding season males sing long underwater songs. Breeding behavior is hard to study because it happens on remote pack ice.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List. Population size is uncertain: the most recent circumpolar estimate exceeds 35,000 animals but is considered a large underestimate, and the population trend is unknown. At Cape Shirreff, leopard seal numbers fell by about 76% from 2017 to 2023. Climate-driven change in sea ice is a commonly raised long-term concern.

### Special features
- Long, streamlined body and large head with a wide gape.
- Long canine-like front teeth for gripping large prey.
- Three-pointed interlocking cheek teeth that sieve krill.
- Large fore-flippers for fast, agile swimming.
- Loud underwater songs from males.
- Spotted throat and flanks that give it its common name.

### Human interaction
Leopard seals are curious and sometimes approach divers, boats and photographers. Most encounters are harmless, but the 2003 death of Kirsty Brown at Rothera led to a study of leopard seal and human interactions over the previous thirty years, which found the ice edge to be the highest-risk setting.

### Genuine fun facts
- Female leopard seals are larger than males, the reverse of most large seals.
- One leopard seal's stomach held more than **10,000** freshly caught krill.
- Males can sing underwater for up to **13 hours a day**.
- About 20 leopard seals at Cape Shirreff were linked to the loss of roughly **70%** of fur seal pups there.
- The deepest dive reported for the species is about **1,256 m**, although most dives are around 30 m.
- Its cheek teeth work like a sieve, the same way a baleen whale strains krill.

### Concise site-ready summary
The leopard seal is Antarctica's top seal predator, a 420 kg, 3 m hunter with a huge head, long gripping teeth and a snake-like, agile body in the water. It ambushes penguins at the ice edge, kills other seals, and also sieves krill through its three-pointed cheek teeth. It is dangerous in the water but slow on land and has no armor.

### Rich narrative profile
A leopard seal at the ice edge is a problem for every penguin that has to cross it. The seal waits just under the surface where birds jump in or scramble out, and when one hits the water it is seized and thrashed against the surface until it breaks apart. The same large head and long front teeth let leopard seals take fur seal pups and other seals, and at Cape Shirreff a few big females were enough to wipe out most of a colony's pups in some seasons.

What makes the species unusual is that the same mouth also works as a strainer. Its cheek teeth have three points that interlock like a comb. When krill are plentiful, a leopard seal sucks them in and pushes the water out through that sieve, eating thousands at a time. It can therefore live at the top of the food web and near its base, switching as conditions change. Males add another surprise, filling the water under the pack ice with patterned songs for up to 13 hours a day.

In ABS terms the leopard seal is an aquatic predator with a strong bite, high Agility in water and strong tactics for a solitary animal. Its Defense is moderate because it relies on size and blubber, not armor, and it is badly outmatched by killer whales. Out of the water it is slow, but in the water it is one of the most dangerous seals in the world.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** the Cape Shirreff female mean (454 kg) is well above the Australian Antarctic Division average (370 kg); canonical 420 kg splits the difference.
- **Speed:** the 37 km/h figure appears only in popular summaries.
- **Record dive:** the 1,256 m figure comes from a university news summary and should be checked against the paper.
- **Population:** estimates range from about 18,000 mature animals to more than 35,000 total, and all are considered uncertain.
- **Human attack details:** reported depth and duration of the 2003 attack vary between accounts.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Sea Lion (300 kg; 55.0 / 49.0 / 84.0 / 84.0 / 78.0 / 72.0):** the leopard seal is heavier and a true predator of warm-blooded prey, so Attack and Defense are higher. The sea lion keeps higher Agility, Stamina and Intelligence for its otariid mobility, long migrations and strong learning evidence.
- **Versus Seal (harbor seal, 105 kg; 44.0 / 48.0 / 82.0 / 82.0 / 74.0 / 84.0):** four times the mass and a macropredator's bite put the leopard seal far above it in Attack. The harbor seal keeps higher Special because its whisker trail-tracking is experimentally proven.
- **Versus Polar Bear (500 kg; 78.0 / 72.0 / 62.0 / 88.0 / 73.0 / 77.0):** the bear adds claws, more mass and strong land performance, so it keeps higher Attack and Defense; the leopard seal is more agile in water.
- **Versus Walrus:** no walrus research report exists yet, and the live walrus values are legacy data (for example Attack 1.7), so the walrus is not usable as an anchor. On evidence, a 1,200 kg tusked walrus should sit above the leopard seal in Defense and Raw Power.
- **Versus Orca (5,000 kg; Attack 94.0):** a killer whale is more than ten times heavier and a specialist hunter of seals; every force category stays far below the orca.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Attack 70.0 reflects a single-weapon predator of prey smaller than itself. Stamina is not raised to deep-diver levels because routine dives are short and shallow.
