# Thresher Shark (*Alopias vulpinus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Thresher shark (common thresher)
- **Scientific name:** *Alopias vulpinus* (Bonnaterre, 1788)
- **Exact animal represented:** the common thresher, the largest of the three thresher shark species. About half of its total length is the long, whip-like upper lobe of the tail. The pelagic thresher (*A. pelagicus*) and bigeye thresher (*A. superciliosus*) are separate species and are used here only where noted.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **450 cm** total length (tail included) and **230 kg**, matching FishBase's common length and the "commonly 5 m and 230 kg" size in secondary summaries, rather than the 5.7 m confirmed maximum or the 510 kg heaviest record.
- **Sex choice:** in the eastern North Pacific, males mature at about 3.3 m and five years, and females at about 2.6–4.5 m and seven years (Florida Museum, Wikipedia). The heaviest recorded animal was a female, and females reach slightly greater maximum lengths in FishBase (549 cm female record). A mature female is used.
- **Population notes:** because half the length is tail, a 4.5 m thresher has a body only about 2.2–2.5 m long. Lengths must not be compared directly with sharks of normal shape.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Small overlapping dermal denticles cover the skin. The long tail is flexible and is often snagged on longline hooks.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The thresher's primary weapon is its tail. Underwater video from southern California recorded 34 common thresher feeding events between 2007 and 2009, and every one began with a strike from the upper lobe of the tail (Aalbers et al. 2010). Threshers circle schools of fish, often in pairs or small groups, splashing to pack them tight, then whip the tail overhead or sideways to stun or kill prey before turning back to collect it.

Kinematics come from the closely related pelagic thresher: tail-slaps are started by snapping the pectoral fins inward, which pitches the body and flings the rear end up and over; the tail tip averaged 14.0 m/s and reached a mean of 21.8 m/s in the fastest strike, fast enough to form bubbles in the water, and successful strikes yielded an average of 3.6 sardines (Oliver et al. 2013). A 2024 study found that the common thresher's vertebrae change in size and mineral content along the body in ways that support the extreme up-and-down bending needed for tail-whipping.

The secondary weapon is a small mouth with small, smooth teeth suited to swallowing fish and squid. Against a large opponent the tail can bruise and stun but cannot cut, and the bite is weak for a shark of this length.

### Defense and durability
Moderate mass with no armor. The long tail is a liability as well as a weapon: threshers are often hooked by the tail after striking at bait. The slender body carries less mass than its length suggests.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A fast, strong swimmer that can leap completely out of the water. The common thresher keeps its red swimming muscle warm (see Endurance), and the tail-whip shows unusual control of pitch and body bending. Its turning in pursuit is good but the long tail makes it less compact than a mako.

### Endurance
The common thresher is regionally endothermic: its red swimming muscle sits deep in the body next to heat-exchanging blood vessels and runs warmer than the surrounding water (Patterson et al. 2011). Its blood carries oxygen equally well across temperatures, like the mako's (J. Exp. Biol. 2023). It is migratory, following warm water to higher latitudes. Archival tags off California recorded dives to 320 m and water as cold as 9.1 °C (Cartamil et al. 2011).

### Senses
Standard shark sensory suite with relatively large eyes for a surface-oriented predator; species-specific thresholds were not found.

### Intelligence and tactics
Hunting shows planning: threshers herd and compact schools, sometimes cooperatively in pairs or small groups, wind up before striking, then turn back to collect stunned prey. They also switch prey according to what is abundant in cold and warm years.

### Hunting behavior
Feeds on schooling fishes such as mackerels, bluefish, herrings, needlefish, lancetfish and lanternfish, plus squid, octopus, pelagic crustaceans and, rarely, seabirds (FishBase). Off California, threshers stay above the shallow thermocline at night and dive deeper by day to follow prey.

### Intraspecific fighting and predator defense
No documented fighting between threshers. Immature threshers are eaten by larger sharks. Adults rely on speed and leaps.

### Aggression
Timid. The Florida Museum calls it mostly harmless to people because of its small mouth and teeth, and the International Shark Attack File lists only one provoked attack and a few incidents with boats, probably from sharks fighting capture.

### Environmental strengths
- Open water around dense schools of small fish.
- Surface and upper water column where the tail can be swung freely.
- Cool temperate seas, thanks to warm swimming muscle.

### Weaknesses
- Small mouth and teeth; cannot cut a large opponent.
- Tail strikes stun small prey but do little to armored or large animals.
- No armor; slender body.
- Tail is easily snagged or damaged.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** schooling fish, squid and small fast prey that the tail can stun.
- **Mixed:** mid-sized fish and sharks that must be hit repeatedly by the tail.
- **Bad:** large biting sharks, orcas and any heavily built opponent that can close to bite.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Overhead Tail-Whip** — the shark snaps its pectoral fins in, pitches its body and throws the upper tail lobe overhead like a whip. In the pelagic thresher the tip moves at about 14 m/s on average and over 20 m/s in the fastest strikes, stunning or killing several fish at once.
2. **Warm-Muscle Cruise** — deep, heat-retaining red swimming muscle keeps working in cool water, supporting long migrations and repeated dives below the thermocline.

### Unique traits
1. **Half-Tail Body Plan** — the upper tail lobe is about as long as the rest of the shark, so a 4.5 m thresher carries a body only about half that length.
2. **School Herder** — threshers circle and splash to pack schools of fish tightly, sometimes in pairs or small groups, before striking.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The common thresher lives in temperate and subtropical seas worldwide, both near the coast and in the open ocean, mostly within about 30 km of land. It is usually near the surface but dives to at least 550 m. It migrates toward higher latitudes with warm water, while juveniles use warm nursery areas.

### Diet and ecology
Feeds mainly on schooling fishes such as mackerels, bluefish, herrings and sardines, needlefish, lancetfish and lanternfish, plus squid, octopus and pelagic crustaceans, and rarely seabirds. It is less selective in warm El Niño years when preferred prey is scarce.

### Social structure
Usually seen alone, but threshers sometimes hunt in pairs or small groups when herding schools of fish.

### Reproduction and life history
Ovoviviparous with oophagy: embryos feed on unfertilised eggs inside the mother. Pups are born at about 114–160 cm and 5–6 kg, and juveniles grow about 50 cm a year. Males mature at about 3.3 m and five years; females at about 2.6–4.5 m and seven years.

### Conservation
Listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List, having been moved from Data Deficient to Vulnerable in 2007 and reassessed in 2018. It is caught in drift gillnet, longline and recreational fisheries, and its slow reproduction makes populations slow to recover.

### Special features
- Upper tail lobe about as long as the rest of the body.
- Tail-whip strikes started by pectoral-fin adduction.
- Vertebrae shaped and mineralised to support extreme up-and-down bending.
- Regionally endothermic red swimming muscle.
- Small, smooth-edged teeth in a small mouth.

### Human interaction
Harmless to swimmers in practice; the few recorded incidents involved hooked sharks. Threshers are a valued sport fish and are often hooked by the tail after striking at bait with it.

### Genuine fun facts
- In a southern California study, every one of 34 recorded thresher feeding events began with a tail strike.
- A thresher's tail tip can move fast enough to make bubbles form in the water.
- One well-aimed tail-slap can stun several fish at once; pelagic threshers averaged 3.6 sardines per successful strike.
- Threshers are often caught by the tail because they hit the bait with it.
- An old sailors' tale claimed threshers teamed up with swordfish to attack whales; it was probably inspired by orcas.
- Thresher pups can be up to 1.6 m long at birth.
- The common thresher keeps its swimming muscles warmer than the sea around it.

### Concise site-ready summary
The thresher shark hunts with its tail. Half of its 4.5 m length is a whip-like upper tail lobe that it throws overhead fast enough to stun whole groups of fish. Warm muscles make it a strong, leaping swimmer, but its small mouth and slender unarmored body make it weak against large opponents.

### Rich narrative profile
The common thresher looks like a normal shark that has been given someone else's tail. The upper lobe is as long as the rest of the body, so a 4.5 m, 230 kg female is really a 2.2 m shark trailing an equally long whip. That whip is its main weapon. Circling a school of fish, sometimes with a partner, the thresher splashes to herd the fish together, snaps its pectoral fins in to pitch forward, and throws the tail over its head or sweeps it sideways.

Studies of the related pelagic thresher show the tail tip moving at around 14 m/s and sometimes over 20 m/s, fast enough to produce bubbles, and a good strike leaves several stunned fish to be collected. In southern California, every recorded common thresher feeding event began with the tail. The shark's vertebrae are built to handle this extreme bending, and its warm red muscle keeps it strong and fast in cool water, even allowing it to leap clear of the sea.

The thresher's weakness is everything except the tail. Its mouth and teeth are small, its body is slender and unarmored, and its long tail is easily hooked or damaged. Against schooling fish it is one of the most specialised hunters in the ocean; against a large biting shark it would have to rely on speed to escape.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** published weights range from about 230 kg (common) to 348 kg (FishBase) and 510 kg (record). 230 kg is conservative.
- **Length:** the Florida Museum's 7.6 m maximum conflicts with FishBase and Wikipedia (5.7 m confirmed) and is not used.
- **Tail speed:** measured in the pelagic thresher, a smaller species of the same genus; common thresher strikes may differ.
- **Lifespan:** only 15 years is confirmed; 25 years follows FishBase.
- **Speed:** no instrumented measurement; `speed_mps` stays 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Mako Shark (275 kg; A75 D53 Ag85 St88 I64 Sp86):** both are warm-muscled pelagic sharks of similar mass. The mako's long grasping teeth and measured bursts keep it above in Attack (75.0 vs 56.0) and Agility (85.0 vs 80.0). The thresher's tail-whip is a rarer mechanism, but the mako's endothermy is stronger, so Special is close (84.0 vs 86.0).
- **Versus Swordfish (legacy, not yet researched; A21.8 D24.2 Ag92 St89.1):** the swordfish values are legacy placeholders. Both use a non-bite weapon (bill vs tail) to slash or stun schooling prey; a researched swordfish would likely land near the thresher in Attack, well above the legacy 21.8.
- **Versus Tiger Shark (legacy, not yet researched; A87.9 D63.6 Ag85.1):** a heavier shark with can-opener teeth should stay far above the thresher in Attack and Defense once researched.
- **Versus Marlin (250 kg; A58 D41 Ag78 St90) and Sailfish (70 kg; A52 Ag89):** billfishes also slash schooling prey with a specialised body part. The thresher's Attack sits between the two (56.0), Defense is higher than the marlin's through denser shark build, and Agility is just above the marlin's.
- **Versus Oceanic Whitetip Shark (150 kg; A71 D53 Ag70 St80):** the whitetip is lighter but has a serrated cutting bite, so its Attack is much higher; the thresher is faster, warmer and more agile.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the tail-whip is counted in Weaponry (as a damage tool) and in Special/Abilities (as a rare mechanism), but Attack is held at 56.0 because it stuns small fish rather than disabling large animals. Ferocity 30.0 reflects its timid nature.
