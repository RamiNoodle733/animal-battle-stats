# Common Eland (*Taurotragus oryx*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Common eland (southern eland, eland antelope)
- **Scientific name:** *Taurotragus oryx* (Pallas, 1766); some recent works place it in *Tragelaphus* as *Tragelaphus oryx*
- **Exact animal represented:** the common eland of East and Southern African savanna, woodland and montane grassland, not the giant or Derby eland (*Taurotragus derbianus*) of West and Central Africa.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature bull of about **700 kg**, **165 cm** at the shoulder and **290 cm** head-body length, with full horns, dewlap and forehead brush. This sits between the commonly quoted typical bull range (500–600 kg) and the heavy old bulls of 900 kg or more.
- **Sex choice:** bulls are much larger and heavier than cows (cows about 300–600 kg), carry thicker, shorter horns and are the sex that wrestles for dominance.
- **Population variation:** bull mass rises with age and body condition; old bulls with large dewlaps and dark facemasks are markedly heavier. Maximum figures near 1,000 kg are not used as the representative baseline.

### Speed
- Wikipedia: the slowest antelope, with a peak of about **40 km/h** that tires it quickly, but able to hold a **22 km/h** trot indefinitely. AWF also states it can trot indefinitely. No instrumented measurement was located.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 11.1 m/s** (40 km/h), low-moderate confidence.

### Lifespan
- Wikipedia: life expectancy generally **15–20 years**, up to 25 in captivity. AWF: 15–20 years. ADW: maximum 25 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 17.5**, the middle of the 15–20 year span.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Horns and bite
- **Horns:** both sexes. Bulls' horns are thicker, shorter and more tightly spiralled, **43–66 cm**; cows' are **51–69 cm** and thinner. The Kruger guide mentions recorded lengths up to 1 m, which is treated as exceptional.
- A 2018 *Journal of Anatomy* study of eight bull horncores found denser, stronger, more mineralized bone at the base than the tip, and suggested the spiral ridge helps grip and may deflect fractures during wrestling.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Elands do not fight by biting; no measurement exists.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Protection comes from sheer size, a thick neck and a large hanging dewlap in old bulls, and the herd's vigilance.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The eland's weapons are thick, tightly spiralled horns on a body that can weigh 700 kg or more. Bulls fight by feeling out each other's horns and then pushing with all their strength, wrestling with locked horns and butting heads. Cows use their horns to defend calves from predators. Mass gives shoving and ramming power, but the eland's temperament is mild; it has been domesticated for meat and milk, and wild animals generally avoid conflict.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It is the slowest antelope in a sprint, peaking around 40 km/h and tiring quickly, but it is a remarkable jumper for its size: it can clear 1.5 m fences from a standstill (ADW, AWF) and leaps up to 2.5 m when startled, according to Wikipedia. Eland farmers report that they jump fences as high as 3 m or break through them with their mass. Turning at speed is limited by weight.

### Endurance
Stamina is the eland's strength. It can hold a trot of about 22 km/h indefinitely, travels nomadically between feeding areas, and gets most of its water from food. It regulates heat by adjusting skin evaporation and can conserve water by letting its body temperature rise. In a Namibian accelerometer study, elands reduced walking effort on the hottest days and were almost never recorded running.

### Senses
Vigilant herd animal with good hearing and smell; old bulls bark and trot back and forth to alert the herd to predators. No sensory thresholds were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Herd alarm signalling, a linear dominance hierarchy among females, and a rich signalling system among bulls. A study of eland bulls found the dominant frequency of their loud knee-clicks honestly signals body size, dewlap size tracks age, and facemask darkness and forehead brush size track aggression. Rivals can assess each other without fighting.

### Sociality and hunting
Nomadic and crepuscular, forming herds of up to 500 that split into male, female and nursery groups. A browser and grazer, using its horns to break off high branches.

### Fighting and aggression
Bull contests are wrestling and shoving matches with locked horns; the multi-signal display system resolves many contests before contact. Elands are generally placid, startle easily and flee.

### Predator defense
Lions and spotted hyenas are the main predators of adults; African wild dogs and cheetahs take calves and younger animals. Defense relies on vigilance, alarm barks, herd bunching, mass and cows' horn defense of calves.

### Environmental strengths
- Open savanna, woodland and montane grassland with room to trot away.
- Hot, dry country, where its water economy and heat control help.
- Broken fence lines and scrub, where standing jumps give escape routes.

### Weaknesses
- Slow in a sprint and quick to tire at top speed.
- No armor; flanks and belly are soft.
- Mild temperament; usually flees rather than fights.
- Horns are wrestling tools rather than lethal stabbing weapons.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** medium predators and smaller herbivores that it can outweigh; opponents that cannot keep up over long distances.
- **Mixed:** single large carnivores such as a lion or big hyena, where mass and horns can deter but not always stop an attack.
- **Bad:** buffalo, rhinos and other heavier, more aggressive herbivores; lion prides and hyena clans; fast sprinters that force short, sharp engagements.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Endless Trot** — holds a trot of about 22 km/h indefinitely and gets most of its water from food, outlasting pursuers rather than outrunning them.
2. **Standing Leap** — despite weighing around 700 kg, it can clear fences of 1.5 m or more from a standstill when startled.

### Unique traits
1. **Giant Among Antelopes** — bulls commonly weigh 500–900 kg, far more than the kudu, sable, oryx or any other savanna antelope on the roster.
2. **Clicking Knees** — bulls' knees make a loud click as they walk, and the pitch honestly reveals body size to rivals.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
East and Southern Africa, from Ethiopia and South Sudan to South Africa, including Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia, Malawi, Mozambique, Zimbabwe, Botswana, Namibia and Angola. It uses savanna, woodland and open and montane grasslands with a wide variety of plants and avoids dense forest.

### Diet and ecology
A mixed feeder that browses leaves, shoots and twigs and grazes grasses, shifting with the season. It uses its horns to break off branches out of reach and can live largely on the water in its food.

### Social structure
Nomadic herds of up to 500 animals with separate male, female and nursery groups; females have a linear hierarchy. Old bulls often wander alone or in small groups. Elands are not territorial.

### Reproduction and life history
Females mature at 15–36 months and males at four to five years. Mating peaks in the rainy season. About a day after birth, mother and calf join a nursery group, where the calf stays until it is about two years old.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. The 2016 assessment estimated 90,000–110,000 mature individuals, about half in protected areas and 30% on private land, with stable or increasing populations in several countries. Numbers are slowly falling elsewhere through habitat loss and poaching for meat. It disappeared from Eswatini and Zimbabwe and has been reintroduced.

### Special features
- Thick, tightly spiralled horns in both sexes.
- Large dewlap and dark forehead brush in old bulls.
- Loud knee-clicks that signal body size.
- Standing jumps of 1.5 m and more.
- Water economy and heat control suited to dry savanna.

### Human interaction
Farmed for meat and milk in South Africa, Zimbabwe, Kenya and Russia; eland milk carries 11–17% butterfat and keeps for months when properly prepared. Farmers note that elands can jump or break through high fences.

### Genuine fun facts
- The common eland is the slowest antelope in a sprint but can trot at about 22 km/h indefinitely.
- A bull's knee-click pitch tells rivals how big he is.
- Elands can clear a 1.5 m fence from a standing start.
- Eland milk carries 11–17% butterfat and, properly prepared, can keep for up to eight months.
- Female elands have longer horns than males; bulls' horns are thicker and more tightly twisted.
- A herd can number up to 500 animals.
- Elands are farmed for meat and milk in Russia as well as in southern and eastern Africa.

### Concise site-ready summary
The common eland is Africa's giant savanna antelope, a 700 kg bull with thick spiral horns, a heavy dewlap and knees that click as it walks. Slow in a sprint but almost tireless at a trot and able to leap fences from a standstill, it wins by outlasting threats rather than fighting them.

### Rich narrative profile
A mature eland bull is built like an ox: deep-chested, heavy-necked, with a pendulous dewlap, a dark tuft of hair on the forehead and short, thick horns wound in a tight spiral. He may weigh 700 kg or more, and as he walks his knees make a sharp click that carries across the savanna. Research on eland bulls shows that the pitch of that click tracks body size, so rivals can judge each other by sound before any contact.

For all that mass, the eland is a gentle animal. Herds drift nomadically across savanna and woodland, browsing and grazing, and bolt when startled. They are slow sprinters that tire at top speed, but they can trot for hours and live mostly on the water in their food. When cornered by a fence they simply jump it from a standstill, which is why eland farming needs very high fences.

On the ABS scale the eland is a heavyweight with a light fighting temperament. Its size and raw power put it above the kudu and sable antelope and level with the moose, while its placid nature, wrestling-style horns and slow sprint keep its Attack and Agility well below the Cape buffalo. Its standout rating is Stamina.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** typical 500–600 kg vs 590–997 kg and ~900 kg summaries; 700 kg bridges typical and old bulls.
- **Jump height:** 1.5 m from a standstill (ADW, AWF) vs up to 2.5 m when startled (Wikipedia); both kept, with 1.5 m as the conservative figure.
- **Horn length:** 43–66 cm in bulls vs a 1 m record on a tourism site; the record is treated as exceptional.
- **Speed:** 40 km/h has no stated method; kept at low-moderate confidence.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Kudu (230 kg; A58 Ag72) and Sable Antelope (235 kg; A62 Ag72):** the eland is three times heavier, so Size, Raw Power, Attack (65.0) and Defense (57.0 vs 43.0/48.0) sit higher, but its slow sprint and heavy frame put Agility (54.0) far below both. The sable keeps higher Weaponry (72.0 vs 60.0) and Ferocity for its lethal scimitar horns and readiness to fight.
- **Versus Cape Buffalo (750 kg; A76 D70):** similar mass, but the buffalo's horn boss, aggression and toughness keep it well ahead in Attack and Defense. The eland edges it in Stamina (76.0 vs 73.0) on its tireless trot and water economy.
- **Versus Moose (550 kg; A69 D50):** the eland is heavier with higher Defense, but the moose keeps higher Attack and Ferocity for its readiness to charge and stamp.
- **Versus African Lion (A70):** lions regularly kill adult elands, so eland Attack stays below lion Attack while its Defense (57.0 vs 54.0) reflects its much greater mass.
