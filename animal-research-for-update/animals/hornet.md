# Hornet (*Vespa crabro*) research report

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Hornet, resolved here to European hornet.
- **Scientific name:** *Vespa crabro* Linnaeus, 1758.
- **Family:** Vespidae.
- **Living:** yes.
- **Exact animal represented:** the production label says only `Hornet` / *Vespa*. For a stable species-level profile this report resolves it to the European hornet, *Vespa crabro*, rather than mixing measurements from giant hornets, yellow-legged hornets, or non-*Vespa* wasps commonly called hornets.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature **adult queen**, approximately **0.0008 kg (0.8 g)** and **3.2 cm** long. Queens are the largest caste and, unlike males, are female and possess the sting apparatus. A queen is therefore the strongest defensible individual specimen, although workers do most routine hunting and colony defense.
- **Dimorphism/caste variation:** Penn State reports workers to about 25 mm and queens to 35 mm. Canadian Journal of Arthropod Identification gives forewing lengths of 16–20 mm in workers and about 22–24 mm in females/queens. Males cannot sting. Published thermoregulation work measured worker hornets around 477.5 ± 59.9 mg; a broader engineering synthesis gives *V. crabro* mass around 0.495–0.900 g. Queen mass is less cleanly standardized, so 0.8 g is a moderate-confidence representative rather than a measured population mean.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Size and mass
The strongest institutional size figure is **up to 35 mm for queens**, versus workers up to about 25 mm. The canonical 32 mm avoids treating the upper reported 35 mm as an average. The 0.8 g mass is placed near the upper portion of published *V. crabro* mass values because the canonical specimen is the larger queen caste.

### Flight and locomotion
A 2006 physiological study experimentally examined hornet flight under changing temperature and illumination. Later peer-reviewed synthesis cites *V. crabro* flight around **1.86 m/s**, while a Pest Management Science engineering table compiles a broader **1.34–5.9 m/s** range for *V. crabro*. These are flight-performance values, not a certified free-flight maximum. To avoid promoting the upper engineering range as a biological maximum, the canonical `speed_mps` is **1.9 m/s**, with the 5.9 m/s literature ceiling retained as context.

Hornets can hover, accelerate, land on small targets and maneuver in three dimensions. Agility therefore legitimately exceeds their absolute Attack/Defense, but speed alone does not determine Agility.

### Sting and venom
A comparative aculeate study measured the *V. crabro* sting at **5.39 ± 0.28 mm**, roughly 60% of mesosoma length. Only females possess the sting. The sting is not honey-bee-like disposable equipment, so a female can deliver repeated stings.

Venom is a real biochemical weapon but must be calibrated by opponent. A peer-reviewed study found an LD50 of **4.0 mg/kg** when crude *V. crabro* venom was experimentally injected into *V. crabro* workers. The same paper summarizes older rodent estimates of **8.7–10.9 mg/kg** and insect-prey estimates around **2.7–7.6 mg/kg**. These are toxicity measures, not delivered dose per sting and not a universal lethality score. They support strong Special/Weaponry for an insect while not making a sub-gram hornet physically powerful against large vertebrates.

### Mandibles and exoskeleton
Mandibles cut prey and wood fibers and help process captured insects. No defensible species-specific bite force or PSI value was found. The chitinous exoskeleton provides meaningful protection at insect scale but negligible absolute protection against vertebrate crushing, swatting or biting.

### Lifespan
The colony is annual. Workers generally live only weeks, while a new mated queen can overwinter and bridge one colony season to the next. A queen lifespan around **1.0 year** is a reasonable canonical caste value; it is not the lifespan of a worker.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offensive mechanics
The primary weapon is the female sting, a roughly 5.4 mm piercing venom-delivery apparatus. Flight allows the hornet to approach from above or behind, disengage, and re-engage. Mandibles are important against arthropod prey and for cutting, but their absolute force is tiny on the full roster scale.

### Defense and durability
The exoskeleton, flight, small target size and rapid repositioning make a hornet difficult for similarly sized opponents to control. Against large animals, however, almost any clean crush or bite is catastrophic. Defense therefore remains extremely low in absolute terms despite excellent evasion.

### Endurance and thermoregulation
*V. crabro* is capable of endothermic thoracic warming during flight and foraging across a wide temperature range. Experimental work found worker flight speed remained comparatively stable across ambient temperatures, with low illumination reducing worker flight speed. This supports useful flight stamina and thermal control but not exceptional roster-wide sustained output.

### Senses
Large compound eyes, ocelli, antennae and chemosensory systems support flight control, nest recognition, prey detection and social communication. The species can forage in low light, although workers experimentally slow under low illumination. Sensory capability is sophisticated for an insect without being treated as vertebrate-level perception.

### Tactics, social behavior and fighting
European hornets are eusocial. A queen founds the colony, workers forage, maintain the nest and defend it, and colonies peak late in the season. Missouri Department of Conservation reports most nests at no more than about 400 workers, with some potentially reaching 1,000. Genetic work on 15 nests found colonies typically founded by a single queen mated to a single male and found no direct queen-worker aggression in the observed colonies.

For individual ABS scoring, colony size does **not** multiply one hornet's Attack or Raw Power. Social coordination is context for Tactics and ecology only.

### Predation and diet
Workers capture other insects and process protein for larvae. Adults also consume carbohydrate-rich resources such as sap, fruit and nectar. Mandibles subdue/process prey while the sting can be used in defense and conflict. Venom research supports greater toxicity against insect targets than vertebrate targets, making the hornet especially dangerous in arthropod matchups.

### Environmental strengths
- Three-dimensional flight and hovering in open or cluttered airspace.
- Small target profile.
- Effective venom against arthropod targets.
- Can operate in relatively low light compared with many day-active insects.
- Social nest defense can be formidable ecologically, though ABS individual ratings remain individual.

### Weaknesses
- Sub-gram body mass gives negligible absolute force against medium and large vertebrates.
- Thin exoskeleton cannot withstand a clean vertebrate strike or bite.
- Cold conditions constrain insect performance despite thermoregulatory ability.
- Venom effect is strongly dose- and target-dependent.
- Queen is larger but is not the routine hunting caste, so some behavioral evidence comes from workers.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favorable:** similarly sized arthropods vulnerable to a piercing sting and venom, especially targets the hornet can outmaneuver in flight.

**Mixed:** larger insects with heavy armor, long weapons or specialized anti-wasp defenses.

**Unfavorable:** nearly all medium and large vertebrates in a direct individual fight. The sting may cause pain or a medically significant reaction, but the hornet lacks the dose and physical force to reliably disable a healthy large opponent before being crushed.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Repeat-Sting Injector** — a female can use the approximately 5.4 mm sting repeatedly to inject venom, allowing hit-disengage-hit combat rather than a one-use attack.
2. **Venomous Airstrike** — powered flight combines with a venom-delivery weapon, letting the hornet attack from three dimensions and disengage from many terrestrial arthropods.

### Unique traits
1. **Twilight Flight Control** — European hornets remain active in low light; experiments show workers adjust flight speed as illumination falls rather than simply losing flight capability.
2. **Seasonal Fortress Society** — an annual eusocial colony divides queen, worker and male roles and can concentrate hundreds of workers around a defended cavity, while individual ABS power remains unmultiplied.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
*Vespa crabro* is native across much of Europe and Asia and was introduced to eastern North America in the nineteenth century. Penn State records it as established across much of eastern North America. Nests are commonly built in protected cavities such as hollow trees and wall voids.

### Diet and ecology
Adults take sugary resources including sap, nectar and fruit. Workers hunt insects to provision developing larvae, making the species both a pollinator/flower visitor in some contexts and a predator of other arthropods.

### Social structure and life history
A mated queen overwinters, emerges in spring, founds a nest and raises the first workers. Workers then assume foraging, nest construction and brood care. Colonies peak in late summer, produce males and new queens, and collapse with cold weather; newly mated queens survive to repeat the cycle.

### Conservation and human interaction
European hornets are ecologically useful insect predators but can conflict with people when nesting in structures. Stings are medically important because venom can cause intense local effects and allergic reactions, but the species should not be represented as automatically lethal to humans. Introduced North American populations are well established.

### Adaptations
- Large vespid flight musculature and thoracic thermoregulation.
- Female venom apparatus with a long sting.
- Strong mandibles for prey processing and nest construction.
- Compound eyes, ocelli and antennae for flight and chemical sensing.
- Eusocial division of labor and cavity-nest defense.

### Genuine fun facts
- European hornet queens can reach about **35 mm**, substantially larger than workers.
- A comparative study measured the sting at about **5.39 mm**.
- Worker body mass in thermoregulation research averaged about **477.5 mg**.
- Low light causes workers to reduce flight speed, showing active adjustment of flight behavior.
- In a 15-nest genetic study, males were queen-produced rather than worker-produced.
- Hornet venom is demonstrably toxic to other hornets, so they are not immune to their own venom.

### Concise site-ready summary
The European hornet is a large eusocial wasp whose individual combat value comes from agile flight and a reusable venomous sting, not brute force. A mature queen is only around a gram, keeping absolute Attack and Defense very low, but three-dimensional maneuvering and a roughly 5.4 mm venom-delivery sting make it a dangerous specialist against other arthropods.

### Rich narrative profile
The hornet is a strong test of ABS's absolute scaling. At insect scale, *Vespa crabro* is a heavyweight aerial predator with a substantial sting, strong mandibles and sophisticated flight control. Across a roster containing wolves, bears and megafauna, however, the same animal has almost negligible raw force and durability. The correct profile therefore preserves a huge gap in physical stats while rewarding the mechanisms that genuinely change matchups.

Its most important weapon is not simply a sharp point. The female sting delivers a complex venom that has experimentally measurable toxicity to other hornets and other insects. Because the sting can be used repeatedly, flight permits a pattern of approach, injection and disengagement. That combination deserves a high Special score even though Attack stays low in absolute terms.

The species is also behaviorally more nuanced than a generic 'angry hornet' stereotype. European hornets forage, process prey, navigate back to cavity nests and operate in a seasonal division-of-labor society. Colony defense can involve many workers, but the individual battle model must not turn hundreds of nestmates into one individual's power. This separation between individual mechanics and colony ecology prevents sociality from becoming hidden stat inflation.

### Confidence and conflicts
- **Taxon:** high confidence in *V. crabro* as the chosen species-level resolution, but production's generic *Vespa* label is taxonomically broader. This should be an explicit product decision during migration.
- **Queen mass:** moderate-low. Published worker mass is strong; the 0.495–0.900 g species compilation supports scale but is not a queen-only distribution. Canonical 0.8 g should be revisited if direct queen mass series is found.
- **Length:** high-moderate; institutional sources agree queens can reach about 35 mm.
- **Speed:** moderate; 1.86 m/s is experimentally linked but not a maximum free-flight value. Do not restore the site's unsupported 36 km/h placeholder.
- **Venom:** high confidence that venom is potent against arthropods; toxicity must not be confused with delivered dose or universal vertebrate lethality.
- **Bite force:** unresolved. Keep 0.0 PSI.
