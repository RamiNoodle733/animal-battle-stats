# Poison Dart Frog / Golden Poison Frog (*Phyllobates terribilis*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Roster label:** Poison Dart Frog
- **Resolved exact taxon:** Golden poison frog, *Phyllobates terribilis* (Myers, Daly & Malkin, 1978), family Dendrobatidae.
- **Why this resolution:** the legacy schema gives only family-level `Dendrobatidae`, which is not an exact animal. For a single battle profile, *P. terribilis* is the strongest defensible species resolution because it is a true dart-poison species, is the best-documented extreme toxin specialist in the family, and matches the legacy profile's defining "deadly skin toxin" concept. This is an explicit research resolution, not an assumption that all dendrobatids share its toxicity.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime **wild adult female**, **0.006 kg**, **5.2 cm snout-vent length**. Females are the larger sex, so female is used for the representative fighter even though toxin is the dominant defense.
- **Dimorphism/variation:** ADW reports an average adult length around 47 mm and females to 50-55 mm. Zoo Liberec reports 4-5.5 cm and 4-6 g. Adult colors vary geographically from golden/yellow through orange to pale or mint green. Juveniles differ strongly, retaining dark bodies with dorsolateral stripes before attaining the uniform adult warning coloration.

## 2. Physical measurements and ranges

### Canonical measurements
- `weight_kg`: **0.006**. Upper end of Zoo Liberec's 4-6 g adult range, appropriate for the larger canonical female without using an implausible internet outlier.
- `height_cm`: **2.0**. Approximate resting body height for schema utility only; no authoritative standardized standing-height series was found, so confidence is low and this should not be treated as a measured anatomical maximum.
- `length_cm`: **5.2** snout-vent length. Representative of a large healthy adult female inside the documented 4-5.5 cm range.
- `speed_mps`: **0.0**. No defensible species-specific maximum running or jumping speed was recovered.
- `lifespan_years`: **10.0**. National Geographic reports an average wild lifespan of about 10 years; Mill Mountain Zoo gives 6-10 years in the wild and up to 20 in captivity.
- `bite_force_psi`: **0.0**. No defensible species-specific bite-pressure measurement exists, and bite is not the animal's important combat mechanism.

### Natural weapons and chemical defense
The frog has no meaningful macroscopic weapon comparable with fangs, claws, horns or a stinger. Its defining system is **passive poisonous skin**, not venom injection. The 1978 species description reports up to about **1,900 micrograms of batrachotoxin-class toxins** in a freshly caught frog. A 1980 *Science* study confirmed exceptionally high batrachotoxin levels in wild *P. terribilis*, declining in captive animals, while captive-bred F1 frogs reared to maturity had no detectable batrachotoxin.

Batrachotoxin acts on voltage-gated sodium channels, disrupting normal nerve and muscle electrical signaling. This can lead to paralysis and lethal cardiac dysfunction in susceptible animals. The frog itself has evolved resistance to its own toxin system. The bright uniform adult coloration is aposematic warning coloration that reduces the likelihood of predators completing an attack.

The toxin is diet-dependent rather than safely assumed to be synthesized de novo. Comparative poison-frog research supports dietary sequestration of defensive alkaloids from ants and other small arthropods, and *Choresine* melyrid beetles contain batrachotoxins and are a plausible source lineage. The exact Colombian dietary source for wild *P. terribilis* remains incompletely resolved.

## 4. Combat biology

### Offensive mechanics
This animal is a poor active attacker. It cannot inject batrachotoxin, spit it, project it, or deliberately coat an opponent at range. Its tiny jaws and limbs have negligible absolute damage output. Toxicity becomes combat-relevant mainly when a predator mouths, bites, swallows, or otherwise contacts the skin secretion in a biologically effective way. Therefore the toxin drives **Special** and deterrent defense far more than **Attack**.

### Defensive adaptations and durability
The primary defense is a two-stage system: conspicuous aposematic coloration discourages experienced predators from attacking, and the skin toxin punishes contact or ingestion if warning fails. This is extraordinary chemical defense but not armor. At only about 6 g, the frog has almost no structural capacity to absorb crushing, tearing, trampling, or a clean strike from a much larger animal. Chemical danger must not be mistaken for physical toughness.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
*P. terribilis* is diurnal, terrestrial and saltatorial. ADW describes it as strictly terrestrial, generally on the forest floor or only centimeters above it. Frog hindlimbs permit quick hops and short evasive repositioning, but no species-specific top speed or jump-performance dataset was found. Agility is therefore scored from the locomotor body plan and escape behavior, not from a fabricated maximum.

### Endurance
This is an ectothermic forest-floor frog, not a sustained pursuit animal. Daily activity, foraging and repeated short movements demonstrate ordinary amphibian endurance, but there is no evidence for exceptional continuous-output capacity. High toxin potency does not raise Stamina.

### Senses and intelligence
The species uses vision to detect moving prey, acoustic communication in courtship, and tactile/visual signaling. ADW reports rapid association of terrarium opening with feeding in captivity, supporting basic learning. This is useful but not evidence for high-order tactical cognition.

### Tactics, fighting and predation
Prey capture is a fast tongue-assisted strike on ants, termites, beetles and other small invertebrates. Same-sex aggression can include calling, chasing and wrestling, but *P. terribilis* is less aggressively territorial than several related dendrobatids. Breeding males transport developing young/tadpoles to water. None of these behaviors justify predator-style Attack scores.

### Environmental strengths
- Humid tropical forest floor where normal hydration and locomotion are maintained.
- Close-contact matchups in which an attacker uses its mouth or exposed tissues against the frog.
- Predators capable of learning or recognizing aposematic warning coloration.
- Small crevices and leaf litter that favor a tiny, maneuverable terrestrial amphibian.

### Environmental weaknesses
- Dry or hot environments that rapidly threaten amphibian hydration and performance.
- Any opponent capable of killing at range or with one fast crushing strike before toxin exposure matters.
- Thick external armor or attack modes that avoid oral/mucosal contact.
- Captive-bred or long-term captive specimens can lose the wild toxin load, which is why the canonical fighter is explicitly wild.
- Tiny body mass means almost no absolute grappling, striking or trauma tolerance.

### Matchup archetypes
**Best against:** toxin-susceptible predators that habitually seize or swallow small prey and cannot recognize/avoid the warning signal.

**Worst against:** large stompers, long-reach strikers, ranged or indirect attackers, resistant predators, and any matchup where the frog must actively incapacitate an opponent without being contacted first.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings

- **Attack: 6.0** - virtually no active damage-delivery system; poisonous skin is dangerous mainly when the opponent initiates contact.
- **Defense: 43.0** - aposematism plus extraordinary toxin can prevent or punish predation, but the frog has almost no structural durability if struck cleanly.
- **Agility: 76.0** - small saltatorial body and short-range evasiveness are strong, without pretending unknown top speed equals agility.
- **Stamina: 44.0** - adequate for diurnal forest-floor foraging, not an endurance specialist.
- **Intelligence: 36.0** - basic associative learning, prey tracking and communication, but little evidence for flexible combat problem solving.
- **Special: 99.0** - batrachotoxin sequestration, self-resistance and aposematic signaling form an extreme matchup-changing chemical-defense system.

## 7. Abilities and traits

### Special abilities
1. **Batrachotoxin Mantle** - a wild adult can carry an exceptionally potent suite of batrachotoxin-class alkaloids in its skin; contact or ingestion by a susceptible attacker can disrupt sodium-channel function with severe or lethal consequences.
2. **Aposematic Lockout** - uniform brilliant adult coloration advertises the chemical defense before contact, allowing learned predators to abort attacks rather than forcing the frog to survive a bite first.

### Unique traits
1. **Diet-Built Arsenal** - the wild chemical defense depends on sequestered dietary alkaloids; captive-bred frogs reared without the relevant natural prey can mature without detectable batrachotoxin.
2. **Toxin-Resistant Carrier** - the frog's own nerve and muscle systems show resistance to batrachotoxin, allowing it to carry a chemical load that is dangerous to many potential attackers.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The golden poison frog is endemic to a very restricted humid lowland rainforest region on Colombia's Pacific side. Zoo Liberec places the species mainly around 100-200 m elevation and in primary rainforest; ADW likewise describes a narrow Pacific Colombian range associated with extremely wet lowland forest.

### Diet and ecology
Adults are diurnal insectivores feeding on ants, termites, beetles and other small invertebrates captured with a rapid adhesive-tongue strike. Dietary specialization is directly tied to chemical defense because poison frogs sequester defensive alkaloids from arthropod prey.

### Social structure and life history
The species is generally terrestrial and not densely aggregated in the wild. Captive observations show relatively low aggression compared with some related poison frogs, although same-sex chasing and wrestling occur. Clutches are small, roughly 8-20 eggs depending on source. Males participate in transporting developing young/tadpoles to water. Sexual maturity is reported by ADW at roughly 12-18 months.

### Conservation
The species is **Endangered** in current conservation references and has a decreasing population trend. Its tiny range makes habitat loss particularly consequential. Reported pressures include deforestation, agriculture, logging, mining/pollution and illegal-crop-related habitat disturbance. Conservation status should be periodically rechecked against the current IUCN assessment before production migration.

### Adaptations
- Extreme diet-derived alkaloid sequestration.
- Physiological resistance to batrachotoxin.
- Bright aposematic adult coloration.
- Terrestrial saltatorial locomotion.
- Adhesive tongue prey capture.
- Male parental transport of developing young.

### Human interaction
The Emberá and related Indigenous communities of western Colombia historically used *Phyllobates* skin toxins on blowgun darts; the 1978 species description specifically documents *P. terribilis* in this context. Wild frogs can be hazardous to handle. Captive-bred frogs on conventional diets lack the wild chemical arsenal and should not be used as the combat baseline.

### Fun facts
- *P. terribilis* was scientifically described only in 1978.
- It is among the largest dendrobatid frogs despite being only about five centimeters long.
- The original species work reported as much as roughly 1,900 micrograms of batrachotoxin-class toxins in one freshly caught frog.
- Captive-bred F1 frogs reared to maturity had no detectable batrachotoxin in the classic 1980 study.
- Juveniles are dark with stripes, then change to the much more uniform bright adult warning coloration.
- The frog is poisonous, not venomous: it has no specialized injection apparatus.
- Its spectacular chemical defense depends on ecology and diet, demonstrating why wild-versus-captive context matters in battle statistics.

### Concise site summary
A tiny Colombian frog with almost no active physical offense but one of the roster's most extreme defensive specializations. A wild golden poison frog combines brilliant warning coloration, diet-sequestered batrachotoxin and toxin resistance, making it disastrous for many predators to bite or swallow despite its extreme physical fragility.

### Rich narrative profile
The golden poison frog is an important calibration case because a single spectacular mechanism can easily distort every other score. At roughly six grams it has essentially no absolute raw power, no armor and no meaningful ability to overpower even modest vertebrate opponents. Its jaws are not a serious weapon and it cannot inject or project its toxin. Attack therefore stays near the roster floor.

Its chemical defense is a different matter. Wild *P. terribilis* can carry extraordinary quantities of batrachotoxin-class alkaloids in the skin. The bright adult coloration advertises that danger before contact, while physiological resistance lets the frog safely carry compounds that interfere with sodium-channel function in susceptible attackers. This combination deserves near-ceiling Special even though the same animal remains mechanically fragile.

The distinction between a wild and captive specimen is crucial. Classic experimental work found toxin levels declining in captivity and no detectable batrachotoxin in captive-reared F1 adults. ABS therefore specifies a healthy wild adult female as the canonical specimen. This preserves the real biology instead of granting every captive golden poison frog an arsenal it may not possess.

### Confidence and source conflicts
- **Mass:** Zoo Liberec's 4-6 g institutional range is preferred. A few hobby/commercial pages claim nearly 30 g, an extreme conflict unsupported by stronger institutional material and therefore rejected.
- **Length:** strong convergence around 4-5.5 cm; the 5.2 cm canonical female is well inside supported ranges.
- **Lifespan:** sources vary from an older ADW captive statement of about 5 years to 6-10 years wild and 10-20 years captive in zoo/husbandry sources. Canonical 10.0 years follows the stronger modern wild-life framing but remains medium confidence.
- **Toxin quantity:** ~1,900 µg is a reported maximum from freshly caught frogs, not a guaranteed load in every adult. It is not used as a linear damage score.
- **Dietary toxin source:** dietary sequestration is well supported; the exact natural arthropod source of batrachotoxin in Colombia remains incompletely demonstrated.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- At **0.006 kg**, this frog must sit vastly below Piranha (1.2 kg), Peregrine Falcon (1.0 kg), Platypus (1.7 kg) and even small mammals in Raw Power and active Attack. Proportional impressiveness does not change that.
- **Attack 6.0** is intentionally far below Piranha 37.0 and Platypus 31.0 because those animals can actively deliver damaging bites or venomous spur strikes. *P. terribilis* cannot actively inject its toxin.
- **Defense 43.0** exceeds many similarly tiny soft-bodied animals only because the toxin plus warning coloration can genuinely prevent predation. It does not imply mechanical toughness comparable to a much larger animal.
- **Special 99.0** can legitimately exceed Platypus 94.0 and Peregrine Falcon 93.0 because Special measures unusual matchup-changing biology rather than body power. Near-ceiling placement is reserved specifically for the wild batrachotoxin system and is not copied into Attack, Defense, Toughness or Stamina.
- **Agility 76.0** reflects short-range frog maneuverability, not an invented 4 m/s speed. It remains below Peregrine 96.0, Otter 91.0 and other elite maneuverability controls.
- No toxin effect is double-counted as Raw Power, Weaponry and Attack. The chemical system is concentrated in Abilities/Special with a limited, explicitly deterrence-based contribution to Defense.
