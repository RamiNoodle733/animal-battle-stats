# Swan (*Cygnus olor*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Swan, represented by the mute swan
- **Scientific name:** *Cygnus olor* (Gmelin, 1789)
- **Exact animal represented:** the mute swan, the large white Eurasian swan with an orange bill and black basal knob shown in the site photo. Other swans (whooper, trumpeter, black and so on) are not averaged in.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male (cob), **11.0 kg**, about **150 cm** long with a wingspan of about **220 cm**.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger and have a bigger bill knob, and the cob leads most territorial defense. British Trust for Ornithology (BTO) ringing data give adult males **10.75 ± 1.56 kg** (N = 314) against females at 8.54 ± 1.23 kg.
- **Population variation:** British studies summarised by Wikipedia put average male weights at 10.6–11.87 kg, with a normal range of 9.2–14.3 kg. One Polish cob of almost 23 kg is the heaviest verified flying bird, but it is an outlier and may not have been able to fly; it is not used as the baseline. Introduced North American populations are the same species.

- ADW: oldest banded bird 19 years.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 10.0**.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Bill, wings and weapons
- The bill is 6.9–9 cm long, used to bite, grip and push. The main striking weapon is the wing: Wikipedia states that swans strike with bony spurs at the wing joints while biting with the bill. The popular claim that a swan can break an adult man's leg is described as a myth.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite measurement exists, and the bill is not a crushing jaw.

### Defensive structure
- Dense contour feathers over thick down; no armor. Legs and the long neck are exposed.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
A mute swan attacks with its wings and bill. Defense usually starts with a loud hiss and the "busking" threat pose, neck curved back and wings half raised. If that fails, the swan charges, strikes with the bony wrist area of its wings and bites. Against other waterbirds it has a specific killing technique: it climbs onto the intruder's back and pecks at the head until it forces the bird underwater and drowns it (Wikipedia). Swans can kill red foxes and have overwhelmed and drowned dogs attacking them or their cygnets. Absolute damage is still limited by an 11 kg body with no claws, talons or teeth.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Three modes: strong swimming, heavy but fast flight, and slow walking. As one of the heaviest flying birds, it needs a long running take-off across the water at about 48 km/h. In the air it cruises fast but turns widely. On land it is clumsy.

### Endurance
Many British mute swans are sedentary, while continental and Asian populations migrate. Flight at this body mass is costly, so the swan is less of an endurance flyer than geese or pelicans, but it swims and feeds for long periods.

### Senses
Good vision for vigilance on open water; the throbbing wing noise in flight can be heard 1–2 km away and serves as a contact signal between birds. No specialist sense.

### Intelligence and tactics
Mute swans hold territories, form long pair bonds and give their young more than four months of care from laying to fledging, one of the longest parental investments among birds (Włodarczyk & Minias 2016). Territory choice is not always sound: a 19-year Polish study found long-occupied territories did not give better breeding success. Aggression is graded from display to attack, and the drowning tactic shows targeted fighting behaviour.

### Fighting and aggression
Among the most territorial waterbirds. Cobs drive off geese, ducks and other swans, attack canoes and small boats near their young and confront dogs and people approaching nests (Wikipedia; ADW). In central Poland, mute swans nesting near expanding whooper swans moved nests deeper into reeds and raised fewer young, a sign of direct confrontations with a larger swan species (Szewczuk et al. 2026).

### Predator defense
Adults have few natural enemies. Foxes, pike, mink and, in New York, snapping turtles take cygnets; adults defend them by displaying, charging and striking. Cygnets shelter under or ride on a parent's back.

### Environmental strengths and limitations
- Open fresh water is ideal: it can charge, strike, drown intruders and retreat to deep water.
- On land its short legs make it slow and awkward.
- Take-off needs a long runway of open water.

### Weaknesses
- Low absolute mass and power on the full roster.
- No claws, talons or teeth; bill and wing blows only.
- Long, exposed neck and legs.
- Poor land mobility and slow take-off.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** ducks, geese, gulls, small dogs and foxes, especially in or near water.
- **Mixed:** large dogs and medium predators on open water, where the swan can use drowning and retreat.
- **Bad:** large carnivores, crocodilians, big raptors and any heavyweight on land.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Drowning Hold** — climbs onto an intruding bird's back and pecks at its head to force it underwater until it drowns; the same approach has overwhelmed attacking dogs in water.
2. **Wing Bludgeon** — hisses, raises its wings in the busking threat pose and then charges, striking with the bony wrist joints of its wings while biting with its bill.

### Unique traits
1. **Heavyweight Flyer** — one of the heaviest flying birds, with cobs averaging about 11 kg; it needs a long running take-off across the water but then reaches about 88 km/h in flight.
2. **Singing Wings** — despite the "mute" name, its wingbeats make a loud throbbing sound audible 1–2 km away, used as a contact signal in flight.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Lakes, lagoons and slow-flowing rivers, preferring medium to large bodies of fresh water. Native across much of Europe and temperate Asia; introduced populations are established in North America, where numbers in the lower Great Lakes grew at over 10% a year between 1971 and 2000, doubling every seven to eight years.

### Diet and ecology
Mainly aquatic plants, reached by plunging the head and neck underwater or up-ending, since mute swans do not dive; small invertebrates are taken too. Grazing can deplete river vegetation, and managers have modelled "sacrificial" feeding areas to protect valuable plant beds (Wood et al. 2014). Ducks and gulls often feed alongside swans on weed the swans bring to the surface.

### Social structure
Breeding pairs hold territories, often a single pair on a small lake, of about 4–10 acres (ADW). Non-breeding birds up to three or four years old gather in flocks that can number several hundred at traditional sites. In a few rich areas pairs nest colonially.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding usually begins at about four years of age (BTO). The large reed nest holds eggs of about 294–396 g each, and cygnets hatch at 180–248 g. Care lasts more than four months from laying to fledging; cygnets keep grey plumage until at least their first year and are driven away before the next breeding season.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. UK numbers fell from about 1960 to the early 1980s through lead poisoning from swallowed fishing weights and shot, then recovered quickly after lead was replaced. The UK wintering population is internationally important, and the species is on the UK amber list (BTO). Whooper swans spreading into central Europe now compete with mute swans for nest sites.

### Special features
- Orange bill with a black basal knob, larger in males.
- Bony wing joints used as striking points.
- Very long neck for reaching submerged plants.
- Loud, throbbing wingbeat noise in flight.
- Among the heaviest flying birds.

### Human interaction
Mute swans are familiar park birds. A UK community-science study found that 22% of systematic observations recorded people feeding swans (Maréchal et al. 2025). Cobs can attack people, boats and dogs that come near nests. Introduced North American populations have grown rapidly, doubling every seven to eight years in the lower Great Lakes between 1971 and 2000.

### Genuine fun facts
- One Polish cob weighed almost **23 kg**, the heaviest verified weight for a flying bird.
- The oldest ringed mute swan in Britain lived over **29 years**.
- Mute swans are not silent: their wingbeats throb loudly enough to be heard 1–2 km away.
- They run across the water at about **48 km/h** to get airborne.
- Swans can kill intruding waterbirds by climbing on them and holding them underwater.
- The story that a swan's wing can break a man's leg is a myth, although the blows are powerful.
- Cygnets often ride on a parent's back between its wings.

### Concise site-ready summary
The mute swan is an 11-kilogram waterbird with a big temper. It defends its nest by hissing, raising its wings, then charging with wing blows and bites, and it can drown intruding birds and even attacking dogs in the water. On the full roster its small body and lack of claws or teeth keep its damage low, and it is clumsy on land.

### Rich narrative profile
The mute swan is one of the heaviest birds that can still fly. A typical cob weighs about 11 kg with a wingspan of more than two metres, and it needs a long, splashing run across the water to get airborne before cruising at up to about 88 km/h. That size makes it the heavyweight of most lakes and rivers it lives on, and it behaves accordingly.

Swans are famous for defending their nests and cygnets. A threatened cob hisses, arches its neck and raises its wings in the busking display. If that does not work it charges, beating with the bony joints of its wings and biting with its bill. Against other waterbirds it has a deadly move: it climbs onto the intruder's back and holds its head underwater. Swans have killed foxes and drowned dogs, and they will chase canoes away from their young.

On the absolute battle scale, though, a swan is still an 11 kg bird. It has no talons, no teeth and no armor, its long neck and legs are exposed, and on land it is slow and awkward. Its best fights happen on open water against animals that cannot swim well. Against real predators of medium size or larger, its courage outruns its weapons.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Speed:** the 88.5 km/h figure is an institutional maximum without a described method.
- **Mass:** a 23 kg record exists but may not represent a bird able to fly; the 11 kg canonical value follows ringing data.
- **Wing weapon:** Wikipedia describes "bony spurs" in the wings. No source consulted here measured or described a spur separately, so the report treats the striking surface simply as the bony wrist area of the wing.
- **Dog drownings and fox kills:** reported in Wikipedia from news and field accounts, not systematic studies; used qualitatively.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Goose (3.5 kg; A25 D20 Ag76 St82 I67 Sp59):** the swan is about three times heavier with longer reach and a drowning technique, so Attack (30.0) and Defense (24.0) sit above the goose. The goose keeps higher Agility and Stamina because it is a lighter, more agile and longer-distance flyer.
- **Versus Pelican (6.9 kg; A28 D22 Ag72 St84 Sp73):** similar absolute damage level; the swan is heavier and far more aggressive, while the pelican's flight endurance and pouch feeding keep its Stamina and Special higher.
- **Versus Emperor Penguin (38 kg; A28 D32 St95 Sp88):** the penguin is heavier and better protected, but far less aggressive; the swan gets slightly higher Attack from its willingness to strike and drown opponents.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholders (Agility 19.9, Stamina 86.7) are not used. Flight gives the swan solid Agility, and its heavy flight does not justify near-elite Stamina. Its fame for aggression is scored under Ferocity rather than inflating Attack.
