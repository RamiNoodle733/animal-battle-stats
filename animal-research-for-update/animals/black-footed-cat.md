# Black-footed Cat (*Felis nigripes*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Black-footed cat (also small-spotted cat)
- **Scientific name:** *Felis nigripes* Burchell, 1824
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild black-footed cat of the arid grasslands and Karoo scrub of South Africa, Namibia and Botswana, treated as one species with no recognized subspecies (the IUCN Cat Specialist Group considers it most probably monotypic).
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **1.9 kg**, matching the Animal Diversity Web male average of 1.93 kg and sitting inside Wikipedia's 1.6–2.45 kg male range.
- **Sex choice:** males are clearly larger. Wikipedia gives males 1.6–2.45 kg and 42.5–50 cm head-body length against females at 1.1–1.65 kg and 33.7–36.8 cm. The Black-footed Cat Working Group's 2010 field report measured four collared females at 1.19–1.35 kg.
- **Population notes:** background colour varies from pale sandy ochre to dark ochre. Densities are naturally low (about 0.17 per km² at Benfontein in 1998–1999, falling to about 0.08 per km² in 2005–2014), and the species is secretive and hard to trap, so most detailed biology comes from a small number of long-term radio-tracking studies in the Northern Cape.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Wikipedia: males **1.6–2.45 kg**, females **1.1–1.65 kg**.
- ADW: species range **1.0–2.75 kg**; male average **1.93 kg**, female average **1.3 kg**.
- PNAS genome paper (2024): "one of the smallest (less than 1.93 kg)" felids.
- **Canonical:** **1.9 kg** adult male.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Height
- Wikipedia: shoulder height **less than 25 cm**.
- BFCWG 2010 field report: four adult females measured **22–23 cm** at the shoulder.
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 23.0 cm**, taken from measured adults; a male is likely slightly taller but stays under the 25 cm ceiling.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Length
- Wikipedia: males **42.5–50 cm** head-body with a **15–20 cm** tail; females 33.7–36.8 cm with a 15.7–17 cm tail. The tail is about half the head-body length.
- BFCWG 2010: female total lengths **53.5–56 cm** including 14.5–16 cm tails.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 45.0 cm** head-body for an adult male, tail recorded separately.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Speed
- Wikipedia describes a "fast hunt" at only **2–3 km/h** (a quick searching trot), a "slow hunt" at 0.5–0.8 km/h, and short explosive chases and leaps at birds. No measured sprint speed was found.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement). The trotting figures describe searching pace, not maximum speed, and are not converted into a sprint value.

### Lifespan
- ADW: up to **13 years** in the wild and **15.6 years** in captivity; listed average **12.0 years** (context appears to be captive records).
- Wikipedia: captive cats up to **15 years 3 months**; AA amyloidosis (kidney failure) is highly prevalent in both captive and free-ranging animals.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 12.0**, the ADW average, flagged as captive-influenced. Typical wild survival is not well measured.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Bite and claws
- Kills shrews and rodents with a bite to the neck or head (Wikipedia, citing Sliwa's field studies). It grounds prey from behind with its forepaws and dewclaws.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No species-specific bite-force measurement was found. No PSI is invented.
- **Weapon dimensions:** skull basal length 77–87 mm and width 38–40 mm (Wikipedia). No canine-length measurement located.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Short fur, small mass and a tiny skull. Defense is almost entirely avoidance: it rests in hollow termite mounds and old springhare, aardvark and porcupine burrows and "seeks refuge at the slightest disturbance".

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The black-footed cat is a miniature but extremely efficient killer. It stalks, rushes and pins prey from behind with its forepaws and dewclaws, then kills with a bite to the neck or head. Its prey averages only about 24 g, but it also takes Cape hares heavier than itself and was once seen pouncing on a resting lamb before giving up when the lamb stood. The PNAS genome paper cites a hunting success of about **60%**, and a 622-hour field watch recorded a kill roughly every 50 minutes and up to 14 small animals in one night. All of this is lethal to mice, gerbils, shrews and larks; at 1.9 kg it is almost meaningless against a medium-sized opponent in absolute terms.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It moves in small circles and zigzags between bushes and termite mounds, uses a slow cautious stalk, a quick trotting search and motionless waiting at rodent burrows. Against birds it makes a quick chase and a leap of up to **1.4 m high and 2 m long**, catching some birds in the air. This is excellent small-cat reflex and jumping, but its stocky build and short legs are suited to ground hunting rather than climbing.

### Endurance
Adults move an average of **8.42 ± 2.09 km per night** in search of prey (Wikipedia, citing Sliwa's radio-tracking), hunting through the whole night at temperatures from −10 to 35 °C. Its energy demand is very high: about 250–300 g of prey per night, roughly a sixth of its body weight (the PNAS paper gives about 20%). This supports strong sustained foraging stamina for a small cat, not burst-only effort.

### Senses
Nocturnal felid vision and hearing. The PNAS genome study found rapid change in sensory-related genes consistent with its night hunting. No measured sensory thresholds were located, so the Senses score rests on documented nocturnal hunting performance.

### Intelligence and tactics
It switches between three hunting modes ("fast hunt", "slow hunt" and "sit and wait") depending on prey and cover, and captive females move kittens to new hiding places every six to ten days, more often than other small cats. This is flexible, efficient solitary behavior, not cooperative tactics.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary outside mating and kitten rearing. Female home ranges are about 6.2–15.5 km² a year and resident males 19.4–23.6 km².

### Fighting and aggression
When threatened it hisses and growls. It has a strong reputation for fierceness in self-defense (a San legend claims it can kill a giraffe by piercing its jugular, which is folklore, not fact). Its first choice is still to vanish into a burrow.

### Environmental strengths
- Open arid grassland and Karoo scrub with termite mounds and burrows for instant refuge.
- Can meet its water needs from prey.
- Hunts in cold desert nights and hot conditions alike.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny absolute mass and power.
- No armor; one bite from a jackal or caracal can be fatal.
- Short stocky legs; not a climber.
- Very high energy demand; it must keep hunting.
- Kittens and subadults are taken by black-backed jackals, caracals and owls.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rodents, shrews, small birds, insects and other animals well under 1 kg.
- **Mixed:** animals of its own weight with some defenses, such as hares or small snakes.
- **Bad:** almost every medium or large predator, any armored animal, raptors and anything that can pin it.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Sixty-Percent Strike** — field observation shows roughly six successful catches from every ten attempts, a kill about every 50 minutes and up to 14 kills in a night. In a matchup against small prey this reliability is its real weapon; against larger opponents it adds nothing to raw damage.
2. **Termite-Mound Bolt** — it rests in hollow termite mounds and old burrows and dives into cover at the slightest disturbance, which makes it very hard for larger predators to corner in its own habitat.

### Unique traits
1. **Africa's Smallest Wild Cat** — adult males weigh only 1.6–2.45 kg and stand under 25 cm at the shoulder, making it the smallest wild cat on the African continent.
2. **Night-Long Hunter** — it eats about a sixth of its body weight in prey each night and hunts through the whole night in temperatures from −10 to 35 °C.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Endemic to southern Africa: arid and semi-arid short grassland, Karoo scrub and savanna with sparse bush cover in South Africa, Namibia and Botswana, marginally into neighbouring countries. It avoids dense woodland and very sandy dune desert. Daytime refuges are hollow termite mounds and old burrows of springhares, aardvarks and Cape porcupines, which it digs to enlarge.

### Diet and ecology
A specialist hunter of small vertebrates. Wikipedia lists 54 prey species, with small rodents such as the gerbil mouse among the most important, followed by mammals over 100 g and small birds; insects such as harvester termites make up only about 2% of prey mass. Average prey weighs about 24 g. It scavenges occasionally. It can meet its daily water needs from prey but drinks when water is available.

### Social structure
Solitary. Males range over larger areas that overlap several females. Scent marking by urine spraying is frequent. Communication includes loud calls for contact at a distance, quieter purrs and gurgles at close range, and hisses and growls when threatened.

### Reproduction and life history
Kittens weigh 60–93 g at birth and are born blind in springhare burrows or hollow termite mounds. Captive males mature at about nine months and females at about seven months. Mothers bring back live prey for kittens to practice on from about three weeks of age (ADW).

### Conservation
Listed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List (latest assessment 2026) and on CITES Appendix I. The Black-footed Cat Working Group estimates fewer than 10,000 mature individuals in the wild. Threats include bait poisoning and steel-jaw traps set for other predators, overgrazing, declining springhare numbers, predation by jackals and caracals, disease and road traffic. The PNAS genome study found high inbreeding that may raise the risk of AA amyloidosis, which causes about 70% of deaths in captive animals.

### Special features
- Tiny, compact body with short legs and a round head.
- Black soles and black bars on the legs; small dark spots on a sandy coat.
- Very high metabolic rate that drives near-continuous night hunting.
- Three hunting modes and bird-catching leaps of up to 1.4 m.
- Heavy reliance on termite mounds and burrows for shelter.

### Human interaction
It kills rodents that damage crops and stores, and is harmless to people. It suffers from indiscriminate poison and trap campaigns aimed at jackals and caracals. It is kept in a small number of zoos, where amyloidosis is a major health problem.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is Africa's smallest wild cat; a big male weighs under 2.5 kg.
- About 60% of its hunts succeed, one of the highest success rates recorded for any cat.
- It can kill up to 14 small animals in one night.
- It eats roughly a sixth of its own body weight in prey every night.
- It can leap about 1.4 m straight up and snatch birds out of the air.
- A San legend says it can kill a giraffe by biting its jugular; this is folklore, not fact.
- It spends the day inside hollow termite mounds or old springhare burrows, and captive mothers move their kittens to a new hiding place every six to ten days.

### Concise site-ready summary
Africa's smallest wild cat, the black-footed cat weighs under 2 kg yet catches prey on roughly six of every ten attempts and hunts all night long. It is a superb killer of mice and small birds, but its tiny body gives it almost no absolute power or protection against larger animals.

### Rich narrative profile
The black-footed cat is a study in efficiency. A male weighing about 1.9 kg leaves its termite-mound den at dusk and spends the whole night on the move, trotting through Karoo grassland, pausing to watch rodent burrows and switching between fast searching, slow stalking and motionless waiting. Field studies in South Africa recorded a kill about every 50 minutes and up to 14 kills in a night, and its hunting success of around 60% is often cited as the best of any cat.

That reputation for deadliness has to be read on the absolute scale. The prey it kills so reliably averages about 24 grams. Its weapons are the same as a house cat's (claws, dewclaws and a neck bite), carried on a body less than half the size. Against a jackal, a caracal or an eagle-owl it is prey, and its survival depends on staying close to burrows and termite mounds where it can vanish instantly.

In a matchup the black-footed cat is therefore dangerous only to animals far smaller than itself. Its strengths are stamina, reflexes and a hunting routine that rarely wastes effort; its weaknesses are size, power and protection. It belongs well below the serval and caracal in every physical category, a little below the house cat in Attack, and above the house cat in sustained Stamina.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Hunting success:** the 60% figure comes from Sliwa's direct observations (about six successes in ten attempts); it is repeated widely in popular sources and should not be treated as a species-wide constant.
- **Energy intake:** Wikipedia gives about a sixth of body weight per night; the PNAS paper gives about 20% per day. Both indicate an unusually high demand.
- **Lifespan:** ADW's 12-year average appears to be captive-based; wild survival is unknown.
- **Height:** only females were measured in the 2010 field report; the male value is estimated inside the published "under 25 cm" ceiling.
- **Speed and bite:** no measurements exist; both fields are 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus House Cat (5 kg; A24 D16 Ag88 St48):** the black-footed cat is less than half the mass, so it sits below in Attack (17.0) and Defense (9.0) and slightly below in Agility (85.0, a less arboreal build). Its night-long foraging puts it well above the house cat in Stamina (64.0 vs 48.0).
- **Versus Serval (14 kg; A41) and Caracal (17 kg; A44):** these are seven to nine times heavier and take prey up to small antelope; the black-footed cat stays more than 20 points lower in Attack and far lower in Defense.
- **Versus Fennec Fox (1.5 kg; A15 D10 St67 Sp79) and Ferret (1.5 kg; A17 D10):** similar body size. Real claws and a felid killing bite put it at or slightly above them in Attack; the fennec keeps higher Special for its hearing and heat tolerance.
- **Versus Mongoose and Meerkat (0.8 kg; A18–20):** the roster's mongoose and meerkat Attack values look generous for their mass; the black-footed cat is more than twice as heavy and is placed at 17.0 to avoid adding to pound-for-pound inflation.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "deadliest cat" is expressed in Ferocity (68.0) and Special (64.0), not in Attack.
