# Tuna (*Thunnus orientalis*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Tuna, represented by the Pacific bluefin tuna
- **Scientific name:** *Thunnus orientalis* (Temminck and Schlegel, 1844), family Scombridae
- **Exact animal represented:** the Pacific bluefin tuna, the species shown in the live site photo (taken at the Kasai Rinkai Park aquarium, Tokyo). Other tunas such as yellowfin, albacore, skipjack and the Atlantic and southern bluefins are not averaged in.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy large adult, **2.0 m fork length and about 150 kg**, sex unspecified because the sources used give no sex-specific size difference.
- **Size choice:** NOAA Fisheries gives about 1.5 m and 60 kg at maturity (around five years old) and a maximum reported size of 3 m and 450 kg. FishBase lists 200 cm fork length as the common length and 300 cm as the maximum. A 2.0 m adult is a common large fish, not a record. Its weight comes from FishBase's genus-level length-weight relationship (about 127–158 kg at 200–215 cm total length), rounded to 150 kg.
- **Population notes:** spawning occurs in the western Pacific (East China Sea and Sea of Japan). Some fish stay in the western Pacific for life; others cross to the eastern Pacific as juveniles and return years later to spawn. As much as 90% of the historic catch was juveniles.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The Pacific bluefin is a fast, powerful predator of small schooling animals: squids, sardines, anchovies, saury, mackerels, hake, other tunas, pelagic red crabs and krill. It catches prey by speed and swallows it whole; its small teeth grip rather than cut. It has no bill like a marlin, no cutting teeth like a shark and no defensive spines. In a matchup its main assets are momentum and endurance, not a weapon, so Attack stays low for a 150 kg animal.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Tuna use thunniform swimming: a stiff front body and a narrow tail stalk that drives a crescent tail. A rigid body might seem to limit turning, but a 2023 study of captive Pacific bluefin found turn rates of up to 302 degrees per second and a minimum turn radius of 0.38 body lengths during "ratchet turns", a series of small, quick tail strokes. Minimum turn radii ranged from 0.4 to 1.7 body lengths. Agility is therefore high, though below the sailfish and the mako.

### Endurance
This is the tuna's strongest trait. Pacific bluefin swim constantly and cross the whole North Pacific: fish carrying radioactive cesium from the 2011 Fukushima release were caught off California within months (Madigan et al. 2012). Archival tags on 242 juveniles over 15 years showed that they shifted their foraging migration about 900 km poleward during a marine heatwave to stay in water where their metabolic costs were lowest (Proceedings B 2021). Warm muscles support sustained high-power swimming across a wide temperature range.

### Thermal physiology
Bluefin keep swimming muscle, eyes and brain warm through counter-current heat exchange. Juvenile Pacific bluefin begin holding body temperature independent of the water at about 40 cm fork length, with heat retention improving sharply by about 52 cm (Frontiers in Physiology 2022). Warm eyes and brain support vision and reaction speed in cold water.

### Senses
Tuna are visual predators with large eyes kept warm by heat exchangers, supported by the lateral line and smell. No quantitative sensory thresholds were located for this species.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior centers on schooling, seasonal migration and flexible use of feeding grounds. Tuna track shifting prey and temperature, but no evidence of complex problem solving or coordinated hunting beyond schooling was located.

### Social behavior and fighting
Pacific bluefin travel in schools. There is no known ritualized fighting between individuals. Tuna are described as schooling alongside dolphins, possibly for protection from sharks.

### Predators
Sharks prey on tunas (Wikipedia, Tuna). Adults rely on size and speed to escape; juveniles are far more vulnerable.

### Environmental strengths
- Open ocean, from warm subtropical to cool temperate water, surface to hundreds of meters.
- Long-range pursuit and escape.
- Cold water, where warm muscles give an edge over cold-bodied fish.

### Weaknesses
- No meaningful weapon; small gripping teeth only.
- No armor; ordinary scaled skin.
- Must keep moving in open water; poor in shallow, cluttered or confined habitats.
- Heavy fishing pressure has made populations far smaller than their historic size.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** squid, schooling fish and other small open-water prey; any contest decided by long-distance swimming.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized fish without strong weapons.
- **Bad:** sharks, billfish, toothed whales and any predator with cutting teeth or a bill.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Warm-Blooded Engine** — counter-current heat exchangers keep swimming muscle, eyes and brain up to 20 °C warmer than cold water, sustaining power and vision where cold-bodied fish slow down.
2. **Ratchet Turn** — a rapid series of small tail strokes swings the stiff body around at up to 302 degrees per second in less than half a body length, letting a torpedo-shaped fish change direction sharply.

### Unique traits
1. **Ocean-Crossing Migrant** — young Pacific bluefin cross the entire North Pacific from Japan to California and return years later to spawn in the western Pacific.
2. **Living Torpedo** — a rigid, streamlined body, crescent tail, finlets and keels make the bluefin one of the most specialized fast-swimming body plans among fishes.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The North Pacific, from the coasts of East Asia to western North America, with occasional records in the South Pacific; a genetically confirmed Pacific bluefin has even been caught off Brazil. Usually in the upper 200 m of open water, down to 550 m.

### Diet and ecology
An open-water predator of schooling squids and fishes (anchovies, sardines, saury, mackerels, hake, other tunas) that also takes pelagic red crabs and krill. Diet studies in the southern California Current show its feeding niche shifting with the abundance of prey such as northern anchovy.

### Social structure
Schooling fish that migrate in groups; no territorial or ritualized fighting behavior is known.

### Reproduction and life history
Spawning takes place in the western Pacific, mainly the East China Sea and the Sea of Japan. Fish mature at about five years, around 1.5 m and 60 kg. The generation length is about 7–9 years. Many juveniles make a trans-Pacific crossing during their first years and feed in the California Current before returning west.

### Conservation
Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List. Wikipedia notes that this listing has not been updated since a 2024 stock assessment found the population had risen from about 2% of historic levels in 2010 to about 23% in 2020. NOAA Fisheries and several international commissions manage the fishery.

### Special features
- Regional endothermy through retia mirabilia.
- Thunniform swimming with a crescent tail, finlets and keels.
- Rigid, streamlined body with exceptional turning ability for its shape.
- Warm eyes and brain for cold-water vision.

### Human interaction
One of the most valuable fish in the world. About 80% of Pacific and Atlantic bluefin is eaten in Japan, and New Year auction prices can reach millions of dollars: a 278 kg Pacific bluefin sold for 333.6 million yen in 2019. Recreational anglers also target the species off California.

### Genuine fun facts
- Pacific bluefin carried traces of the 2011 Fukushima release across the entire North Pacific to California within months.
- A 278 kg Pacific bluefin sold for 333.6 million yen (about US$3.1 million) at a Tokyo auction in 2019.
- Bluefin can keep their muscles up to 20 °C warmer than the surrounding sea.
- Despite a stiff body, a bluefin can turn at up to 302 degrees per second.
- Juvenile bluefin start holding their own body temperature at about 40 cm long.
- During a marine heatwave, young bluefin moved their feeding migration about 900 km toward the pole.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Pacific bluefin tuna is a 2 m, 150 kg warm-bodied fish built for speed and endurance, crossing the North Pacific and turning sharply despite its torpedo shape. It hunts small schooling prey and has no real weapon, so it wins by outlasting and outrunning rather than by fighting.

### Rich narrative profile
The Pacific bluefin tuna is an engine in the shape of a fish. A common large adult is about 2 m long and 150 kg, most of it swimming muscle. Heat exchangers in its circulation hold that muscle, along with its eyes and brain, well above the temperature of the surrounding sea, so the bluefin can keep swimming hard in cold water where other fish slow down. That warm engine powers some of the longest migrations of any fish: young bluefin hatched off Japan cross the whole North Pacific to feed off California, as shown when fish carrying Fukushima-derived cesium turned up there within months.

The same body is surprisingly nimble. Its front half is stiff and streamlined, but the narrow tail stalk and crescent tail flex hard. Filming of captive bluefin showed turns of up to 302 degrees per second and turn radii under half a body length, achieved with rapid ratchet-like tail strokes. Combined with its size, this lets the tuna run down fast squid and schooling fish, which it swallows whole.

In a battle, though, the bluefin has little to hurt an opponent with. Its teeth are small and meant for gripping, it has no bill like a marlin or sailfish, and it carries no armor. Its best tactic is to use its speed and stamina to escape. On the absolute roster scale it scores near the top in Stamina and high in Agility and Special, but its Attack stays far below the billfish and sharks it shares the ocean with.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Weight:** 150 kg is derived from a genus-level length-weight model, not from weighed fish; 2 m fish may range from about 127 to 158 kg depending on how fork length converts to total length.
- **Maximum mass:** 450 kg (NOAA, FishBase) versus 550 kg reported elsewhere (Wikipedia).
- **Speed:** popular tuna speed figures refer to other species or lack a documented method; no Pacific bluefin maximum is used.
- **Lifespan:** 15 years average versus 26 years maximum (NOAA); the average is used.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Marlin (A58.0 D41.0 Ag78.0 St90.0 I62.0 Sp70.0):** the marlin is heavier (250 kg) and armed with a bill, so it keeps far higher Attack. Defense is close. The tuna's warm-muscle endurance and trans-Pacific migrations put its Stamina slightly higher, and regional endothermy gives it a higher Special.
- **Versus Sailfish (A52.0 D38.0 Ag89.0 St79.0 I53.0 Sp82.0):** the sailfish is lighter but has a bill and elite maneuvering, so it stays above the tuna in Attack and Agility. The heavier tuna gets slightly higher Defense and much higher Stamina; the sailfish's stealth bill slash and deployable sail keep its Special higher.
- **Versus Mako Shark (A75.0 D53.0 Ag85.0 St88.0 I64.0 Sp86.0):** makos are warm-bodied too and are documented tuna predators. The mako's cutting teeth and larger size keep it far above the tuna in Attack and Defense; the tuna's Stamina edges above the mako's.
- **Versus Oceanic Whitetip Shark (A71.0 D53.0 Ag70.0 St80.0 I60.0 Sp76.0):** similar mass, but the shark's serrated teeth more than double the tuna's Attack, while the tuna is more agile and has more stamina.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholder values (A21.2 D23.9 Ag81.9 St86.3 I21.7 Sp42.2) are not used as anchors. Speed and endurance are not converted into Attack; a fish with no weapon stays in the low Attack band even at 150 kg.
