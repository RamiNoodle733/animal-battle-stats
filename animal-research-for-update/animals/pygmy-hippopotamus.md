# Pygmy Hippopotamus (*Choeropsis liberiensis*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Pygmy hippopotamus (pygmy hippo; historically "water cow" in Liberia)
- **Scientific name:** *Choeropsis liberiensis* (Morton, 1849); some accounts, including Animal Diversity Web, still file it as *Hexaprotodon liberiensis*
- **Exact animal represented:** the West African forest pygmy hippo of Liberia, Sierra Leone, Guinea and Côte d'Ivoire, not the much larger common hippopotamus and not the island dwarf hippos known only from bones.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult of about **250 kg**, **90 cm** at the shoulder and **165 cm** head-body length. This sits in the upper half of the 160–275 kg range without using an overweight zoo animal as the baseline.
- **Sex choice:** sexual dimorphism in adult mass is slight and poorly documented; newborn males weigh only about 0.25 kg more than females. The specimen is treated as an adult male, but the figures describe either sex.
- **Population variation:** nearly all body data come from zoo animals, because wild pygmy hippos are nocturnal, solitary and very hard to observe. A zoo mortality review found many adult and geriatric captive animals overweight to obese, so heavy zoo weights should not set the wild baseline. A separate Niger Delta subspecies (*C. l. heslopi*) has had no reliable records since the 1940s.

### Bite and teeth
- ADW gives the dental formula as 2/1, 1/1, 3/3, 3/3 (34 teeth) and states that the **canines are ever-growing**.
- A 2023 *PLoS One* study of hippo dentition (live Basel Zoo animals plus museum skulls) found the tusks and incisors are not used for feeding but serve in defense and fighting in both hippo species, with a very wide gape.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-force measurement exists for this species, and common-hippo figures must not be scaled down to fill the gap.
- **Weapon dimensions:** no standardized canine length for adult pygmy hippos was located.

### Defensive structure
- Skin with a thin epidermis over a dermis several centimetres thick, like the common hippo's, covered in a pinkish skin secretion (the so-called "blood sweat") that helps protect the skin from sun. No armor. The skin dries and cracks quickly out of water.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The pygmy hippo's weapons are its mouth: a wide gape, ever-growing canines and forward incisors that hippos use for defense and fighting rather than feeding. Behind them sits a dense, barrel-shaped 250 kg body that can shove and bowl over a smaller animal. It is a scaled-down version of the common hippo's system at roughly one-seventh of the mass, with none of the common hippo's reputation for territorial violence. Its offense is defensive in character: a cornered or startled animal bites and pushes.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It spends more time on land than the common hippo and moves along game trails and tunnel-like paths through swamp forest. Ear and nostril valves close when it submerges, and it escapes by slipping into streams and resting spots in rivers or bank-side dens. No reliable speed data exist. On open ground its short legs and rotund body limit sharp turns.

### Endurance
Pygmy hippos spend about six hours a night foraging and rest in water during the day, often in the same spot for several days. Their skin depends on water to stay moist and cool, so prolonged exertion on dry land in heat is a real weakness.

### Senses
A nocturnal forest animal that relies on smell and hearing. It marks trails by waving its tail while defecating to spread dung. In a Taï National Park camera-trap study, pygmy hippos reacted to the cameras in about 53% of distance records, and reactions stayed high after installation, suggesting strong responsiveness to unfamiliar objects and smells. No sensory thresholds are published.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior is simple and cautious: hide by day in water, forage along known trails at night, retreat when disturbed. Individuals meeting each other tend to ignore rather than fight.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary or in pairs and mother-calf units. Field estimates give male ranges of about **1.85 km²** and female ranges of **0.4–0.6 km²**, with male ranges overlapping several females. It is a herbivore: ferns, broad-leaved plants, forbs, sedges, shoots and fallen fruit.

### Fighting and aggression
Generally docile, but can be highly aggressive at times. There have been several attacks on people, mostly provoked, with no recorded human deaths. In zoos, parent-inflicted trauma accounted for **28%** of neonatal deaths in a review of 404 captive animals, showing that adults can bite with lethal force against small targets.

### Predator defense
Adults have few natural enemies; leopards are the main predator named by ADW. Young are taken by leopards, Central African rock pythons and West African crocodiles. The main defense is concealment and escape into water.

### Environmental strengths
- Swamp forest, riverbanks and dense undergrowth with trails and water close by.
- Streams and pools, where it can submerge and hide.
- Night conditions, when it is naturally active.

### Weaknesses
- Low absolute mass compared with the roster's large herbivores.
- Short legs and rotund build limit speed and turning.
- Skin dries and cracks quickly away from water.
- Generally shy; tends to hide rather than fight.
- Bite is the only real weapon.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** smaller mammals and reptiles that must close to biting range; opponents that cannot follow it into water.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized herbivores with horns; medium carnivores such as leopards.
- **Bad:** big cats, bears and large crocodiles; large herbivores with horns or tusks and a big mass advantage; open, dry, hot ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Hippo Gape Bite** — a wide-opening mouth armed with ever-growing canines and forward incisors, used for defense and fighting rather than feeding; dangerous to small opponents at close range.
2. **Streambed Vanish** — closes its ears and nostrils with muscular valves and slips underwater or into bank-side resting spots, breaking contact with land predators.

### Unique traits
1. **Forest Hippo** — one of only two living hippo species, and the one built for rainforest trails and swamp streams instead of open rivers and grassland.
2. **Blood-Sweat Skin** — thick-dermis skin coated with a pinkish secretion that helps protect it from sun, but which dries and cracks quickly without water.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Lowland primary and secondary forest near rivers, streams and palm swamps in West Africa. The largest populations are in Liberia, with smaller ones in Sierra Leone, Guinea and Côte d'Ivoire, mostly near the Liberian border. The Niger Delta form has not been reliably recorded for decades. Its range does not overlap with the common hippopotamus.

### Diet and ecology
A selective herbivore of the forest floor: herbs, ferns, broad-leaved plants, herbaceous shoots, forbs, sedges and fallen fruit, rarely grass or aquatic plants. It forages for about six hours a night along game trails and returns to water by day.

### Social structure
Solitary or in pairs or mother-calf groups. Individuals tend to ignore each other when they meet rather than fight. Male ranges (about 1.85 km²) overlap those of several females (0.4–0.6 km²).

### Reproduction and life history
Sexual maturity at three to five years. Gestation is 190–210 days and usually produces a single calf of 4.5–6.2 kg, born on land or in water. Calves hide in water while the mother forages and are weaned at six to eight months. Almost all breeding data come from zoos, where the international studbook is kept by Basel Zoo.

### Conservation
Endangered on the IUCN Red List and listed on CITES Appendix II. The main threat is loss and fragmentation of forest through logging, settlement and farming, with bushmeat hunting in Liberia as a further pressure. Camera traps in Sapo National Park, Liberia, produced the first photographs confirming the species survived the Liberian civil wars. The captive population breeds well and more than doubled between 1970 and 1991.

### Special features
- Ever-growing canines and a wide, fighting gape.
- Muscular valves that close the ears and nostrils underwater.
- Thick dermis coated with a pinkish, sun-screening skin secretion.
- Tail-waving dung spreading to mark forest trails.
- Mates and gives birth on land or in water.

### Human interaction
Usually shy and seldom seen, but has attacked people on several occasions, mostly when provoked; no human deaths are recorded. It is hunted illegally for meat in Liberia. A zoo-born pygmy hippo, Moo Deng of Khao Kheow Open Zoo in Thailand, became a worldwide internet celebrity in 2024.

### Genuine fun facts
- Only two hippo species are alive today, and the pygmy hippo weighs less than a quarter as much as the common hippo.
- Its ears and nostrils have muscular valves that seal when it dives.
- Its skin secretes a pinkish fluid nicknamed "blood sweat", which is neither blood nor true sweat.
- Pygmy hippos spread their dung by vigorously waving their tails, marking the trails they use.
- Calves can swim almost immediately after birth.
- The species was unknown outside West Africa until the 19th century.
- Camera traps in Liberia's Sapo National Park provided the first proof that pygmy hippos survived the country's civil wars.

### Concise site-ready summary
The pygmy hippopotamus is a shy, nocturnal forest hippo from West Africa, about 250 kg of dense body with thick skin and a wide mouth of ever-growing tusks. It prefers to vanish into streams rather than fight, but can bite hard when cornered, making it a sturdy mid-weight defender rather than a heavy hitter.

### Rich narrative profile
The pygmy hippo is what a hippo looks like when it lives under a rainforest canopy. It stands under a metre tall, weighs about a quarter of a tonne, and is shaped like a smooth, glossy barrel on short legs. By day it rests in streams or bank-side hollows; by night it walks the same worn trails through the swamp forest, browsing ferns, shoots and fallen fruit and marking the path with tail-flung dung.

Its fighting equipment is the hippo family's: a gape that opens very wide and canines that never stop growing, used for defense and contests rather than chewing. Zoo records show that adults can inflict lethal bites on small animals, and people have been attacked when they pressed too close. Yet the species' usual answer to danger is to disappear. It seals its ears and nostrils and sinks into the nearest stream, where a leopard cannot follow.

On the ABS scale that makes the pygmy hippo a compact defender with a real but modest bite. It sits close to other 250–300 kg forest herbivores such as the okapi and bongo in size and power, trades their speed for thicker skin and a biting weapon, and remains far below the common hippopotamus in every physical category. Heat and dry ground are its limits; water is its refuge.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** lower bound differs (160 vs 180 kg) and all figures lean on zoo animals; 250 kg avoids overweight captives.
- **Lifespan:** only captive data exist; 30 years is a low-confidence placeholder at the bottom of the captive range.
- **Speed:** no measurement; popular speed claims were not accepted.
- **Taxonomy:** *Choeropsis* and *Hexaprotodon* are both in use; the site follows *Choeropsis liberiensis*.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hippopotamus (1,800 kg; A90 D84):** the pygmy hippo shares the weapon type and skin but has about one-seventh of the mass and a far milder temperament, so Attack (50.0) and Defense (52.0) sit 30-plus points lower. Agility (42.0) stays near the common hippo's 48.0 because neither is nimble on land.
- **Versus Okapi (275 kg; A53 D44) and Bongo (300 kg; A59 D49):** similar mass forest herbivores. The pygmy hippo gets higher Protection and Defense for its thick dermis, slightly lower Attack than the horned bongo, and much lower Agility and Speed.
- **Versus Tapir and Wild Boar:** neither has a research report yet; their live values (Tapir A2.3, Wild Boar A75.4) are legacy placeholders and are not used as anchors. A 250 kg pygmy hippo should end up heavier and better protected than a lowland tapir, with a comparable or stronger bite.
- **Versus Leopard (60 kg; A60):** the leopard keeps higher Attack and much higher Agility as a dedicated killer, while the pygmy hippo's mass and thick skin give it the higher Defense.
