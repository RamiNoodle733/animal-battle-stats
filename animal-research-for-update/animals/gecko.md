# Gecko research profile: Tokay Gecko (*Gekko gecko*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Roster label:** Gecko
- **Live schema taxon:** Gekkonidae, a family-level label rather than a species.
- **Staging resolution:** **Tokay Gecko, *Gekko gecko* (Linnaeus, 1758)**. A species-level resolution is necessary for defensible physical fields. It is a large, well-studied true gecko that strongly represents the live profile's climbing concept. This is a staging proposal, not a production taxonomy edit.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature **male**, 0.25 kg and 38.0 cm total length. Males are materially larger than females and are the primary territorial calling sex.
- **Dimorphism:** Smithsonian gives males about 35–40 cm and females about 20–30 cm. Behavioral Ecology at the University of Bern reports males as territorial and both sexes as highly aggressive. Male size therefore fits the project's stronger/fighting-sex rule.
- **Population variation / taxonomy caution:** Published size values vary substantially by population and source. WWF India gives broad male and female length ranges and 150–400 g mass for the species, while Sacramento Zoo gives 250–350 g. Northern populations historically confused with the Tokay complex require care because *Gekko reevesii* is now recognized separately.

## 2. Physical measurements and factual evidence

### Mass
WWF India reports **150–400 g** and Sacramento Zoo reports **250–350 g**. A 2025 comparative gecko-trade study lists *G. gecko* body mass at **169.8 g**, illustrating how datasets can differ with sampling and definitions. For a robust but non-record adult male, **250 g** is a conservative canonical choice within institutional adult values.

### Length
Smithsonian reports males at **35–40 cm** and females at **20–30 cm**. WWF India reports broader male **28–51 cm** and female **18–48 cm** ranges. A documented adult male museum specimen from Timor-Leste (USNM 573671) measured **142 mm SVL and 236 mm total length**, showing substantial geographic/specimen variation. Canonical total length is **38.0 cm**, not the live placeholder 100 cm.

### Height
A standard standing/shoulder-height measurement is not biologically useful for this sprawling arboreal lizard and no defensible representative value was found. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0**, meaning unsupported/not applicable rather than literally zero body thickness.

### Speed
A 2021 controlled locomotion study on *G. gecko* demonstrated strong temperature dependence: animals ran significantly slower and stopped more at 12°C than at 32°C, while humidity and substrate wettability did not produce the predicted running-speed effect. The accessible abstract does not provide a defensible species maximum in m/s. Internet claims of 15 km/h or higher are not adopted. **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** pending a directly supported maximum.

### Lifespan
ADW reports **10.5 years average in captivity**. Smithsonian/SeaWorld and zoo sources report roughly 10–20 years as captive potential. Canonical `lifespan_years` is **10.5**, explicitly a captive-demographic figure rather than a measured wild mean.

### Bite and jaws
Tokays have a broad head, strong jaw musculature and a defensive bite capable of drawing blood. A peer-reviewed 1997 prey-handling experiment quantified the mechanical force/work required to process prey items, but it does not provide a valid whole-animal bite-pressure PSI field. **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0**. No PSI is fabricated from force or anecdotal bite severity.

### Adhesive system
Tokay toe pads use microscopic setae and spatulae to generate dry adhesion through intermolecular interactions. This permits rapid movement on vertical and overhanging surfaces. Smithsonian describes attachment powerful enough for the animal to cling with very little toe contact, but the popular whole-animal load-equivalent statement should not be treated as a combat lifting-force measurement.

### Tail and regeneration
The tail contributes balance, maneuvering and energy storage. Tokays can autotomize the tail under threat and regenerate it. Smithsonian states regeneration can occur in roughly three weeks, with the replacement generally not as long as the original. Tail loss is an escape mechanism with real biological cost, not free armor.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons
The principal offensive weapon is a rapid jaw strike followed by a tenacious bite. Tokays also use body positioning and close-range lunging. They lack venom, armor, horns or specialized slicing claws. Their small absolute mass sharply limits damage against medium and large roster animals.

### Prey handling and predation
Tokays are predominantly visual nocturnal foragers. The University of Bern notes excellent night vision, color vision and UV perception. Diet is dominated by invertebrates, but small vertebrates are also taken. The 1997 prey-handling experiment showed that bite number and mechanical work vary with prey type and size, evidence of mechanically responsive prey processing rather than a single stereotyped bite.

### Intraspecific fighting
Males defend territories and advertise with the characteristic call. Aggressive displays include mouth gaping and barking, and direct fights can involve biting. Both sexes can show high aggression. This supports meaningful Ferocity for a tiny vertebrate, but absolute Attack remains low.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The adhesive toe system is the defining combat-relevant adaptation. It allows abrupt changes between floor, wall, trunk and ceiling, creating escape and approach routes unavailable to most terrestrial opponents. The 2021 experiment also shows that locomotor performance is temperature-sensitive, a critical environmental limitation.

### Defense and durability
Physical protection is weak: ordinary reptilian scales and a small body provide little resistance to crushing, biting or blunt trauma. Defense instead comes from adhesion, crevice use, camouflage, threat displays, rapid repositioning and tail autotomy.

### Endurance
Tokays are ectotherms. Their locomotor output depends strongly on temperature, and there is no evidence for mammal-like sustained pursuit. Routine nocturnal activity and repeated climbing support moderate stamina for a lizard, not high absolute endurance.

### Senses
Large lidless eyes are adapted to nocturnal vision. The species also uses olfaction and the vomeronasal/Jacobson's system. Its vocal communication is unusually conspicuous for a lizard. These are useful awareness tools but do not justify treating generic sensory excellence as a direct damage multiplier.

### Intelligence and tactics
Tokays show territorial decision-making, visual prey selection, vocal communication and parental care. Bern reports captive parental behavior including egg/juvenile defense and assistance around hatching. This supports more behavioral complexity than the live placeholder suggests, while remaining far below corvid, primate and advanced social-mammal cognition.

### Environmental strengths
- **Excellent:** trees, walls, cliffs, buildings, dry clean glass and complex vertical terrain.
- **Good:** warm tropical environments with abundant cover and climbing routes.
- **Poor:** cold conditions, where locomotion slows markedly.
- **Compromised:** wet/hydrophilic surfaces can reduce adhesive performance or alter willingness to traverse surfaces.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny absolute mass and low stopping power.
- No armor and little trauma tolerance against much larger animals.
- Bite requires very close range.
- Adhesion is environment-dependent.
- Ectothermy makes cold a direct performance penalty.
- Tail autotomy helps escape but sacrifices tissue and function.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** similarly tiny arthropods and fragile prey, especially in vertical terrain.
- **Mixed:** other small lizards where adhesion, bite placement and territorial aggression matter.
- **Bad:** almost every medium or large vertebrate, heavily armored arthropods of comparable scale, and opponents able to pin or crush it.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Van der Waals Grip** — microscopic adhesive toe structures permit secure locomotion on vertical and overhanging surfaces, dramatically expanding usable combat terrain and escape routes.
2. **Sacrificial Tail Escape** — caudal autotomy can leave an attacker with the tail while the gecko escapes; the tail later regenerates, though the replacement is imperfect and the event has energetic/function costs.

### Unique traits
1. **Tokay Territorial Bark** — the loud species-defining call advertises territory and reproductive presence, paired with aggressive mouth-gaping and barking displays.
2. **Nocturnal Color Vision** — unusually capable low-light visual performance, including color and UV sensitivity, supports prey detection and navigation after dark.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
*G. gecko* is native across South and Southeast Asia and parts of the Indo-Australian region, using tropical forests, trees, limestone/rock environments and human structures. Introduced populations occur outside the native range, including parts of the United States. Taxonomic boundaries in northern portions of the historical Tokay range require care because *G. reevesii* is distinct.

### Diet and ecology
The species is a nocturnal carnivore dominated by large insects and other invertebrates, with small lizards and other small vertebrates taken opportunistically. It is both predator and prey and can reduce insect abundance around buildings.

### Social structure and reproduction
Tokays are territorial, particularly males, but breeding pairs/family associations can be more socially complex than the old stereotype of a purely solitary gecko. Females commonly cement pairs of hard-shelled eggs to vertical surfaces. Bern researchers report parental care behaviors in captivity, including defense and interactions with juveniles.

### Conservation
The 2019 IUCN assessment lists *G. gecko* as **Least Concern**, while CITES regulates international trade under Appendix II. TRAFFIC/IUCN analysis notes a very broad range and adaptability but also substantial trade and reported local declines in parts of Asia. Global LC status therefore should not be interpreted as absence of local pressure.

### Human interaction
Tokays readily inhabit buildings and can benefit people by consuming insects. They are also heavily traded as pets and for traditional-medicine markets. Handling can provoke a persistent bite, so they are poor candidates for casual handling.

### Adaptations
- Hierarchical toe-pad setae for dry adhesion.
- Nocturnal visual system with strong low-light/color performance.
- Vocal territorial communication.
- Tail autotomy and regeneration.
- Cryptic blue-gray/orange patterning and some physiological color modulation.
- Broad habitat tolerance including human structures.

### Genuine fun facts
- The common name “tokay” imitates the male's famous call.
- A documented adult male museum specimen from Timor-Leste measured only 236 mm total length, showing why one internet “maximum size” should not define every population.
- Tokays can traverse vertical and inverted surfaces using microscopic toe structures rather than glue or suction cups.
- Their eyelids are fused and transparent rather than blinking like mammalian eyelids.
- The species is regulated under CITES Appendix II despite its global Least Concern assessment because international trade is substantial.
- Controlled experiments show that cold, not simply humidity, can sharply depress running performance.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Tokay Gecko is a large, territorial nocturnal gecko whose real advantage is not brute force but extraordinary three-dimensional mobility. Adhesive toe pads let it sprint across walls and ceilings, while a tenacious bite and detachable regenerating tail give it useful close-range offense and escape tools. At only a few hundred grams, however, it remains physically fragile against larger roster animals.

### Rich narrative profile
The Tokay Gecko turns architecture into a weapon. A wall that blocks a terrestrial animal is simply another running surface, and a ceiling can become an escape lane. Its toe pads create this advantage through millions of microscopic contact structures, giving the animal exceptional control without requiring claws to penetrate the surface. In warm conditions, that makes a tokay extraordinarily difficult for a similarly small ground-bound opponent to corner.

Its reputation for aggression is real but must be scaled correctly. A mature male has a broad head, strong jaws and a willingness to bite during territorial conflict, yet it weighs only a fraction of a kilogram. Against insects, small vertebrates or another gecko, that bite is meaningful. Against a coyote, cat or even many mid-sized birds, it is not. The ABS profile therefore places its physical Attack and Defense near the low end while reserving elite values for Agility and Special, where its biology genuinely competes with the roster's specialists.

The tail adds another layer. Autotomy can convert a successful grab into an escape, but losing the tail is costly and the regenerated structure is not identical to the original. The result is a specialist whose best battle plan is positioning, surface access and disengagement, not trading damage.

### Source conflicts and uncertainty
- Adult size varies materially by population and source. The 38 cm canonical male represents a robust adult, not a universal mean.
- Mass reports span roughly 150–400 g. The combat specimen uses ~250 g, while the one-decimal site field rounds to 0.3 kg.
- No accepted maximum speed or bite PSI is supplied. These remain evidence gaps rather than invitations to copy tertiary values.
- The live family-level label Gekkonidae is too broad for species-level physical claims. All proposed numbers in this report are specifically for *Gekko gecko*.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Fennec Fox / Ferret:** Tokay Attack and Defense must remain far lower because 0.25 kg is roughly one-sixth their canonical mass and it lacks mammalian jaw/body power. Its Agility can exceed them because wall/ceiling adhesion is a distinct maneuverability advantage.
- **Versus Flying Squirrel:** both are tiny locomotor specialists. Flying Squirrel's glide creates longer-range aerial repositioning, while Tokay adhesion creates finer close-surface control. Both deserve elite Agility/Special without proportional-strength leakage into Attack.
- **Versus Emperor Scorpion:** Tokay has much greater vertebrate mobility, vision and tactical flexibility, but the scorpion's venom system justifies a different kind of high Special. Tokay's nonvenomous bite should not approach Gaboon Viper attack/special lethality.
- **Versus Coyote/Dingo/Dhole:** no physical score should be compressed toward canids merely because the Tokay is exceptionally strong for its size. Absolute scaling requires a very large power and durability gap.
- **Inflation check:** 96 Agility and 94 Special are high because they describe rare capabilities that genuinely scale independently of body mass. Raw Power 7, Attack 12 and Defense 11 preserve the enormous absolute combat gap.
