# Africanized Honey Bee (*Apis mellifera scutellata* hybrid) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Africanized honey bee ("killer bee")
- **Scientific name:** *Apis mellifera scutellata* (hybrid) — the African subspecies *A. m. scutellata* crossed with European honey bee subspecies in the Americas; family Apidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the Africanized honey bee of the Americas, not the pure African *A. m. scutellata* of eastern and southern Africa and not European honey bees. It arose after 26 swarms with their queens escaped from an experimental apiary in Brazil in 1957 and hybridised with local European bees; colonies were found in southern Texas in 1990.
- **Living/extinct:** Living
- **Canonical combat specimen:** a healthy adult female worker acting as a guard or forager. Workers are the only caste that defends the nest by stinging; queens rarely sting outside fights with rival queens, and drones have no sting. Following the hornet and ant reports, all ratings describe one worker, not the colony.
- **Population notes:** Africanized workers are smaller than European workers; in a study of swarms, European workers were significantly heavier (Otis 1982). Identification relies on measurements such as forewing length: bees with forewings above 9.12 mm are very likely European (FABIS method), with genetic tests for confirmation.

### Sting and venom
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-force measurement; mandibles are used for handling wax and food, not fighting.
- **Sting:** a barbed sting shaft that lodges in the skin of mammals. When the bee pulls away, the sting apparatus tears off (sting autotomy) with the venom sac, pumping muscles, terminal ganglion and the Koschevnikov gland that releases alarm pheromone. The detached apparatus keeps pumping venom into the victim. The worker dies soon after.
- **Venom quantity:** Schmidt et al. (1990) found Africanized workers carry **less** venom than European workers (about **94 µg** versus **147 µg** per bee) with lower melittin content, and concluded that the danger of Africanized attacks comes from the number of stings, not stronger venom.
- **Venom chemistry:** melittin, the main pain-producing and cell-damaging peptide, makes up roughly 50–60% of dry honey bee venom, with phospholipase A2 as another major component.
- **Human effects of mass stinging:** kidney failure has followed a few hundred stings in adults, and deaths occur in massive attacks, though some people have survived 600–1,500 stings with treatment.

### Defensive structure
- A lightly sclerotised, hairy body. No armour.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The primary weapon is the barbed venom sting, which can be used once against a mammal. The secondary weapon is chemical: the alarm pheromone, mainly isopentyl acetate (smelling like banana oil), released from the sting apparatus marks the target and recruits more defenders. The mandibles can nip but do little.

### Offensive mechanics
A single worker delivers one sting and a small venom dose (about 94 µg). The detached sting keeps pumping venom and keeps releasing alarm scent, so the spot it hits becomes a beacon for other bees. Studies of honey bee defense show guards are alerted by the pheromone, and a moving target triggers stinging. In a one-on-one contest, however, the single sting is a painful but minor wound for any large animal.

### Defensive features and durability
The worker has almost no physical protection and is easily crushed. Its value to the colony is its willingness to die defending it.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A strong, nimble flier that can hover, dodge and chase. Africanized bees pursue threats up to about 400 m from the nest, much farther than European bees.

### Endurance
Foragers make repeated long flights, and honey bees are documented foraging over several kilometres. Pursuit flights of hundreds of metres show good sustained flight for an insect, though modest at absolute scale.

### Senses
Colour vision, scent detection sensitive enough to pick up alarm pheromone and floral odours, and sensitivity to movement, which guards use to pick targets.

### Intelligence and tactics
Honey bees are among the best-studied insect learners. Workers learn scents, colours and routes, and communicate the direction and distance of food with the waggle dance: the angle of the waggle run encodes direction relative to the sun and its duration encodes distance. A 2023 Science study showed young bees improve their dancing by following experienced dancers. In defense, individual workers act on pheromone and movement cues in a flexible, coordinated way.

### Sociality and hunting
Highly social. A colony has one queen, thousands of workers and seasonal drones. Workers do not hunt; they gather nectar and pollen. Africanized colonies swarm (split to form new colonies) as many as 16 times a year versus once or twice for European colonies, abscond (abandon the nest) readily when disturbed or short of food, and sometimes take over European colonies.

### Fighting and aggression
The defining trait. Given the same disturbance, Africanized colonies sting 4–10 times more often and send 10–30 times more bees in pursuit than European colonies, and victims receive far more stings. They defend a larger zone around the nest and react faster.

### Environmental strengths
- Warm tropical and subtropical open country, including savanna, farmland and towns.
- Near the nest, where hundreds of defenders can join in.
- Open air, where a flying target is hard to swat.

### Weaknesses
- Each worker can sting a mammal only once and then dies.
- Small venom dose per bee; one sting rarely matters to a large animal.
- Tiny, fragile body.
- Poorer tolerance of cold climates than European bees, which limits its spread north.
- Colony defense is strongest near the nest and fades with distance.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** mammals and other large animals that approach the nest (as a colony); other insects at the nest entrance.
- **Mixed:** birds and animals that eat bees quickly or have thick coats.
- **Bad:** one-on-one fights with anything larger than an insect, specialist bee predators, and armoured or thick-skinned opponents that the sting cannot penetrate.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Tear-Away Venom Pump** — the barbed sting lodges in skin and tears free with the venom sac, muscles and nerve ganglion, which keep pumping venom after the bee has gone.
2. **Banana-Scent Target Marking** — the sting apparatus releases alarm pheromone, mainly isopentyl acetate, that marks the victim and brings guards and other workers to the same spot.

### Unique traits
1. **Long-Range Pursuit** — Africanized colonies chase threats up to about 400 m, sting 4–10 times more often and send 10–30 times more bees than European colonies.
2. **Waggle Dance Navigator** — workers tell nestmates the direction and distance of food with a dance, and young bees get better at it by following experienced dancers.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The pure African subspecies *A. m. scutellata* lives in the savannas and open country of eastern and southern Africa. The Africanized hybrid spread from southern Brazil after 1957 through much of South America, Central America and Mexico, and has been in the southern United States since 1990. It does best in warm climates and nests in cavities, including in and around buildings.

### Diet and ecology
Workers collect nectar (stored as honey) and pollen, plus water and plant resins. As pollinators they visit many crops and wild plants. Predators include birds and other insects that eat bees, and the honey and brood attract nest raiders.

### Social structure
A colony has one egg-laying queen, thousands of female workers and seasonal male drones. Workers change jobs with age, from nursing and nest work to guarding and foraging. Africanized colonies swarm very often, up to 16 times a year, and readily abscond.

### Reproduction and life history
The queen lays the eggs; workers raise larvae in wax cells. New colonies form by swarming, when the old queen leaves with part of the workforce. Summer workers live about a month; queens live for years.

### Conservation
Not Evaluated: no IUCN Red List assessment of the Africanized hybrid was located in this pass. It is an abundant, widespread and in many places invasive hybrid; in the Americas it is managed as a public-safety issue and also kept by beekeepers.

### Special features
- Barbed sting that tears free and keeps pumping venom.
- Alarm pheromone (mainly isopentyl acetate) that marks targets.
- Very high colony defensiveness and long pursuit distance.
- Waggle dance communication.
- Frequent swarming and absconding.
- Smaller body and smaller venom load per bee than European honey bees.

### Human interaction
Africanized bees are responsible for many serious mass-stinging incidents in the Americas, and tertiary sources credit them with around 1,000 human deaths since their arrival. Research shows their venom is not stronger than European bee venom; the danger comes from the number of bees and stings. Public-safety agencies, including the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services, publish guidance on avoiding colonies and escaping attacks. Many beekeepers in Latin America keep Africanized bees for honey and pollination.

### Genuine fun facts
- Africanized bees came from 26 swarms that escaped a Brazilian research apiary in 1957.
- Each Africanized worker carries less venom than a European honey bee: about 94 µg versus 147 µg.
- Their danger lies in numbers: colonies send up to 10–30 times more bees after a threat.
- They may chase a threat for about 400 m.
- The alarm pheromone smells like bananas.
- A detached sting keeps pumping venom after the bee flies off.
- Africanized colonies can swarm up to 16 times a year.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Africanized honey bee is a hybrid of African and European honey bees that has spread from Brazil across the Americas. One worker carries only a small venom dose and dies after stinging, but its tear-away sting keeps pumping and marks the target with banana-scented alarm pheromone. Its reputation comes from colonies that attack in huge numbers and pursue for hundreds of metres.

### Rich narrative profile
The Africanized honey bee began with an accident. In 1957, 26 swarms of African honey bees escaped from a research apiary in Brazil and bred with local European bees. Their descendants spread through South and Central America and Mexico and reached Texas in 1990. They look almost identical to European honey bees, a little smaller, and they make honey and pollinate crops in the same way.

The difference is behaviour. Disturb an Africanized colony and it reacts faster, sends 10–30 times more bees, stings 4–10 times more often and chases for up to about 400 m. Each worker's barbed sting tears away and keeps pumping venom, while the sting apparatus releases isopentyl acetate, an alarm scent that marks the victim for the next wave. Research shows the venom per bee is actually smaller than a European bee's; people are hurt by the sheer number of stings.

On the absolute battle scale, the ratings describe one worker, and one worker is a tiny, fragile insect with a single small dose of venom. It gets low Attack and Defense but strong Agility, high Ferocity and one of the best insect Intelligence scores, thanks to the learning and waggle-dance communication honey bees are famous for. The colony-scale threat is recorded in the profile and traits rather than inflated into one bee's physical ratings.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass and length:** no reliable measurement retrieved; the 70 mg and 1.2 cm values are placeholders. Web values of 19 mm and 3.5 cm are rejected as implausible for workers.
- **Worker lifespan:** 31 days comes from a search summary without a pinned source.
- **Defensiveness multipliers:** 4–10 times more stings and 10–30 times more pursuing bees appear in extension sources via search summaries; the 400 m pursuit distance is widely repeated.
- **Death toll:** about 1,000 human deaths is a tertiary figure.
- **Colony versus individual:** the colony's mass attack is real but is not scored as one worker's Attack.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hornet (A 10.0, D 5.0, Ag 79.0, St 39.0, I 43.0, Sp 76.0):** a hornet worker is larger, can sting repeatedly and hunts other insects, so it keeps higher Attack, Defense, Agility and Special. The honey bee gets higher Intelligence (50.0) for its well-documented learning and dance communication, and higher Ferocity because Africanized colonies defend so aggressively.
- **Versus Bullet Ant (A 18.0, D 2.0, Sp 96.0):** the bullet ant's venom causes extreme pain from a single sting and can be used repeatedly; the bee's single, smaller dose keeps its Attack and Special well below.
- **Versus Army Ant (A 1.8, D 0.9, I 11.0, Sp 24.0):** both are famous for colony-scale attacks that are excluded from individual scores. The bee's sting, flight and dance communication justify higher Attack, Agility, Intelligence and Special for one worker.
- **Versus Mosquito (A 0.6, D 0.5):** the bee's venomous sting causes real pain and swelling, so its Attack is clearly higher, while both remain at the floor for Size and Raw Power.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the "killer bee" reputation rests on hundreds of bees stinging together. That is recorded in Ferocity, Tactics, traits and the profile, not converted into one worker's Attack. Venom potency is not inflated: Africanized venom is weaker per bee than European venom.
