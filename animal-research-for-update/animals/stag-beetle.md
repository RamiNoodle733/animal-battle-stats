# Stag Beetle (*Lucanus cervus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Stag Beetle (European stag beetle, greater stag beetle)
- **Scientific name:** *Lucanus cervus* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** the European stag beetle, the largest beetle in Europe and the species in the site's photo, not the many other stag beetles of Asia and the tropics.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult major male, **6.0 cm** long including its antler-like mandibles and about **2.5 g**, in the upper half of the 35–75 mm male range.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger, carry the enlarged mandibles and are the sex that fights, so a male is used. Females are 30–50 mm long with small mandibles, although they give the more painful bite to a human finger.
- **Population notes:** body size varies across Europe, with beetles from Spain, Germany and the Netherlands larger than those from Belgium or the UK. Males come in two forms, small "minor" males and large "major" males with disproportionately long mandibles. Four subspecies are recognised.

- **Canonical `length_cm`: 6.0 cm** for a major male including mandibles.

- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- Larvae feed underground for **3–7 years** (Wikipedia); PTES says at least 3–4 years depending on temperature.
- Adults live only **a few weeks** in summer.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 4.0** for the whole life cycle, almost all of it as a larva.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Bite and grip
- Redolfi De Zan, Carpaneto and Romiti (preprint) measured 60 males with a force sensor: mean bite strength **6.39 N**, maximum **20.19 N**. The mean force needed to pull 133 males off an oak branch (grip strength) was **1.62 N**.
- Giannetti et al. (2024) tested 70 males and found no link between bite force and mandible length.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** The measurements are forces, not pressures, and are not converted to PSI.

### Weapon dimensions
- Male mandibles are enlarged and antler-shaped; relative mandible size increases with body size, and larger males win more ground fights. No standard mandible-length figure for a representative male was located.

### Armor
- Hard elytra (wing cases) with a mean thickness of **0.73 mm** (J Exp Biol 2026).
- Kundanati et al. (2019): elytra resist a needle puncture force of **1.8 ± 0.4 N**, with an outer layer of modulus 10.3 GPa and hardness 0.7 GPa, and flex with a "snap-through" that absorbs impact energy and springs back into shape.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The male's weapons are its mandibles, used almost only against other males. Fighting males grab a rival with the mandibles, lift it and throw it off the log or branch (Wikipedia; Redolfi De Zan et al.). Measured bites average 6.39 N with a maximum of 20.19 N, strong for a 2.5 g insect but tiny in absolute terms. Wikipedia notes that the male's mandibles are too weak to harm a person, while females can give a painful bite.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Adults walk and climb well on bark, using their tarsal claws to hold the surface. Flight is slow, low-pitched and lumbering, mostly at sunset. Before flying, males warm up their flight muscles: thermal imaging showed the thorax rising above 30 °C while the abdomen stayed near air temperature (Giannetti et al. 2026). In flight aggregations smaller males are more agile and catch females in mid-air more often than large males (Giannetti et al. 2024).

### Endurance
Adults live only a few weeks and cannot eat solid food; they run on fat reserves built up as larvae and drink liquids such as sap, nectar and fruit juice. Radio-tracking studies found dispersal distances from a few hundred metres up to about 5 km, with males the more active flyers (reviewed by Cox et al. 2020).

### Senses
Pheromones guide males to females, the antennae carry chemical and touch sensors, and the mandibles have many mechanoreceptors used to judge a rival during a tussle. Vision plays a minor role at dusk.

### Intelligence and tactics
Contests follow a fixed escalation: threat posture, body raising and rubbing mandibles, then bite attempts, tussles, clamping, lifting and throwing. Males appear to assess both their own and their rival's fighting ability and give up early against much larger opponents, so serious injury is rare: only one injury (a damaged leg) was seen in 286 staged interactions (Redolfi De Zan et al.). Smaller males use a second mating tactic, catching flying females in swarms, instead of fighting on the ground (Giannetti et al. 2024).

### Sociality and hunting
Not a predator. Males gather in small ground aggregations (3–6 males in an area 1–2.5 m across) or in flying swarms of 10–30 males 2–16 m above the ground, both centred on females.

### Fighting and aggression
Ground fights last about 4–11 minutes, until a female walks up and is grabbed by one male, who then carries her away from rivals before mating. The natural reaction to an approaching large animal is to stay still.

### Environmental strengths
- Old broadleaf woodland, parks and gardens with dead wood and stumps.
- Tree trunks and logs, where grip decides who is thrown off.
- Warm, dry summer evenings for flight.

### Weaknesses
- Negligible absolute mass and power.
- Adults cannot refuel on solid food.
- Slow, clumsy flight and exposed ground behaviour make it easy prey for crows, magpies, cats, foxes and badgers.
- The exoskeleton protects against small pinches and punctures, not against a bird's beak or a mammal's bite.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** other beetles and small insects that can be gripped and thrown.
- **Mixed:** large insects of similar size with their own mandibles or horns.
- **Bad:** birds, mammals, spiders large enough to wrap it, and essentially every vertebrate on the roster.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Antler Throw** — males seize a rival with their enlarged mandibles, lift it off the bark and drop or throw it off the branch, winning without needing to wound.
2. **Snap-Through Armor** — the elytra flex with a snap-through that absorbs impact energy and springs back, and resist a needle puncture of about 1.8 N, protecting the beetle during fights and falls.

### Unique traits
1. **Years Underground** — it spends three to seven years as a blind, C-shaped larva eating rotting wood below ground, then only a few weeks as a winged adult.
2. **Two Kinds of Male** — small males with modest mandibles and large males with huge ones follow different strategies: big males win ground fights, while small males win more often in mid-air mating swarms.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Widespread across Europe, absent from Ireland, and found east to the Caucasus, Asia Minor, Syria and western Kazakhstan. In Britain it is mostly confined to south-east England. It lives in old broadleaf woodland, parks, hedgerows and gardens with rotting wood, especially oak, and is linked with lime, beech, willow, poplar, ash, horse chestnut, cherry and walnut.

### Diet and ecology
Larvae eat decaying wood below ground, in stumps, old trees, fence posts, compost heaps and leaf mould. Adults drink sap, nectar and juice from fallen fruit and otherwise live on fat stored as larvae. It is a flagship species for dead-wood conservation, since its larvae depend on buried, rotting broadleaf wood.

### Social structure
Adults are not social, but males gather around females in small fighting groups on the ground or in flying swarms. Males are more active flyers than females; genetic data suggest that longer-term dispersal is mainly by females.

### Reproduction and life history
Adults appear from late May to early August and are most active in the evening. Males carry a won female to a quieter spot before mating. Females lay about 20–30 eggs in decaying wood in the soil. Larvae communicate by stridulating with combs on their legs. The mature larva builds a fist-sized chamber in the soil, pupates, and the adult emerges the following summer.

### Conservation
Listed as **Near Threatened** in the IUCN Red List European assessment (2010), because it is declining in the north and centre of its range. It is on Annex II of the EU Habitats Directive, Appendix III of the Bern Convention and Schedule 5 of the UK Wildlife and Countryside Act. It has disappeared from Latvia, vanished from Denmark around 1970 and was reintroduced there in 2013. Main threats are the tidying away of dead wood and stumps, loss of old trees and road traffic.

### Special features
- Antler-like mandibles in males, reddish in colour.
- Hard, puncture-resistant elytra with a snap-through response.
- Pre-flight warm-up of the flight muscles.
- A multi-year underground larval stage.
- Two male forms with different mating tactics.

### Human interaction
Harmless to people apart from a pinch, mainly from females. Many people build "log pyramids" (stumperies) of upright buried logs in gardens to give larvae rotting wood. In Germany it was chosen as Insect of the Year 2012, and road casualties are collected by volunteers for monitoring.

### Genuine fun facts
- Both the scientific name *cervus* ("deer") and the common name come from the male's antler-like mandibles.
- A fully grown larva can be 11 cm long, longer than the adult beetle.
- Small males catch females in mid-air in swarms of 10–30 males flying up to 16 m above the ground.
- Female stag beetles, with their small jaws, give a more painful bite than the big-jawed males.
- Males warm their flight muscles above 30 °C before take-off.
- Larvae "talk" by rubbing combs on their legs.
- In one study of 286 staged contests, only a single injury occurred.

### Concise site-ready summary
Europe's largest beetle, the stag beetle is famous for the male's antler-like mandibles, used to lift rivals and throw them off branches. It spends years underground as a larva and only weeks as an adult, and at about 2.5 g its fighting power is negligible outside the insect world.

### Rich narrative profile
The European stag beetle lives most of its life out of sight. For three to seven years it is a fat, cream-coloured grub chewing rotting wood underground, growing to 11 cm. Then, in a fist-sized chamber in the soil, it turns into the beetle everyone recognises: glossy black and chestnut, with the male's huge reddish jaws branching like a deer's antlers.

Adult life is short and busy. On warm summer evenings males warm up their flight muscles and take off on slow, buzzing flights in search of females. Where females gather, big males wrestle on logs, gripping rivals in their jaws, lifting them and throwing them off, while small males often do better by catching females in mid-air swarms. The contests are mostly shows of strength: males size each other up and the weaker one usually backs off before anyone is hurt.

In an Animal Battle Stats matchup, the stag beetle is an impressive insect wrestler but a tiny animal. Its bite of a few newtons cannot hurt a person, its armor protects it only against other insects, and crows, magpies, cats and foxes eat it readily. It belongs near the floor of the roster, alongside the other large beetles.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** the only readable measured mass is a five-specimen, unsexed mean of 2.47 g; the canonical 2.5 g may understate a large major male.
- **Bite:** the 6.39 N mean and 20.19 N maximum come from a preprint; the 6.9 N male bite quoted in the elytra paper refers to *Cyclommatus metallifer*, not this species, and is not used.
- **Larval duration:** 3–7 years (Wikipedia) versus at least 3–4 years (PTES); 4.0 years is used for the whole life cycle.
- **Speed:** no measured flight speed for this species; 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hercules Beetle (34 g; A7 D12 Ag44 St32 I20 Sp60):** a similar lift-and-throw fighter but more than ten times heavier, so the stag beetle sits just below it in Attack, Defense and Agility; the hercules horn clamp keeps the higher Special.
- **Versus Titan Beetle (37.5 g; A11 D14 Ag36 St26 I16 Sp42):** the titan's cutting mandibles can break human skin and its armor is heavier, so it stays above in Attack and Defense; the stag beetle flies more readily and has a more elaborate contest routine, hence higher Agility, Intelligence and Special.
- **Versus Bombardier Beetle (A5 D16 Sp85):** the stag beetle's grip does slightly more physical damage, but the bombardier's boiling chemical spray is a far rarer mechanism and deters predators better.
- **Anti-inflation check:** a 20 N maximum bite is remarkable for a 2.5 g insect, but on the absolute scale it keeps Attack in single digits.
