# Spider Monkey (*Ateles geoffroyi*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Spider Monkey (Geoffroy's spider monkey, also called the black-handed or Central American spider monkey)
- **Scientific name:** *Ateles geoffroyi* Kuhl, 1820
- **Exact animal represented:** Geoffroy's spider monkey of Mexico and Central America, the species shown in the site's photo (a Central American spider monkey at Caño Negro, Costa Rica). Other *Ateles* species of South America are not used for the measurements.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **7.5 kg**, near the Animal Diversity Web average adult mass (7.27 kg) and in the middle of the 6–9 kg range given by Wikipedia.
- **Sex choice:** males and females are roughly the same size, so size does not force the choice. A male is used because adult males form bonded coalitions, carry out the documented lethal attacks and the silent ground raids into neighbouring ranges.
- **Population notes:** at least five subspecies are listed, and coat colour varies from buff and reddish to brown or black, always with dark hands and feet and often a pale mask around the eyes and muzzle. Some authorities treat the black-headed spider monkey (*A. fusciceps*) as part of this species; it is not used here.

- No armor. Fur only. Defense rests on height in the canopy, speed of movement, group alarm calls and threat displays.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The spider monkey's weapons are its bite and its grip. It is a fruit-eater, not a predator, so it has no killing technique for prey. Aggression is aimed mostly at other spider monkeys. On Barro Colorado Island, Panama, three coalitionary attacks by adult males on subadult males were recorded, two followed by the victim's disappearance and presumed death (Campbell 2006). In Yucatán, Mexico, a young adult male that had been repeatedly injured was killed by at least one adult male in an intragroup attack (Valero et al. 2006). These show that males can inflict fatal injuries when several gang up on one victim, but in a one-on-one fight a slender 7.5 kg monkey has little power.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Movement is its great strength. It walks and runs on branches, climbs, and swings by its arms beneath branches (brachiation) with help from the tail. It can hang by the tail alone. It usually crosses between trees by pulling a branch of the next tree towards itself ("bridging") and leaps when needed. ADW calls spider monkeys among the most agile of the primates. Geoffroy's spider monkey swings less than other spider monkeys and comes to the ground more often, so it sits a little below the gibbon, the roster's brachiation specialist.

### Endurance
Groups use home ranges that can exceed 900 ha, and individuals travel about 2 km a day, with males and dominant animals covering more ground. Feeding peaks in the early morning and much of the day is spent resting. This is good daily travel without evidence of sustained high-intensity exertion.

### Senses
A daytime canopy animal that relies on vision (to find ripe fruit), hearing (individually distinctive calls) and touch through the sensitive tail pad. No measured sensory thresholds were found.

### Intelligence and tactics
Spider monkeys have a brain of about 107 g, twice that of a howler monkey of similar body size. In tests of inhibitory control (holding back a wrong but tempting response), spider monkeys performed at the level of chimpanzees, bonobos and orangutans and better than gorillas (Amici et al. 2008). Wikipedia reports that meta-analyses of primate cognition rank spider monkeys as the most intelligent New World monkeys. Males raid neighbouring ranges in single file on the ground in unusual silence, much like chimpanzee patrols (Aureli et al. 2006), and group members manage tension when subgroups meet by embracing (Aureli and Schaffner 2007). They do not use tools.

### Sociality and hunting
Groups of 20–42 members split into small, changing subgroups of two to six that forage separately (fission–fusion). Males stay in their birth group and are usually related, while females leave. Males bond through prolonged embracing and tail-wrapping.

### Fighting and aggression
Threat displays include a curled tail, an arched back, head shaking, branch shaking, barking and growling. When approached, spider monkeys shake branches, drop or throw branches (the genus page records limbs of up to 4 kg dropped towards intruders), and urinate or defecate on the intruder. Aggression rises when subgroups fuse, and it is usually defused by embraces.

### Environmental strengths
- Tall, continuous rainforest canopy, where it can move in three dimensions and escape by swinging.
- Semideciduous and mangrove forest.
- Height advantage for dropping branches on ground threats.

### Weaknesses
- Low absolute mass and power.
- No armor and a slender, long-limbed frame.
- Lethal aggression depends on coalitions; alone it has only a bite and a grip.
- Much weaker on the ground, where pumas have killed adults (Busia et al. 2018).

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small ground animals that cannot climb, slow climbers, animals that can be avoided or pelted from above.
- **Mixed:** other monkeys of similar size, where grip, bite and footing decide the fight.
- **Bad:** big cats (jaguar and puma are its main natural predators), large eagles, large constrictors, and any heavy predator that catches it on the ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Fifth-Hand Tail** — the prehensile tail has a bare, ridged pad near the tip and can hold the monkey's full weight, so it can hang, feed and grab with its hands free. In a fight it can stay anchored in a branch while its arms and teeth work.
2. **Canopy Bombardment** — when threatened from below it barks, shakes branches, and breaks off and drops limbs of up to 4 kg towards the intruder, letting it harass a ground opponent without coming within reach.

### Unique traits
1. **Thumbless Hook Hands** — the thumb is reduced to a tiny stub and the long fingers form hooks, a hand shape suited to swinging under branches rather than fine manipulation.
2. **Silent Raiding Parties** — adult males sometimes leave the trees and move single file on the ground in unusual silence into neighbouring ranges, a behaviour otherwise best known from chimpanzees.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Southern and eastern Mexico south through Guatemala, Belize, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua and Costa Rica to Panama, and possibly a small part of Colombia. It lives in rainforest, semideciduous forest and mangrove, mostly in the upper canopy, with the highest densities in evergreen forest. It needs large tracts of forest.

### Diet and ecology
Mainly ripe, fleshy fruit (70–80% of feeding time), with young leaves supplying protein, plus flowers, bark, insects, honey, seeds and buds. It gets much of its water from fruit and leaves and drinks from tree holes and bromeliads, and unlike other spider monkeys it also drinks from ground water. By swallowing fruit whole and passing the seeds, spider monkeys spread the seeds of many forest trees.

### Social structure
Fission–fusion communities of 20–42 members that break into small subgroups during the day. Males stay in their birth group and form strong bonds; females move to other groups at maturity. Each monkey has a distinctive whinny, which may allow individuals to recognise each other by voice alone.

### Reproduction and life history
There is no fixed breeding season. Gestation lasts about 7.5 months (226–232 days) and one young is born, rarely twins, every two to four years. Infants ride on the mother's chest, then her back, nurse until about one year, and are helped across canopy gaps by adults stretching across as living bridges. Females mature at about four years and males at about five.

### Conservation
Listed as **Endangered** by the IUCN (2021 assessment), mainly because of habitat loss, hunting and capture for the pet trade. Its slow reproduction means populations recover slowly. It was wiped out by hunting on Barro Colorado Island, Panama, by 1912; a reintroduction in 1959–1966 left one male and four females, which grew to 28 monkeys by 2003.

### Special features
- Fully prehensile tail with a ridged, bare gripping pad.
- Arms about 25% longer than the legs and hook-like hands with a tiny thumb.
- Arm-swinging, tail-hanging and branch-bridging locomotion.
- A large brain for its body size.
- Fission–fusion social life with bonded males.

### Human interaction
Hunted for food and captured for the pet trade in parts of its range. Frida Kahlo kept several as pets and painted this species in her 1938 *Self-Portrait with Monkey*. When people approach, wild groups bark, shake branches and may drop branches or urinate towards them.

### Genuine fun facts
- The genus name *Ateles* means "imperfect", referring to the missing thumb.
- The tail pad has skin ridges similar to fingerprints.
- Spider monkeys have dropped branches of up to 4 kg on people who followed them.
- They sometimes rub a mix of saliva and crushed lime leaves into their fur, which is thought to repel insects.
- Adults stretch across gaps in the canopy to form bridges for their young.
- Female spider monkeys have an enlarged, dangling clitoris that often leads observers to mistake them for males.
- In inhibitory-control tests, spider monkeys matched chimpanzees and outperformed gorillas.

### Concise site-ready summary
A long-limbed, thumbless acrobat of Central American rainforests, Geoffroy's spider monkey swings beneath branches and hangs by a tail that works like a fifth hand. It is clever and socially coordinated, but at about 7.5 kg it relies on height, speed and branch-shaking threats rather than fighting power.

### Rich narrative profile
Geoffroy's spider monkey is built for hanging. Its arms are a quarter longer than its legs, its thumb has shrunk to a tiny stub, and its long fingers curl into hooks that clip over branches as it swings. Its tail is stronger still: bare and ridged near the tip, it can hold the monkey's entire weight, pick fruit and scoop water, leaving the hands free.

Its social life is just as unusual. Communities of 20 to 42 animals rarely gather in one place; instead they split into small, shifting parties that forage separately and reunite with embraces that calm the tension of meeting. The males, who stay in the group they were born into, are bonded allies. They have been seen leaving the canopy to walk single file and silent into a neighbouring range, and on rare occasions groups of males have fatally attacked a younger male of their own community.

In a matchup, the spider monkey is fast, hard to reach and smart, but light. Its best defense is to climb out of range, bark, shake branches and drop heavy limbs on whatever is below. If a big cat, a large eagle or a heavy predator catches it, a slender 7.5 kg frame with only a bite and a grip gives it little chance.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** ADW's average (7.27 kg) and Wikipedia's range (6–9 kg) agree; 7.5 kg is used because the sexes are about the same size.
- **Lifespan:** wild lifespan is unknown; 27.0 is the top of the usual range reported for the genus, and captive records (33 years, one exceptional 61-year-old) are not used as the baseline.
- **Speed and bite:** no measurements; both 0.0.
- **Taxonomy:** some authorities include the black-headed spider monkey in this species; the measurements here come from Geoffroy's spider monkey sources.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Howler Monkey (7 kg; A27 D20 Ag76 St43 I70 Sp73):** almost the same mass and also prehensile-tailed. The spider monkey is far more agile (91.0), travels more (58.0) and is cognitively stronger (82.0); its Attack (26.0) sits just below the howler's because its hook-like hands are built for hanging.
- **Versus Gibbon (6.5 kg; A28 D22 Ag98 St62 I76 Sp72):** both are arm-swingers of similar size. The gibbon keeps the top Agility score because it is the specialist brachiator, while the spider monkey swings less and walks more; the spider monkey's tail and inhibitory-control results earn higher Special and Intelligence.
- **Versus Capuchin Monkey (4 kg; A23 D17 Ag84 St56 I92 Sp72):** the spider monkey is heavier, so Attack and Defense are slightly higher, and it is more agile; the tool-using capuchin keeps the higher Intelligence.
- **Versus Japanese Macaque (11.3 kg; A31 D28 I84):** the heavier, sturdier macaque stays above in Attack and Defense; Intelligence is similar.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the documented lethal attacks are coalitionary and are reflected in Tactics, not in Attack.
