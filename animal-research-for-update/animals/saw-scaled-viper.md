# Saw-scaled Viper (*Echis carinatus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Saw-scaled Viper (Indian saw-scaled viper, little Indian viper, Sindh saw-scaled viper)
- **Scientific name:** *Echis carinatus* (Schneider, 1801), family Viperidae, subfamily Viperinae
- **Exact animal represented:** the Asian saw-scaled viper *Echis carinatus* of the Indian subcontinent, the Middle East and Central Asia, covering the nominate *E. c. carinatus* (southern India, Sri Lanka) and *E. c. sochureki* (north-western India, Pakistan, Iran, Arabia, Central Asia). The African carpet vipers (*E. ocellatus*, *E. pyramidum* and others) are separate species and are not used here.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult, **0.15 kg** and **50 cm** total length, sex unspecified. Venom-yield work on 30 wild *E. c. carinatus* found no difference between males and females, and this pass found no reliable size difference between the sexes, so no sex-based modifier is applied.
- **Population notes:** the two subspecies differ sharply in venom output. Wild southern Indian *E. c. carinatus* averaged only 2.76 mg of dry venom per extraction, while north-western *E. c. sochureki* averaged about 50 mg (Gond et al. 2025). Venom composition also differs between Rajasthan and southern India, and Indian polyvalent antivenom works poorly against *E. c. sochureki*. The canonical specimen is a mid-range adult, not a large *sochureki*.

### Bite, fangs and venom
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-pressure measurement exists; the weapon is venom through hinged front fangs.
- No reliable fang-length measurement was located.
- **Yield:** wild *E. c. carinatus* 2.76 mg mean (range 0.2–8.2 mg); *E. c. sochureki* 50.0 mg mean (range 9.9–123.2 mg) (Gond et al. 2025). Older literature quoted by Wikipedia gives about 18 mg average and a 72 mg maximum, with an estimated adult human lethal dose of about 5 mg.
- **Potency:** mouse LD50 of *E. c. carinatus* venom about **0.64 mg/kg**, the least toxic of India's "big four" in the Gond et al. comparison.
- **Composition:** snake venom metalloproteinases (dominant, especially class III), C-type lectins, L-amino acid oxidases, phospholipase A2s and serine proteases (Javed et al. 2025; Kumar et al. 2026).
- **Action:** local swelling and pain within minutes, blistering, then venom-induced consumption coagulopathy, systemic bleeding and acute kidney injury. In a Jodhpur series, 103 of 105 *E. c. sochureki* patients developed consumption coagulopathy, 52% had systemic bleeding, 68% of treated patients did not respond to antivenom, and 8.6% died.

### Defensive structure
- Keeled scales with serrated keels on the flanks, set at an angle; these are used to make the warning sound, not as armor. No real physical protection.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The primary weapon is a fast strike through hinged front fangs, delivering a metalloproteinase-rich venom that breaks down clotting factors and blood-vessel walls. The secondary tool is the threat display: the snake forms parallel C-shaped coils and rubs its serrated flank scales together to make a loud "sizzling" sound, speeding up as it becomes more agitated.

### Offensive mechanics
From its figure-of-eight coil with the head in the center, the viper can lash out "like a released spring". Against rodents, lizards, frogs and arthropods the venom is ample. Against large opponents the limit is dose: a southern Indian adult may carry only a few milligrams, so its bite depends on potency and on clotting failure that develops over hours.

### Defensive features and durability
Physically this is a tiny, fragile snake of around 150 g. It relies on camouflage, hiding under rocks or in sand, the sizzling warning and a quick strike.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Mostly moves by sidewinding, which it does quickly and which keeps only two points of the body on hot sand. It also climbs readily: in rain up to 80% of adults may climb into bushes, sometimes 2 m up.

### Endurance
An ectotherm active mainly at dusk and night, most active after rain. Not built for sustained exertion; northern populations hibernate.

### Senses
Normal viper vision and chemoreception. It has no heat pits.

### Intelligence and tactics
Its defense is graded: warning sound first, faster and louder as the threat continues, then a strike. Beyond that, it shows no evidence of flexible problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Eats rodents, lizards, frogs, scorpions, centipedes and large insects, and its broad diet supports dense local populations; about 20 individuals were once seen massed on a single shrub.

### Fighting and aggression
It is widely described as irritable and quick to strike, and its small size and camouflage mean people often step close before noticing it. This combination, not venom volume, is why it causes so many bites.

### Environmental strengths
- Sand, rock, soft soil and dry scrub, where camouflage and sidewinding work best.
- Warm humid nights and the period after rain, when it is most active.
- Close encounters with barefoot or unarmored opponents.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny mass and no armor: any sizable predator can crush it.
- Small venom yield, especially in southern populations.
- Slow-acting venom: the damage comes from clotting failure over hours.
- Performance drops in cold; northern populations hibernate.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rodents, lizards, frogs and arthropods; animals that blunder into close range without seeing it.
- **Mixed:** other small venomous snakes and small carnivores.
- **Bad:** snake-eating raptors, mongooses and other snake-killers, large hoofed animals that can trample it, and anything armored.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Sizzling Scale Alarm** — the snake loops into parallel C-shaped coils and rubs its angled, serrated flank scales together to make a hissing, sizzling sound that grows faster and louder as the threat continues. The sound comes from the scales, not from breath or a rattle.
2. **Coil-Spring Hemorrhage Strike** — from a figure-of-eight coil with the head in the center, it strikes like a released spring. Its metalloproteinase-rich venom causes consumption coagulopathy and bleeding, and in north-western India it is often poorly neutralized by the available antivenom.

### Unique traits
1. **Sand-Swimming Sidewinder** — it moves mainly by fast sidewinding across hot sand, touching the ground at only two points, and can bury itself with only the head showing.
2. **Big Four's Smallest Member** — at usually under 60 cm it is the smallest of India's "big four" medically important snakes, yet it is one of them because it is common, well hidden and quick to bite.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The saw-scaled viper lives in India, Sri Lanka, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Afghanistan, Iran, southern Iraq, Oman, the United Arab Emirates and parts of Central Asia (Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Tajikistan). It uses sand, rock, soft soil and scrubland, often hiding under loose rocks, in rodent burrows, rock fissures or rotting logs, and has been recorded up to 1,982 m in Balochistan.

### Diet and ecology
It eats rodents, lizards, frogs and many arthropods, including scorpions, centipedes and large insects. The broad diet allows high local densities. It is mostly crepuscular and nocturnal, most active after rain and on humid nights, and often climbs into shrubs.

### Social structure
Solitary, though many individuals can gather in the same shrub during rain.

### Reproduction and life history
Indian populations give birth to live young. In northern India mating takes place in winter and young are born from April to August, usually 3–15 per litter (up to 23 recorded), each 115–152 mm long.

### Conservation
Listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (2021 assessment, as reported by Wikipedia). It is common across much of its range and often lives close to farms and villages.

### Special features
- Serrated keeled flank scales used for a stridulating warning sound.
- Hinged front fangs and a metalloproteinase-rich hemorrhagic venom.
- Fast sidewinding locomotion.
- Figure-of-eight defensive coil.
- Frequent climbing into bushes, especially in rain.

### Human interaction
It is one of India's "big four" snakes named in the Million Death Study as responsible for most of the country's snakebite deaths, which that study estimated at about 45,900 a year in 2001–03. A later analysis estimated about 58,000 snakebite deaths a year in India from 2000 to 2019. Bites cause severe bleeding and kidney injury; in Rajasthan, a 2019–2024 series found poor antivenom response to *E. c. sochureki* bites. Its venom has also given medicine useful molecules: echistatin (an anticlotting peptide), ecarin (the reagent in the ecarin clotting time test) and the design basis of the drug tirofiban.

### Genuine fun facts
- It warns intruders by rubbing its saw-edged scales together, making a sizzling sound like water on a hot plate.
- In rain, up to 80% of adults may climb into bushes; about 20 were once seen on one shrub.
- Southern Indian adults average under 3 mg of venom per extraction, yet the species is still one of India's big four.
- Its venom supplied ecarin, used in a hospital blood-clotting test.
- The anticlotting drug tirofiban (Aggrastat) is listed among the medicines derived from this snake's venom.
- In sand it can bury itself so that only its head shows.
- It gives birth to live young, unlike the egg-laying African carpet vipers.

### Concise site-ready summary
The saw-scaled viper is a tiny desert viper, usually under 60 cm and about 150 g, that warns by rubbing its saw-edged scales into a sizzling hiss and strikes from a spring-like coil. Its venom destroys the blood's ability to clot. It is one of India's big four medically important snakes, but its small size and small venom doses keep its absolute combat power low.

### Rich narrative profile
The saw-scaled viper is proof that a snake does not need size to matter. Most adults are under 60 cm and weigh roughly 150 g, and in southern India an adult may give only a few milligrams of venom. Yet the species ranks among India's four most medically important snakes because it is common, well camouflaged on sand and rock, active at night when people walk barefoot, and quick to defend itself.

Its warning is unusual. Instead of a rattle or a loud hiss, it arranges its body into parallel C-shaped loops and rubs the angled, saw-edged scales on its flanks together. The result is a sizzling sound that speeds up as the threat grows. If the warning fails, the viper strikes from a figure-of-eight coil with its head at the center. The venom, rich in metalloproteinases, uses up clotting factors and damages blood vessels, leading to bleeding and kidney injury over hours rather than minutes.

For battle ratings, the saw-scaled viper sits near the bottom in every physical category. It has almost no mass, no armor and very little strength, and even its venom delivery is limited by dose. Its strengths are a fast strike, quick sidewinding, readiness to fight and a warning display that is one of the most distinctive on the roster.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Venom yield:** older figures (18 mg average, 72 mg maximum) are far above the 2.76 mg wild mean for southern *E. c. carinatus*; they probably reflect larger northern or captive animals. The canonical specimen uses the modern wild data.
- **Mass:** only one aggregated figure (142 g); low-moderate confidence.
- **Lifespan:** 23.8 years is a captive maximum, not a typical wild lifespan.
- **Death toll:** the Indian mortality figures cover all snakes, and the saw-scaled viper's share was not separated.
- **Aggression:** "extremely aggressive" is a common description rather than a measured behavior score.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Rattlesnake (2.5 kg):** the rattlesnake is about 17 times heavier, has heat pits, a rattle and much larger venom doses, so it keeps clearly higher Attack, Defense, Senses and Special. Both use a sound warning, but the rattle and heat pits outrank scale stridulation, so Special stays five points lower here.
- **Versus Gaboon Viper (7 kg):** the Gaboon viper has 5 cm fangs and huge venom reserves, so its Weaponry and Special stay far higher. The saw-scaled viper is much more agile (sidewinding, fast strikes) and far more ready to fight.
- **Versus Indian Cobra (2 kg):** a fellow big four snake from the same region. The cobra's larger size and bigger doses of neurotoxic venom keep its Attack about ten points higher; the viper's quicker strike and sidewinding give it slightly higher Agility.
- **Versus Eastern Brown Snake (1 kg):** the brown snake is about seven times heavier with a more toxic venom, so it keeps higher Attack and Special; both have fragile bodies, and the viper's Defense sits lower for its much smaller size.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the snake's large share of human bites reflects how often people meet it, not combat power, and is not counted in Attack. Physical ratings stay near the floor for a 150 g snake.
