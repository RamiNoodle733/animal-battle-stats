# Binturong (*Arctictis binturong*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Binturong (also bearcat)
- **Scientific name:** *Arctictis binturong* (Raffles, 1821)
- **Exact animal represented:** the living binturong of South and Southeast Asia, a large, shaggy, tree-dwelling member of the civet family (Viverridae). It is neither a bear nor a cat.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Status:** Living; Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult **female** of about **16.0 kg**, head-body about **80 cm**, with a tail of similar length. Females are the larger and dominant sex.
- **Sex choice:** San Diego Zoo and ADW report that females are about 20% larger and heavier than males, and a study of a captive population notes that females dominate males. As with the spotted hyena on the roster, the larger dominant sex is used.
- **Population variation:** several subspecies are recognised across the range, including the Palawan bearcat. Zoo animals can become much heavier than wild ones (the Smithsonian gives up to 36 kg); those weights are not used as the baseline.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The binturong has the teeth and claws of a carnivore, with a long, sharp canine in each jaw quadrant and long claws, but it lives mostly on fruit, especially figs. It also takes birds, small mammals, eggs and occasionally fish when it finds them. Nothing in its anatomy or behavior marks it as a specialist killer, so its weapons are mainly defensive in practice.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
An arboreal specialist with a long, heavy body, short, stout legs and a prehensile tail. It climbs skillfully but slowly, moving with equal ease along the tops of branches or hanging beneath them, and seldom leaps. Its ankles turn about 180 degrees so its hind claws can grip while it climbs down trunks head-first. The last third of the tail is prehensile, with a leathery tip for grip; youngsters can hang by the tail alone, while adults use a paw as well. It usually has to climb down to cross between trees, and it swims fairly well.

### Endurance
A slow, low-energy forager. Home ranges measured by radio tracking in Thailand were about 4 km² for a female and 4.5–20.5 km² for males, and males elsewhere used 0.9–6.1 km². Digestion of its fruit diet is fast (mean retention time 6.5 hours), similar to that of fruit-eating spider monkeys. Nothing suggests high sustained exertion.

### Senses
San Diego Zoo reports good vision by day and night. Long, stiff whiskers and a strong reliance on scent communication suggest good touch and smell. No quantitative sensory data were found.

### Intelligence and tactics
Mostly solitary, with a small repertoire of calls (grunts, hisses, chuckling) and heavy use of scent marks. The tail is used for balance and communication. No problem-solving studies were located, so Intelligence is scored moderately.

### Sociality and hunting
Lives alone or in small groups of a female with her young; a male sometimes stays with a female after mating. Females in estrus call to attract mates. Foraging is mostly for fruit, moving slowly from tree to tree.

### Fighting and aggression
No detailed studies of fighting were found. San Diego Zoo notes that, as a tree dweller, it is rarely threatened by other predators, and ADW lists tigers and dholes as known predators when it comes to the ground. If cornered or badly agitated it may urinate or defecate directly onto the threat. Its sharp canines and claws and 16 kg mass make it capable of a serious defensive bite, but it prefers to avoid conflict.

### Environmental strengths
- Tall, dense tropical forest with continuous canopy.
- Branches where its prehensile tail can anchor it while it bites or claws.
- Night-time and dim conditions.

### Weaknesses
- Slow, rarely leaps, and must often descend to the ground to move between trees.
- Low stamina for intense activity.
- Not a practised fighter or predator.
- Vulnerable to tigers, dholes and other large predators on the ground.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small animals and arboreal opponents that cannot hold on in the canopy; attackers that must approach it on a branch.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized carnivores on the ground, where its mass and teeth help but its slowness hurts.
- **Bad:** big cats, dhole packs, large snakes and any fast, powerful predator catching it on open ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Fifth-Limb Tail** — the last third of its thick, muscular tail is prehensile, with a leathery tip for grip. It can anchor itself to a branch, freeing its paws, and it is one of only two carnivorans (with the kinkajou) that have such a tail.
2. **Reverse-Ankle Descent** — its ankles turn about 180 degrees, so the hind claws keep gripping when it climbs down a trunk head-first, letting it retreat or descend under control.

### Unique traits
1. **Popcorn Musk** — its urine contains 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline, the compound that gives popcorn its smell. The compound was found in every animal tested, more in males, and its level rises with the hormone androstenedione.
2. **Strangler Fig Gardener** — figs are a major food, and radio-tracked binturongs in Borneo were by far the most effective canopy dispersers of hemi-epiphytic fig seeds, depositing droppings on the branch sites where the seeds can grow.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Tropical forest in South and Southeast Asia, including India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Myanmar, Thailand, Malaysia, Laos, Cambodia, Vietnam, China, Indonesia and the Philippines (Palawan). On Palawan it uses primary and secondary lowland forest and grassland–forest mosaic up to about 400 m. It is essentially arboreal and depends on forest canopy.

### Diet and ecology
Mostly fruit, especially figs, plus leaves and shoots, eggs, birds, small mammals and occasional fish. It is an important seed disperser, especially of strangler figs, and its fast digestion is similar to that of highly fruit-eating monkeys. Because it moves seeds within the canopy, its loss may reduce fig recruitment.

### Social structure
Solitary or in small family groups of a female and her young. Females are about 20% larger than males and dominant. Both sexes scent mark with glands and urine; males produce a richer set of scent compounds.

### Reproduction and life history
Mating can happen year-round because females can delay implantation of the fertilised egg until conditions are good. Females mature at about 30 months. Young may stay with the mother until they are ready to breed.

### Conservation
Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List and listed on CITES Appendix III. ADW reports that populations have declined by more than 30% over about 30 years. The main threat is forest degradation through logging and conversion to plantations; it is also hunted for meat and skins used as food and in traditional medicine, and juveniles are captured for the pet trade and for kopi luwak coffee production. In zoos, cancers such as renal carcinoma are a leading cause of death.

### Special features
- Prehensile tail with a leathery gripping tip.
- Ankles that rotate about 180 degrees.
- Popcorn-scented urine from 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline.
- Long, coarse black coat and white whiskers.
- Delayed implantation in females.

### Human interaction
Hunted and traded in parts of its range and sometimes kept as a pet. It is a popular zoo animal, in part because of its smell and prehensile tail, and zoo records show kidney and other cancers are common in older animals. It is not considered dangerous to people when left alone.

### Genuine fun facts
- It smells like hot buttered popcorn, thanks to the same chemical found in popcorn.
- It is one of only two carnivores with a prehensile tail, and the only one outside the Americas.
- Its ankles turn 180 degrees so it can climb down trees head-first.
- Females are about 20% bigger than males and are the boss.
- Young binturongs can hang by their tails alone.
- Its gut moves fruit through in about six and a half hours.
- It is a key gardener for strangler figs in the rainforest canopy.

### Concise site-ready summary
The binturong, or bearcat, is a shaggy 16 kg tree-dweller of Asian rainforests that smells like popcorn and climbs with a grasping tail. It has sharp teeth and claws but eats mostly figs, moves slowly and avoids fights, so it relies on the canopy and its grip rather than speed or aggression.

### Rich narrative profile
The binturong looks like something assembled from spare parts: a cat-like face with stiff white whiskers, a bear-like body covered in long, shaggy black hair, and a thick tail as long as its body that can grip branches like a hand. A grown female weighs around 16 kg and is larger than the males and in charge of them.

Most of its life is spent slowly moving through the canopy of South and Southeast Asian forests, feeding on fruit, especially figs. It climbs along the tops of branches or hangs beneath them, turns its ankles around to climb down head-first, and wraps its tail around branches for security. It marks its path with scent, and its urine carries the same compound that makes popcorn smell the way it does.

In a fight the binturong is better armed than its gentle habits suggest, with long canines and sharp claws on a heavy body. But it is slow, rarely leaps and is not a practised predator, and on the ground it is vulnerable to tigers and dholes. It rates in the low range for Attack and Defense, with low Agility for a climber because of its slowness, and a solid Special score for its prehensile tail, rotating ankles and unusual scent chemistry.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** wild-based ranges top out around 20–22 kg, while the Smithsonian's 36 kg likely reflects captive animals; the canonical 16 kg is a mid-range adult female.
- **Length:** sources differ on head-body range (61–96 vs 71–84 cm); 80 cm fits all three.
- **Lifespan:** the 16–18 year wild figures are from zoo fact pages without a cited field study; low-moderate confidence.
- **Height and speed:** no reliable values; both fields are 0.0.
- **Bite:** no measurement or estimate exists.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Fossa (8 kg; A37 D24 Ag89 Sp70):** the fossa is half the binturong's mass but a skilled predator with far greater agility in trees, so it keeps higher Attack and much higher Agility. The binturong's extra mass and coat give it a higher Defense.
- **Versus Raccoon (6.5 kg; A30 D29 Ag74 I76 Sp68):** the binturong is more than twice as heavy with longer canines, so it sits slightly higher in Attack and Defense, but the raccoon is quicker, more dexterous and far more of a problem solver, so it keeps higher Agility, Stamina and Intelligence.
- **Versus Red Panda (5 kg; A24 D24 Ag77 Sp67):** both are slow-feeding tree dwellers of Asian forests, but the binturong is three times heavier, so it rates higher in Attack and Defense and lower in Agility because it is slower and rarely leaps.
- **Versus Clouded Leopard (22 kg; A49 Ag94):** a similar-weight tree-dweller that is a true predator; the binturong stays far below it in Attack and Agility.
- **Anti-inflation check:** sharp canines and claws do not make it a predator; its Attack stays in the low band because it rarely uses them.
