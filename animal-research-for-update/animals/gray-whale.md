# Gray Whale (*Eschrichtius robustus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Gray whale (also grey whale; historically "devil fish")
- **Scientific name:** *Eschrichtius robustus* Lilljeborg, 1861
- **Exact animal represented:** the gray whale species, a coastal baleen whale of the North Pacific and the only member of its genus. The canonical animal is based on the well-studied eastern North Pacific population.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult **female**, **13.0 m** long and **25,000 kg**.
- **Sex choice:** females are slightly larger than males (ADW; Wikipedia), and mothers are the individuals recorded fighting hardest, defending calves against killer whales and, historically, against whaleboats (NOAA "devil fish" nickname; ADW "fiercely defensive of their young"). The female is therefore both the larger and the primary fighting sex.
- **Population notes:** there are two North Pacific populations. The eastern population is much larger; the western population is very small and listed as endangered under the US Endangered Species Act (NOAA). Recent satellite tags show some western whales migrating to the eastern Pacific (Mate et al. 2015).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The gray whale has no teeth. Its weapons are its broad flukes, nearly 10 feet across (NOAA), its heavy body and a barnacle-crusted hide. What sets it apart from rorquals like the fin whale is behavior. NOAA notes gray whales earned the nickname "devil fish" because of their aggressive reactions when harpooned, and ADW describes mothers as fiercely defensive of their young against predators. Ford & Reeves (2008) list the gray whale among five "fight" species that use active physical defense: self-defense by single individuals, mothers defending calves and coordinated defense by groups.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Gray whales are slow. They migrate at roughly 5–10 km/h and reach about 16–17 km/h at most. Ford & Reeves (2008) describe fight species as robust, slow but relatively maneuverable, and gray whales work in water shallow enough to feed on the seafloor, rolling onto their sides to suck up sediment (NOAA; Clayton et al. 2026). This comfort in shallow coastal water is a real tactical asset.

### Endurance
Gray whales make one of the longest annual migrations of any mammal, about 10,000 miles round-trip and in some cases more than 14,000 miles (NOAA). A satellite-tagged western female traveled **22,511 km** in one round trip from Sakhalin Island to Mexico's breeding lagoons and back, at the time the longest documented mammal migration (Mate et al. 2015). They feed little on migration and rely on fat reserves (Wikipedia; ADW).

### Senses
Gray whales hear low-frequency sound well, produce a repertoire of calls, and spyhop (raise the head vertically) while scanning for predators or other whales (ADW). They do not echolocate.

### Intelligence and tactics
Mothers use shallow lagoons and coastlines to protect calves, and Ford & Reeves (2008) note fight species often calve or migrate near shallow water where it gives them a defensive advantage. In the Mexican lagoons some gray whales approach boats closely enough to be touched (ADW). They can switch between benthic, planktonic and opportunistic feeding along the migration route (Wikipedia).

### Sociality and hunting
Gray whales usually travel alone or in small, unstable groups, with larger gatherings on feeding and breeding grounds (NOAA). They are mainly bottom feeders on amphipods and other invertebrates. Tagged whales in Puget Sound pressed an average of **4.9 m²** of skull against the seafloor in each feeding roll and made **292 ± 153** feeding events a day (Clayton et al. 2026).

### Fighting and aggression
Breeding is competitive and females mate with several males (Wikipedia). Toward predators, gray whales fight rather than flee; toward whalers they were famous for smashing boats.

### Environmental strengths
- Shallow coastal water and lagoons, where killer whales lose the advantage of attacking from below.
- Long encounters, where endurance and mass favor the whale.
- Defending a calf, when the mother will commit fully.

### Weaknesses
- No teeth or cutting weapon.
- Slow; cannot outrun killer whales and must stand and fight.
- Calves are especially vulnerable during migration (Barrett-Lennard et al. 2011 studied killer whale predation on migrating gray whales at Unimak Island, Alaska).
- Population health depends on Arctic feeding; unusual mortality events have left many whales thin (Magallón Flores et al. 2024).

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** smaller predators that must survive fluke strikes from a 25-tonne body; opponents that tire before the whale.
- **Mixed:** a single orca or large shark that can wound but not easily kill a healthy adult.
- **Bad:** coordinated killer whale packs in open water, toothed giants of similar size, and any contest where speed decides.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Devil Fish Defense** — gray whale mothers fight rather than flee, driving flukes and a barnacle-crusted body against attackers, a behavior strong enough to earn the species its old whalers' name. In a battle it commits to a stand-up fight.
2. **Seafloor Suction Roll** — the whale rolls onto its side and sucks sediment and prey off the seabed, pressing about 5 m² of its head against the bottom in each roll. It lets the whale work comfortably in shallow water where many ocean predators lose their edge.

### Unique traits
1. **Record Migrator** — one tagged female swam 22,511 km in a single round trip between Russia and Mexico, at the time the longest migration documented for any mammal.
2. **Barnacle-Crusted Hide** — the skin carries dense barnacles and whale lice, giving the gray whale its mottled look and a rough outer layer that may help in defense.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Gray whales live mainly in shallow coastal waters of the North Pacific, though they cross deep water during migration (NOAA). Most eastern whales feed in summer in the northern Bering and Chukchi seas and winter in the lagoons of Baja California, Mexico, where calves are born. A small western population feeds off Sakhalin Island, Russia. Rare individuals have been sighted in the Atlantic and Mediterranean in recent years (Wikipedia).

### Diet and ecology
Gray whales are primarily bottom feeders, taking amphipods and other seafloor invertebrates by rolling on their sides and sucking up sediment, which they filter through coarse baleen, leaving mud trails and feeding pits (NOAA). They also feed on plankton and take prey opportunistically along the migration route (Wikipedia). In Puget Sound, tagged whales fed on burrowing ghost shrimp almost 300 times a day on average (Clayton et al. 2026).

### Social structure
They usually travel alone or in small, unstable groups, but gather in large numbers on feeding and breeding grounds (NOAA). Mother-calf pairs are the strongest bonds, and mothers defend calves vigorously (ADW).

### Reproduction and life history
Gray whales reach sexual maturity at 6 to 12 years, on average 8 to 9 (NOAA). Gestation lasts about 13 to 14 months, and calves are about 4.6 m long and 500–600 kg at birth, born mostly in a six-week window from early January (NOAA; ADW; Wikipedia).

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** globally on the IUCN Red List (2018 assessment, as summarized by Wikipedia). The eastern North Pacific population recovered from whaling and was removed from the US endangered list in 1994, while the western population remains endangered (NOAA). Since 2019, unusual mortality events have left many whales starving and in poor body condition (Magallón Flores et al. 2024), and Wikipedia reports a steep decline in the eastern population to about 13,000 by 2025, a figure not verified from a NOAA page in this run.

### Special features
- Seafloor suction feeding by rolling on the side.
- Coarse, short baleen suited to filtering sediment.
- Barnacle- and lice-encrusted skin.
- Broad flukes nearly 3 m across.
- One of the longest migrations of any mammal.

### Human interaction
Whalers called gray whales "devil fish" for their violent resistance when harpooned (NOAA). Today they are the focus of whale watching along the North American Pacific coast, and some individuals in the Mexican lagoons approach boats to be touched (ADW). Vessel strikes and entanglement remain threats.

### Genuine fun facts
- Whalers named gray whales "devil fish" because they fought back so fiercely when harpooned.
- A tagged female swam 22,511 km on one round trip from Russia to Mexico and back.
- Gray whales leave "feeding pits" and mud trails on the seafloor where they have fed.
- The tail flukes of a big gray whale are nearly 10 feet wide.
- Calves are born tail first and are about 4.6 m long.
- Some gray whales in Mexico's lagoons swim up to boats close enough to be touched.

### Concise site-ready summary
The gray whale is a 25-tonne, barnacle-crusted coastal baleen whale that fights instead of fleeing: whalers called it the "devil fish" for its violent resistance. Slow but tough and handy in shallow water, it holds the record for the longest documented mammal migration, though it has no teeth and relies on mass, flukes and nerve.

### Rich narrative profile
The gray whale is the bottom-feeder of the great whales. It rolls onto its side in shallow coastal water, presses its head into the seabed and sucks up mud full of amphipods and shrimp, filtering them through short, coarse baleen. That habit keeps it close to shore for much of its life, and it shapes how it fights. Where fast rorquals run from killer whales, gray whales belong to the group Ford and Reeves call "fight" species: robust, slow but maneuverable, often in shallow water, and willing to defend themselves.

Whalers learned this the hard way. Mothers in the Mexican calving lagoons smashed boats when harpooned, earning the name "devil fish". The gray whale's weapons are blunt: broad flukes nearly 3 m across, a heavy body and a hide crusted with barnacles. It cannot bite or cut, so its fights are about deterrence and endurance, and killer whales that hunt migrating calves still find healthy adults a dangerous target.

Its stamina is extraordinary. Eastern gray whales travel between the Arctic and Mexico every year, and a tagged western female swam 22,511 km in one round trip, the longest documented migration of any mammal when it was published. The result is a very durable, very persistent heavyweight with strong Defense and elite Stamina, lower Agility because it is slow, and an Attack limited by the lack of any true weapon.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** NOAA (~40.8 t), ADW (36 t) and Wikipedia (typical 15–33 t) give different framings. The canonical 25,000 kg is an editorial large-adult placement.
- **Speed:** the 16–17.5 km/h danger speed comes from a tertiary summary; the measurement method was not retrieved.
- **Lifespan:** unknown per NOAA; estimates range from 25 to 80 years. The canonical 55 years is low confidence.
- **Population trend:** the Wikipedia figure of ~13,000 in 2025 was not verified from a NOAA stock assessment in this run.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Humpback Whale (30 t; A72 D85 Ag56 St89 I78 Sp78):** both are fight species, but the humpback is heavier and has the longest flippers of any whale for striking and turning. The gray whale is therefore lower in Attack (66.0), Defense (81.0), Agility (48.0) and Intelligence, and slightly higher in Stamina (91.0) because of its record migration.
- **Versus Fin Whale (45 t; A68 D83 Ag52 St90):** the fin whale has almost twice the mass and far more speed, but flees rather than fights. The gray whale's fighting behavior narrows the Attack gap (66.0 vs 68.0) without closing it.
- **Versus Orca (5 t; A94 D78 Ag84 I99):** the orca is its main predator and is far higher in Attack, Agility and Intelligence. The gray whale's five-fold mass gives higher Size, Raw Power and Defense (81.0 vs 78.0), matching the record that orcas mostly take calves.
- **Versus Elephant Seal (3 t; A64 D76):** the gray whale is eight times heavier with similar lack of true weapons, so it sits slightly higher in Attack and Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Attack stays at 66.0 despite 25 t because the species has no bite or cutting weapon. Its slow speed keeps Agility below 50.
