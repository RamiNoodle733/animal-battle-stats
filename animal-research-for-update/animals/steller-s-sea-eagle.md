# Steller's Sea Eagle (*Haliaeetus pelagicus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Steller's Sea Eagle (also Pacific sea eagle or white-shouldered eagle)
- **Scientific name:** *Haliaeetus pelagicus* (Pallas, 1811)
- **Exact animal represented:** the living species *Haliaeetus pelagicus*, family Accipitridae, which breeds around the Sea of Okhotsk and Kamchatka in the Russian Far East and winters as far south as Hokkaido, Japan.
- **Status:** Living; Vulnerable.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **9.0 kg**, **105.0 cm** total length and about **2.2 m** wingspan. She is the heavier sex and therefore the stronger fighting form.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Animal Diversity Web gives average masses of about 9 kg for females and 6 kg for the smaller sex. Wikipedia summarizes female mass at 6.2–9.5 kg and the smaller sex at 4.9–6.8 kg.
- **Population notes:** most adults winter in Kamchatka (up to about 3,500 birds) and on Hokkaido (roughly 2,000). Breeding pairs along sea coasts are more productive than river-nesting pairs in the northern Sea of Okhotsk.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The feet and talons seize prey and the enormous arched bill tears it apart. Salmon are the base of the diet in many places, but the species also takes birds (ducks, gulls, ptarmigans, capercaillies), mammals (hares, young seals, and carnivores including sable, American mink, Arctic fox and red fox) and carrion. In the northern Sea of Okhotsk, birds dominated the diet of coastal pairs (73%) and especially pairs nesting near seabird colonies (91%). Along the Amur River, summer diet was about 80% fish, 10% birds and 5% mammals. In autumn, salmon that have died after spawning become a major food for eagles wintering on inland rivers that stay unfrozen.

This record shows a large raptor able to kill foxes and seabirds, but most of its food is fish, birds and carrion, not large mammals. Attack is therefore set below the harpy eagle's specialized mammal-killing system despite the Steller's greater mass.

### Offensive mechanics
Hunting and feeding combine several methods: taking fish, catching birds, scavenging carrion, and stealing food from other birds. Stealing fish from smaller raptors (kleptoparasitism) is a documented habit. Where food is concentrated, large numbers gather on productive salmon rivers.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
This is a heavy coastal and river-valley eagle with a very large wingspan. Its build favors carrying power and flight over open water and coast, not the tight maneuvering of forest eagles. Agility is scored well below the forest specialists.

### Endurance
Many birds move seasonally between breeding areas around the northern Sea of Okhotsk and wintering areas in Kamchatka and Hokkaido. That supports strong travel endurance, though precise tracked distances were not retrieved.

### Senses
A diurnal visual hunter over water, coast and river. No species-specific sensory measurement was located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Flexible foraging (live fish, birds, mammals, carrion and stolen food) and seasonal use of salmon runs, open rivers and fishing areas show useful behavioral flexibility. This supports good raptor-level tactics, not exceptional cognition.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Adults are solitary outside breeding but tolerate large gatherings where food is abundant. Competition at carcasses and fish concentrations, including food theft, is part of its normal behavior. No evidence of routine lethal conflict between eagles was found.

### Environmental strengths
- Cold coasts, estuaries and river valleys with salmon runs.
- Open water and shoreline where its size and reach dominate smaller raptors at food.
- Winter conditions in Kamchatka and Hokkaido, where many other raptors are absent.

### Weaknesses
- No armor; still a light-boned bird on an absolute roster.
- Heavy build and long wings reduce agility in tight spaces.
- Diet dependent on fish, especially salmon and walleye pollock, which makes it vulnerable to food shortages.
- Loses its advantage on the ground against larger terrestrial grapplers.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** fish, seabirds, waterfowl, foxes and other small-to-medium mammals, smaller raptors competing for food.
- **Mixed:** similar-mass predators such as other large eagles; outcomes depend on who strikes first.
- **Bad:** large carnivores, armored animals, large ungulates and any heavy grappler once the eagle is grounded.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Great Arched Beak** — an enormous, strongly arched yellow bill, one of the most distinctive features of any eagle, tears apart fish, birds and mammal carcasses. In a fight it adds a powerful secondary tearing weapon to the talons.
2. **Salmon-River Piracy** — Steller's sea eagles steal fish from smaller raptors and gather in large numbers at productive salmon rivers. This size-backed piracy lets it win food contests without a costly kill.

### Unique traits
1. **Heaviest Eagle** — usually the heaviest eagle in the world, with females averaging about 9 kg.
2. **Okhotsk Winter Gatherer** — outside the breeding season, up to about 3,500 birds winter on Kamchatka and around 2,000 on Hokkaido, often concentrating where fish are available.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The species breeds along coasts and river valleys around the Sea of Okhotsk, on the Kamchatka Peninsula and along the lower Amur and Sakhalin. In the northern Sea of Okhotsk, about 70% of nests were found along sea coasts and the rest along rivers. In winter many birds move to Kamchatka and south to Hokkaido, with smaller numbers reaching Korea and China.

### Diet and ecology
It is a top avian predator and scavenger of northern Pacific coasts. The diet centers on fish, especially Pacific salmon and walleye pollock, plus birds, mammals and carrion. The balance shifts by place and season: coastal pairs near seabird colonies eat mostly birds; Amur pairs eat mostly fish in summer; in Kamchatka, ducks and gulls can make up 50–70% of nestling food before salmon arrive; and dead salmon after spawning feed many wintering birds.

### Social structure
Adults are solitary and territorial when breeding but gather in large numbers where food is concentrated, especially on salmon rivers and in winter.

### Reproduction and life history
Pairs nest in tall trees along coasts and rivers, and felling of these trees is a listed threat. Long-term monitoring in the northern Sea of Okhotsk began in 1992; coastal pairs breed more successfully than river pairs. Productivity has fallen from about 1.0 fledgling per nest in the 1980s to an average of about 0.55 fledglings per occupied nest in recent years.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Vulnerable**, with a decreasing population. Published estimates put mature individuals at roughly 3,500–4,670 (about 4,600–7,000 birds in total). Threats include coastal, oil and gas, and hydroelectric development; pesticides; competition with fisheries (walleye pollock stocks have been reduced by overfishing); felling of tall nest trees; direct persecution; and lead poisoning after scavenging deer carcasses shot with lead ammunition in Japan. It is protected in Russia, Japan, South Korea and China.

### Special features
- Enormous, strongly arched yellow bill.
- Dark plumage with white shoulders, thighs, forehead and wedge-shaped white tail.
- The heaviest eagle, with a wingspan often over 2 m.
- Flexible diet of fish, birds, mammals and carrion.
- Seasonal gatherings at salmon rivers and wintering areas.

### Human interaction
In Hokkaido, winter eagle gatherings draw wildlife watchers and photographers. The same region revealed a serious threat: eagles poisoned by lead fragments in deer carcasses left by hunters, which led to lead-ammunition restrictions in parts of the wintering range. Fisheries also compete with the eagle for fish such as walleye pollock.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is usually the heaviest eagle in the world; females average about 9 kg.
- The largest recorded wingspan is about 287 cm.
- It steals fish from smaller raptors.
- Near seabird colonies, birds made up about 91% of the diet of nesting pairs.
- Mammal prey has included Arctic foxes, red foxes, sable and American mink.
- Up to about 3,500 birds may winter on Kamchatka alone.
- Lead poisoning from hunted deer carcasses in Japan led to restrictions on lead ammunition in parts of its winter range.

### Concise site-ready summary
Steller's Sea Eagle is the heaviest eagle in the world: about 9 kg in the canonical female, with a wingspan often over 2 m and an enormous arched yellow bill. It hunts salmon, seabirds and even foxes along the cold coasts of the Sea of Okhotsk and steals fish from smaller raptors. It is powerful for a bird, but its heavy build limits agility and it has no armor.

### Rich narrative profile
Steller's Sea Eagle is a heavyweight of the northern Pacific coast. Females average around 9 kg and carry a bill so large and arched it dominates the bird's silhouette. The species eats what its cold coastline provides: salmon on their spawning runs, walleye pollock, gulls, ducks and seabirds from colonies, hares, young seals, and occasionally carnivores as capable as foxes and sable.

Its combat style reflects that life. It is less a precision forest ambusher than a powerful, opportunistic coastal predator that can kill, scavenge and steal. On a salmon river its size lets it take fish from smaller raptors, and its long wings carry it between breeding coasts and wintering grounds in Kamchatka and Hokkaido.

On the absolute roster it remains a bird. Its mass gives it an edge over other eagles in food contests and a little extra durability, but it has no armor, a light skeleton and heavy, less nimble flight. Against large mammals it relies on staying out of reach. Its best matchups are over water and open coast, against prey and competitors smaller than itself.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Heaviest versus largest:** heaviest by mass, but not necessarily the longest or largest by wing surface; the Philippine and harpy eagles can exceed it on other measures.
- **Length:** 105–112 cm could not be traced to a primary morphometric source; moderate confidence.
- **Population:** estimates differ (3,500–4,500 versus 3,600–4,670 mature individuals); the Vulnerable listing and declining trend are consistent.
- **Speed:** the popular 100 mph figure is rejected; no instrumented measurement.
- **Lifespan:** 30 years is a captive-derived potential lifespan; wild longevity is unmeasured.
- **Diet shares:** figures differ strongly by site (coastal seabird colonies versus the Amur River) and should not be treated as species-wide averages.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Bald Eagle (5.3 kg; A36.0 D22.0 Ag79.0 St75.0 I65.0 Sp63.0):** a closely comparable *Haliaeetus* fish eagle. Steller's is nearly twice as heavy with a much larger bill, so Attack (43.0), Size and Defense are higher; Agility is lower because of the heavier build; Intelligence is about the same.
- **Versus Harpy Eagle (8.0 kg; A44.0):** similar mass, but the harpy's feet are specialized for killing large arboreal mammals, so Attack stays just above Steller's and Weaponry (59.0 versus 54.0) reflects that.
- **Versus Osprey (1.8 kg; A31.0 Ag87.0 Sp83.0):** both fish-eaters, but Steller's is about five times heavier and much stronger. The osprey keeps higher Agility and Special because of its specialized plunge-diving fishing technique.
- **Versus Golden Eagle (5.0 kg; A40.0):** Steller's is heavier and slightly higher on Attack, but far less agile (75.0 versus 88.0).
- **Versus Arctic Fox (4 kg; A17.0):** documented fox predation is consistent with the eagle's much higher Attack; the fox is not a meaningful threat to a healthy adult eagle in open terrain.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "heaviest eagle" is expressed in Size 20.0 and a small Defense edge, not in a large Raw Power jump. Special stays moderate because the bill and piracy are not rare mechanisms.
