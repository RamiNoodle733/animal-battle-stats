# Bengal Tiger (*Panthera tigris tigris*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Bengal tiger (also Royal Bengal tiger)
- **Scientific name:** *Panthera tigris tigris* (Linnaeus, 1758), mainland tiger populations of the Indian subcontinent
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild tiger of India, Nepal, Bhutan and Bangladesh. It is kept separate from the roster's Siberian (Amur) Tiger so that the two populations can be compared directly. The IUCN SSC Cat Specialist Group groups all mainland tigers under *P. t. tigris*; ABS uses that name here for the South Asian population only.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime adult male from a mainland population (central India, Terai), **200.0 kg**, about **285.0 cm** total nose-to-tail length and **100.0 cm** shoulder height. This sits between the central-India male mean (190 kg) and the heavier Chitwan sample (235 kg) and deliberately avoids record animals.
- **Sex choice:** strong size dimorphism. Chitwan males averaged about 235 kg against about 140 kg for females; males hold larger territories and fight rival males, so the adult male is the appropriate combat baseline.
- **Population variation:** body size differs sharply by habitat. Tigers of the Bangladesh Sundarbans mangroves are much smaller (adult females recorded around 75–80 kg) and must not be used as the representative mainland animal. Chitwan males captured in the 1970s and 1980s include the heaviest free-ranging tigers on record (two males above 270 kg); those are outliers, not the baseline.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Central India: 42 adult males averaged **190 kg** (range 167–234 kg), as compiled in the Wikipedia Bengal tiger article from field capture data.
- Chitwan National Park, Nepal: seven adult males captured in the early 1970s averaged **235 kg** (range 200–261 kg); females averaged about 140 kg (116–164 kg).
- San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance species fact sheet: male tigers across the species **100–261 kg**, females 75–177 kg.
- **Canonical:** **200.0 kg** mature mainland male.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high. The central-India series is the larger sample; Chitwan males are heavier but the sample is small.

### Height
- Mean male shoulder height about **99 cm** in the compiled Bengal data; the SDZWA fact sheet gives roughly 80–100 cm across the species.
- **Canonical:** **100.0 cm** shoulder height.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Length
- Compiled Bengal male total length about **282 cm** (range 267–312 cm) including the tail.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 285.0 cm total nose-to-tail length.** This follows the roster's Siberian Tiger report, which also records total length, so the two tigers remain directly comparable. Tail is roughly a third of the total.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Speed
- No instrumented sprint measurement of wild Bengal tigers was found in this run. Popular and zoo-oriented pages give short-burst figures in the 60–80 km/h band without method.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 16.7 m/s (60 km/h)**, the same conservative value used for the Amur tiger, retained at **low confidence** and never used as a proxy for Agility.

### Lifespan
- Wild tigers generally live about 10–15 years; captive animals live longer. Several popular summaries give shorter wild figures (8–10 years) for Bengal tigers, reflecting high mortality in conflict-prone landscapes.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 12.0 years**, a representative wild adult lifespan below the Amur report's 14.0 to reflect the heavier human-conflict mortality reported for South Asian populations.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-low.

### Bite force
- Hartstone-Rose et al. (2012) estimated felid bite forces from dissected jaw-muscle architecture, including tiger specimens. The roster's Siberian Tiger report records the modelled *Panthera tigris* canine value (about 6.9 kN). That is force, not pressure.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0** (unresolved; not an absence of bite). No defensible Bengal-specific pressure measurement with contact area exists, and force is not converted to PSI.

### Natural weapons
- **Claws:** retractile claws reported up to about **10 cm** long (ADW and zoo summaries); the SDZWA fact sheet gives 3–4 in (7.6–10.2 cm). Five digits on each forefoot including a dewclaw, four on each hindfoot.
- **Canines:** the SDZWA fact sheet gives canines of 1–2 in (2.5–5 cm) for the exposed crown, while other summaries quote 7.5–10 cm, which likely includes the root. The measurement convention differs, so no single canonical canine length is promoted.
- **Forelimbs:** massive shoulders and broad forepaws for seizing, pulling down and pinning prey.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Defense comes from body mass, dense musculature and a robust skeleton. The coat is shorter and thinner than the Amur tiger's winter pelage, so it gives even less incidental protection.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons and offensive mechanics
The Bengal tiger kills with an integrated system: a short explosive rush from cover, forelimb seizure with hooked retractile claws, and a sustained killing bite to the throat or nape. Broad forepaws and a dewclaw let the cat anchor itself to a struggling animal while the jaws find position. Karanth and Sunquist's Nagarahole studies show tigers taking the largest prey in the local guild (sambar, gaur and adult wild pig) while leopards and dholes take smaller prey, which is the clearest field evidence for its absolute killing power.

### Hunting behavior
Tigers are solitary stalk-and-ambush hunters. They use dense cover, tall grass and twilight to close distance, then commit to a short charge rather than a long chase. Radio-collar work on tigers in Thailand found that about 45% of the gaur killed there were adults, confirming that a single tiger can bring down wild cattle weighing several times its own mass, though juveniles and weakened animals remain the safer targets.

### Intraspecific fighting
Males hold territories that overlap several females and are challenged by transient males. Fights between males can cause serious or fatal injuries. Solitary life means a tiger that is badly injured has no group to fall back on.

### Defense and durability
Around 200 kg of muscle and bone gives real trauma tolerance, but tigers are injured and sometimes killed by gaur horns, wild boar tusks, porcupine quills and rival tigers. There is no armor, and penetrating wounds are the main threat.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Tigers are powerful leapers and strong swimmers; the Smithsonian notes a horizontal jump of roughly 5.5–6 m (18–20 ft). Acceleration, balance and twisting control during a grapple are excellent for a 200 kg animal. Sundarbans tigers routinely cross tidal channels, and mainland tigers use rivers and pools freely, so water is not a barrier the way it is for many cats.

### Endurance
Tigers patrol large territories and can walk many kilometres in a night, but their killing strategy is burst-based. A charge that fails is usually abandoned after a short distance. Heat is a real limit in the plains; tigers spend hot hours resting in shade or water.

### Senses
Excellent low-light vision and hearing drive stalking. Smell is used mainly for scent-marked territorial communication. The whiskers support close-range orientation during the bite.

### Intelligence and tactics
Solitary, flexible hunters that read cover, wind and prey behavior, adjust approach routes and learn from failed hunts. Tigers in human-dominated landscapes shift their activity toward night. This is strong predator cognition without the social problem-solving of great apes, cetaceans or social canids.

### Aggression and ferocity
Tigers routinely tackle dangerous prey and defend kills against leopards and dholes. They generally avoid humans, but the Sundarbans has a long record of attacks on people, which shows willingness to treat large mammals as prey under local conditions.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense forest, tall grass and mangrove give ideal ambush cover.
- Rivers, swamps and tidal channels, where tigers swim readily.
- Twilight and night conditions.

### Environmental limitations
- Open ground removes the ambush advantage.
- Midday heat limits sustained exertion.
- Steep rock and tree canopy are weaker than for the lighter leopard.

### Weaknesses
- Burst physiology: poor sustained chase capacity.
- No armor; horns, tusks and quills can cause disabling wounds.
- Solitary: an injured tiger has no support.
- Heavy reliance on a successful first strike.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** medium and large ungulates, pigs, similar or smaller predators without armor, anything that can be ambushed in cover.
- **Mixed:** very large wild cattle such as adult bull gaur, other tigers and large bears, where horns, mass or equal weapons make the outcome uncertain.
- **Bad:** megafauna (elephants, rhinos), heavily armored reptiles in water, and opponents that deny cover and force a long exchange.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Tall-Grass Ambush** — vertical striping that breaks up the outline in grass and dappled forest, padded silent feet and patient stalking let the tiger close to a few metres before a single explosive rush. In cover, the tiger usually lands the first strike.
2. **Mangrove Swimmer** — tigers swim strongly and hunt across rivers and tidal channels; Sundarbans tigers cross open water routinely. Water that stops many land predators is part of the Bengal tiger's hunting ground.

### Unique traits
1. **Wild-Cattle Killer** — the South Asian tiger is the only land predator in its range that regularly kills adult gaur and sambar alone, taking the largest prey in its predator guild.
2. **Subcontinent Stronghold** — India holds most of the world's wild tigers, with the 2022 national estimate at roughly 3,167–3,682 animals.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Bengal tigers live across India, Nepal, Bhutan and Bangladesh in tropical moist and dry deciduous forest, Terai grassland, hill forest and the Sundarbans mangroves. They need dense cover, water and a large base of hoofed prey.

### Diet and ecology
Tigers are hypercarnivores. Principal prey are sambar, chital, wild pig and gaur, with smaller mammals, livestock and occasionally other predators taken. In Nagarahole, tigers selected larger prey than co-occurring leopards and dholes, which lets the three predators share the same forest.

### Social structure
Solitary and territorial. A male's territory overlaps several females, and residents defend them against transient males. Adults meet mainly to mate or at shared kills.

### Reproduction and life history
Females give birth to litters usually of two to four cubs after roughly a three-and-a-half-month gestation. Cubs stay with their mother for about two years while learning to hunt. New resident males may kill cubs sired by previous males.

### Conservation
The tiger (*Panthera tigris*) is assessed as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List (2022, criteria A2abcd). The 2021 global estimate was roughly 3,726–5,578 tigers, with about 3,140 mature individuals, and most live in India. Main threats are poaching, prey depletion, habitat loss and fragmentation, and conflict with people.

### Special features
- Retractile claws up to about 10 cm, kept sharp inside sheaths.
- Long canines for a sustained throat or nape hold.
- Broad forepaws and dewclaws for seizing large prey.
- Striped coat that breaks up the outline in grass and forest shade.
- Strong swimming and horizontal leaping ability.

### Human interaction
Tigers are a flagship species for Indian wildlife protection and tourism. Conflict arises where tigers kill livestock or people, especially in the Sundarbans and in forest-edge villages; management focuses on protected-area design, compensation and relocation of problem animals.

### Genuine fun facts
- Chitwan National Park produced the heaviest free-ranging tigers ever recorded, including two males above 270 kg.
- Sundarbans tigresses can weigh only about 75–80 kg, less than half of a large mainland male.
- A tiger can leap roughly 5.5–6 m horizontally, according to the Smithsonian's National Zoo.
- In Thailand, radio-tracked tigers killed gaur of which about 45% were adults.
- Tigers readily swim and can cross wide rivers and tidal channels.
- India's 2022 tiger estimate put the national population at about 3,167–3,682.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Bengal tiger is the big cat of South Asia's forests, grasslands and mangroves, a roughly 200 kg ambush hunter that kills sambar, wild pig and even adult gaur alone. It wins with stealth, an explosive grapple, long claws and a crushing throat bite. It lacks armor and tires quickly in a prolonged fight.

### Rich narrative profile
The Bengal tiger is built for one decisive moment. A prime male of around 200 kg spends most of a hunt unseen, working through tall grass or forest shade until the prey is within a few bounds. Then the charge, the forelimb strike and the bite arrive almost together. Broad paws with hooked retractile claws anchor the cat to an animal that may outweigh it several times, and the long canines hold the throat or nape until the struggle ends. Field studies in southern India show that tigers take the largest prey available, above the leopards and dholes that share their forests.

That power comes with clear limits. The tiger is a sprinter, not a runner, and a failed ambush usually ends within a short distance. There is no armor under the striped coat, and the animals tigers hunt fight back with horns, tusks and weight. Gaur, wild boar and rival males all wound tigers, and a solitary cat carrying a serious injury has no pride or pack to cover for it. Heat on the plains also limits how long a tiger can keep working, which is why so much of its activity is at dawn, dusk and night.

Compared with its northern cousin in the roster, the Amur tiger, the Bengal tiger is at least as heavy in its mainland populations, carries a thinner coat, and is equally comfortable in water. In a matchup it is one of the most dangerous close-range predators on land: it should overwhelm most medium-sized animals and trouble large ones, while armor, overwhelming mass or a long open-ground fight turn the odds against it.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** Chitwan males (235 kg mean) are heavier than central-India males (190 kg). The 200.0 kg canonical sits between them.
- **Canines:** 1–2 in (SDZWA) versus 7.5–10 cm (popular summaries) reflect exposed crown versus whole tooth. No canonical value is set.
- **Speed:** no instrumented data. 16.7 m/s is a low-confidence placeholder shared with the Amur tiger.
- **Lifespan:** wild figures vary from 8 to 15 years across summaries; 12.0 is a middle value.
- **Bite:** modelled force only; PSI stays unresolved at 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Siberian Tiger (190 kg; A84.0 D61.0 Ag84.0 St62.0 I66.0 Sp61.0):** same species and weapon system. The Bengal canonical is 10 kg heavier, so Attack is 1.0 higher and Defense 0.5 higher; its thinner coat gives slightly lower Protection. Stamina is 1.0 lower because heat limits sustained work; Special is 1.0 lower because swimming competence replaces the Amur tiger's cold tolerance rather than exceeding it.
- **Versus African Lion (190 kg; A70.0 D54.0 Ag72.0):** similar mass, but the tiger is a solitary killer of larger prey with a longer, more powerful grapple, so its Attack and Agility stay well above the lion's. The lion's coalition behavior is not counted in a one-on-one rating.
- **Versus Jaguar (100 kg; A68.0 D48.0 Ag82.0 Sp75.0):** double the mass gives the tiger a large Attack and Defense lead. The jaguar keeps the higher Special for its skull-piercing bite.
- **Versus Grizzly Bear (220 kg; A66.0 D59.0):** similar mass; the tiger's specialised killing weapons put Attack well above the bear, while Defense sits close.
- **Versus Saltwater Crocodile (A92.0 D83.0):** the tiger stays clearly below in Attack and Defense.
- **Speed/agility check:** 16.7 m/s is scored only in the Speed substat.
- **Flag:** Bengal and Siberian tigers are deliberately close; a future audit could decide whether the roster needs both at nearly identical ratings.
