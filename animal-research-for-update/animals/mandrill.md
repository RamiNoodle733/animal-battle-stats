# Mandrill research report

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Common name:** Mandrill
- **Scientific name:** *Mandrillus sphinx* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact taxon represented:** living mandrill, not the closely related drill (*Mandrillus leucophaeus*).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy fully mature **adult male**, represented at **32.0 kg**. Adult males are the much larger and more heavily armed sex and are the principal direct intrasexual fighters.
- **Dimorphism:** exceptionally strong. Long-term growth work found adult males about **3.4 times female body mass** and about **1.3 times female crown-rump length**; females reach adult mass around 7 years, males around 10 years. Reviews give adult male asymptotic/average mass around 31-33 kg versus roughly 9-13 kg in females. Adult male canines average about 44-45 mm exposed crown height versus about 10 mm in females. Exceptional males up to about 54 kg are reported, but are not the canonical baseline.

## 2. Physical measurements
### Mass
- Peer-reviewed/reference synthesis: adult male *M. sphinx* about **31.2-33.0 kg**, females about **9.1-12.9 kg**.
- ADW gives male average about 25 kg and reports animals up to 54 kg; zoo references commonly give males roughly 18-45 kg depending population/individual.
- **Canonical `weight_kg`: 32.0. Confidence: high.** This tracks the stronger modern morphometric synthesis rather than a record individual.

### Length and height
- ADW reports head-body length **61.0-76.4 cm**. Guinness gives adult males 61-76 cm head-body plus a short 5.2-7.6 cm tail.
- San Diego Zoo reports males around 62-110 cm in its height/size presentation, but measurement convention is unclear enough that it should not be converted into a standardized shoulder-height field.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 72.0**, representative adult-male head-body length within the measured range. **Confidence: moderate.**
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0** because a sufficiently standardized adult-male shoulder-height dataset was not located. This avoids mixing upright, crown-rump and all-fours measures.

### Speed and locomotion
- Mandrills are diurnal, semiterrestrial digitigrade quadrupeds that also climb and sleep in trees. Wild work recorded daily paths from **0.44 to 6.50 km/day** in a roughly 867 ha home range.
- Repeated web claims of 25 mph / 40 km/h were not traced to a strong primary speed measurement.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** pending a defensible species-specific measured maximum. This does not mean the animal is motionless; it means maximum-speed evidence is inadequate.

### Lifespan
- Zoo and field summaries place normal wild lifespan around **18-20 years**. Managed-care animals can exceed 30 years, with verified exceptional individuals approaching 40.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 20.0. Confidence: moderate-high.** This represents normal wild longevity, not captivity records.

### Bite force and dentition
- No defensible species-specific bite pressure in PSI was located. The legacy 400 PSI figure is therefore rejected.
- Adult male canine crown height averages about **44-45 mm**, with reproductive success strongly associated with canine development; males sire offspring mainly when canines exceed about 30 mm in the long-term study.
- Some zoo references report canines up to about **6.35 cm**, but the peer-reviewed adult mean is the stronger canonical weapon dimension.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0**, because force/contact-area evidence is insufficient for PSI.

### Natural weapons and defenses
- **Primary weapon:** paired long maxillary canines, about 44-45 mm average adult-male crown height, backed by a robust cercopithecine jaw and powerful neck/head musculature.
- **Secondary weapons:** hands/forelimbs for grappling, pulling and striking at close range; body mass and leverage in wrestling.
- **Defense:** muscular compact body, mobility, social threat assessment and avoidance. No armor, thick hide, horns or other dedicated passive protection.

## 4. Combat biology
### Offensive mechanics
The adult male mandrill is a compact close-range primate fighter. Its decisive weapon is not generic primate strength but unusually large canine weaponry. Male canines average about 45 mm and are sufficiently consequential that male-male fights are described as potentially lethal. At contact range the animal can combine biting with forelimb grappling, pulling, positional control and rapid quadrupedal repositioning.

### Intraspecific fighting
Male mandrills form dominance relationships, but physical fighting is costly enough that avoidance, appeasement and status signals are heavily used. Setchell and Wickings found fights rare in semi-free-ranging males, yet documented that escalated fights can result in death. Similar male coloration was associated with more aggression and stand-offs, while large color differences more often produced clear submission. This is strong evidence that the species assesses fighting ability before risking its long canines.

Canine weaponry is not ornamental trivia. Long-term demographic/morphometric research found male lifetime reproductive success covaries with canine ontogeny and adult maximum canine size. Males sired offspring almost exclusively when their canines exceeded roughly 30 mm, about two-thirds of average adult size.

### Defense and toughness
A 32 kg male has meaningful muscular bulk and can deter similarly sized opponents with its teeth, but passive protection is modest. Fur and skin do not function as armor. Against large carnivores, penetrating weapons or major size disadvantages, tissue remains vulnerable. Defense therefore stays far below heavily armored animals and large megafauna.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Mandrills are semiterrestrial quadrupeds but retain strong climbing competence. Their shoulder anatomy and grasping hands permit climbing, branch use and terrestrial locomotion. Wild groups may travel several kilometers in a day. They are more maneuverable than their heavy male build suggests, especially in cluttered forest, but there is no basis for granting elite small-primate agility or substituting an unsupported top-speed number for maneuverability.

### Endurance
Wild mandrills range over large forest areas and recorded daily paths reached 6.5 km. Seasonal field research shows behavioral flexibility, with groups ranging more broadly during fruit-scarce periods. This supports solid routine locomotor endurance. It does not demonstrate cursorial pursuit specialization or extreme sustained high-output performance.

### Senses
Vision is central to status signaling, including conspicuous rank-dependent coloration and facial/gestural signals. Olfaction also matters: adult males possess a developed sternal scent gland used in social communication and marking. Hearing supports a diverse vocal repertoire. None is an extreme echolocation/electroreception-like combat sense.

### Intelligence, learning and tactics
Mandrills have sophisticated social assessment. Males read dominance/submission signals and adjust conflict escalation, while wild groups flexibly alter diet and ranging with seasonal resource availability. A published zoo observation documented a male selecting, breaking and modifying splinters and using them beneath his toenails, a rare case of tool modification for apparent hygiene in an Old World monkey. This supports high primate cognition without assuming ape-level generalized intelligence.

### Hunting and predation behavior
Mandrills are omnivores rather than specialized predators. Fruit is a major dietary component, supplemented by seeds, leaves/woody tissues, fungi, invertebrates and vertebrates. Reference syntheses report vertebrate prey including juvenile bay duikers. They forage heavily on the forest floor but also climb for food.

### Predator defense
Likely predators include leopards, crowned eagles, large snakes and humans. Adult males can respond to danger and their weaponry makes close attack costly, but avoidance, group vigilance and arboreal refuge remain important. A mandrill should not be rated as though its canines erase the size advantage of a leopard.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense tropical forest: strong, with mixed terrestrial and arboreal movement.
- Cluttered close quarters: favors grappling, short canine delivery and rapid positional changes.
- Social context: visual, vocal and scent communication support threat assessment.

### Environmental limitations
- Open pursuit: no evidence for specialized cursorial speed/endurance.
- Water/deep aquatic settings: no specialized aquatic combat morphology.
- Armored or very large opponents: canines may fail to reach vulnerable tissue effectively.

### Major weaknesses
1. No passive armor.
2. Short weapon reach compared with horns, antlers and tusks.
3. Close-range bite delivery exposes head/neck and limbs.
4. Large male body sacrifices some nimbleness relative to smaller monkeys.
5. Not a specialized predator and lacks a documented crushing bite-force measurement.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Best against:** similarly sized unarmored animals lacking equivalent close-range weapons, especially in cluttered forest where grappling and climbing matter.
- **Worst against:** large cats, heavily armored animals, long-reach horn/tusk users and much larger mammals that can absorb or prevent canine access.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings
- **Attack: 47.0** - a 32 kg male combines penetrating ~45 mm canines with grappling and potentially lethal male-male combat, but remains far below large carnivores and megafauna in absolute damage output.
- **Defense: 34.0** - muscular build, mobility and dangerous deterrent weaponry help, but passive protection is poor and deterrence is not counted as armor.
- **Agility: 76.0** - strong forest maneuverability, climbing and grasping control, tempered by the mass of a mature male and lack of measured elite sprint evidence.
- **Stamina: 60.0** - field-measured daily ranging and seasonal expansion support solid endurance without evidence for cursorial or migratory extremes.
- **Intelligence: 82.0** - sophisticated social assessment, flexible foraging/ranging and documented tool modification justify a high primate score, below the strongest ape/corvid/cetacean anchors.
- **Special: 61.0** - unusually developed male weapon/status signaling plus manipulative climbing anatomy matter, but the species lacks a singular extreme mechanism such as venom, electricity or armor.

## 7. Abilities and traits
### Special abilities, exactly two
1. **Canine Arsenal** - adult male canines average about 44-45 mm and are genuine weapons tied to male reproductive success; at grappling range they provide a high-consequence penetrating bite.
2. **Status-Signal Readout** - rank-dependent coloration, facial/gestural signals and scent communication allow males to assess rivals and often resolve dangerous contests without blind escalation, improving tactical decision-making.

### Unique traits, exactly two
1. **Extreme Male Dimorphism** - adult males reach roughly 3.4 times female body mass, making sex and maturity unusually important to any representative combat profile.
2. **Forest Hand-and-Foot Versatility** - grasping hands, mobile shoulders and mixed terrestrial/arboreal locomotion let a heavy male transition between ground fighting, climbing and positional grappling in cluttered habitat.

## 8. Expanded profile
### Habitat and range
Mandrills occupy tropical rainforest and related forest mosaics in western Central Africa south of the Sanaga River, principally Cameroon, mainland Equatorial Guinea, Gabon and southwestern Republic of the Congo. They use primary, secondary, riparian and inundated forest. Recent camera-trap work in Equatorial Guinea found occupancy associated with proximity to water and reduced near hunting camps.

### Diet and ecology
They are broad omnivores. Fruit is a major resource, with seeds, roots/woody tissues, leaves, fungi, invertebrates and small vertebrates supplementing the diet. Field studies show seasonal flexibility: when fruit is scarce, mandrills broaden foods and range more widely. By consuming and transporting fruit/seeds they contribute to forest seed dispersal.

### Social structure
Wild social organization is more complex than a simple one-male harem description. A 15-month study of a habituated wild group in Gabon found about **120 individuals**, including several natal and non-natal adult/subadult males, and no one-male units. Other populations can aggregate into very large hordes. Female kin form a stable social core while male presence can vary.

### Reproduction and life history
Females mature physically earlier than males. Longitudinal work places female adult mass around age 7, while males continue growth to roughly age 10. Gestation is about six months and single offspring are normal. Male reproductive success is strongly concentrated among animals with fully developed canine weaponry and high competitive status.

### Conservation
The species is generally assessed as **Vulnerable**. Major threats include commercial bushmeat hunting, illegal hunting pressure, logging, agricultural clearing and forest fragmentation. Zoo conservation summaries emphasize habitat loss and bushmeat trade; recent occupancy work independently finds reduced mandrill occurrence near hunting camps.

### Human interaction
Mandrills are hunted for bushmeat and may raid crops or oil-palm fruit where forest foods are scarce. Close contact also matters for zoonotic research: naturally infected mandrills have been studied for simian foamy virus and cross-species transmission. Captive animals require substantial behavioral enrichment and careful management because adult males are powerful, well-armed primates.

### Major adaptations
- Large male canine weaponry.
- Extreme sex-specific growth and body size.
- Cheek pouches for temporary food storage.
- Grasping hands and mixed terrestrial/arboreal locomotion.
- Rank-dependent facial/rump coloration and scent communication.
- Flexible omnivory in seasonal rainforest environments.

### Genuine fun facts
- Mandrills are the largest living monkeys by adult male body mass.
- Adult males are about 3.4 times the mass of adult females in longitudinal growth research.
- Male canine crowns average roughly 4.5 cm, more than four times the female average.
- Male-male fights are uncommon because signaling and avoidance often resolve contests, but escalated fights can be fatal.
- A male mandrill has been documented modifying splinters and using them beneath his toenails for apparent hygiene.
- A habituated wild Gabon group traveled as much as 6.5 km in a day and occupied a home range of roughly 867 ha.

### Concise site-ready summary
The mandrill is the world's largest living monkey and an unusually formidable close-range primate. A mature male combines a roughly 32 kg muscular body with 4.5 cm canine weapons, climbing/grappling control and sophisticated rival assessment. Its real strength is a balanced package of weaponry, maneuverability and primate tactics rather than the exaggerated speed or bite-PSI figures often repeated online.

### Rich narrative profile
A mature male mandrill is built around costly competition. Its huge size advantage over females develops slowly, with males continuing physical maturation years after females. At adulthood the male carries long canine weapons whose size is directly associated with reproductive success. Those teeth make escalated fights dangerous enough that mandrills have evolved an equally important second layer of combat biology: assessment. Color, posture, facial displays and scent help males decide whether to press, threaten, appease or retreat.

That combination matters for ABS calibration. The mandrill is much more dangerous than its old placeholder Attack score suggested, but it is not a miniature gorilla or leopard. At about 32 kg, it lacks the absolute muscular mass of great apes and the dedicated killing anatomy of a large cat. Its canines are formidable at contact range, yet reaching that range against horns, tusks, armor or a larger predator is itself the problem.

Ecologically, the species is a flexible rainforest generalist. Mandrills forage on the ground and in trees, change their diet and ranging as fruit availability shifts, travel several kilometers on active days, and maintain complex multi-individual social systems. Manipulative intelligence is not merely inferred from sociality: a published observation documents deliberate selection and modification of splinters for hygiene. The resulting combat profile is a smart, agile, heavily armed medium-small mammal whose best outcomes come from close-range precision and tactical assessment, not raw roster-leading force.

### Source conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** older/general references often give 25 kg male averages, while later scholarly syntheses cluster around 31-33 kg. Canonical 32 kg follows the stronger adult-male synthesis and remains far below exceptional 54 kg records.
- **Speed:** repeated 25 mph / 40 km/h claims lack a traced primary measurement. `speed_mps` stays 0.0 rather than manufacturing certainty.
- **Bite force:** the old 400 PSI value is unsupported. Canine dimensions are well supported, but force/contact area are not, so PSI remains 0.0.
- **Social organization:** older zoo summaries often describe harem-like troops, while direct wild study found a stable ~120-member group with several adult/subadult males. The report prioritizes direct field evidence and treats organization as population/context dependent.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes
- **Versus Japanese Macaque (11.3 kg; Attack 31.0):** Mandrill must be substantially higher in Attack because it is roughly triple the canonical mass and carries much larger male canine weapons. It is slightly lower in Agility and comparable but not superior in broad Intelligence.
- **Versus Lynx (24 kg; Attack 48.0):** Mandrill at Attack 47.0 sits just below the lynx. The mandrill is heavier and has serious canines, but the lynx is a specialized predator with claws, predatory bite delivery and kill mechanics. This prevents primate fame from overpowering functional weapon specialization.
- **Versus Leopard (60 kg; Attack 60.0):** the gap remains large. A leopard has nearly twice the mass plus dedicated predatory jaws/claws and prey-killing behavior.
- **Versus Llama (150 kg; Attack 40.0):** Mandrill can exceed Llama in Attack despite much lower mass because its 45 mm canines are more efficient damage weapons. Llama remains much larger and stronger in absolute body scale.
- **Intelligence 82.0:** below Magpie 91.0 and Macaw 86.0 controls, near but slightly below Japanese Macaque 84.0. The score reflects strong primate social cognition and tool modification without assuming ape-level general cognition.
- **Agility 76.0:** below Japanese Macaque 79.0 and well below Lynx 85.0. A mature male mandrill is versatile but heavy for a monkey.
