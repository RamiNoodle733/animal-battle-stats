# Giant Otter (*Pteronura brasiliensis*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Giant otter (also giant river otter; *ariranha* or "water jaguar" in Brazil)
- **Scientific name:** *Pteronura brasiliensis* (Gmelin, 1788)
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild giant otter of the rivers, lakes and swamps of the Amazon, Orinoco and La Plata basins and the Pantanal of South America.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **30.0 kg** and **160.0 cm** total length, inside the male ranges of 26–32 kg and 1.5–1.7 m (Wikipedia).
- **Sex choice:** males are larger: 26–32 kg and 1.5–1.7 m against 22–26 kg and 1.0–1.5 m for females. Adult males typically lead in aggressive encounters, although alpha females have also been seen guarding groups.
- **Population notes:** early reports described males up to 2.4 m long; heavy hunting probably removed such very large animals, and they are not used as the baseline. Each animal has a unique pattern of white or cream throat markings.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The giant otter is a fish specialist and an apex predator of South American rivers. In one Amazonian study, fish appeared in every scat, with cichlids and perch in 97% and characins such as piranhas in 86%. It chases prey in rapid, twisting lunges through the shallows, attacks from above or below, and swivels at the last instant to clamp prey in its jaws, with few misses. It eats about 10% of its body weight daily, about 3 kg. When fish are scarce it takes crabs, snakes, small caimans and anacondas; truly coordinated hunting may occur mainly when prey is too big for one otter, such as small green anacondas and young black caimans. Its only real weapon is the bite, backed by a strong neck and body; its claws are for holding prey, not for fighting.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Superb in water: webbed feet for slow paddling, an undulating wing-like tail for fast swimming, and quick twists and lunges. On land it is slower but mobile, patrolling campsites and crossing hundreds of metres between waters. Agility is elite in the water and ordinary on land.

### Endurance
Family groups patrol and mark their territory's campsites, usually each morning, and hunt for hours along river margins. Dry-season ranges in Ecuador were 0.45–2.79 km², with estimated wet-season ranges up to about 19.6 km². This is strong sustained activity in and around water.

### Senses
Hunts mainly by sight in daylight, and can recognize observers at great distances above water. Highly sensitive whiskers detect changes in water pressure and currents. ADW notes hearing, smell and touch help in murky water.

### Intelligence and tactics
Highly social and cooperative. Groups are centred on a dominant breeding pair, share roles in raising young, and defend territory with latrines, scent and calls. Adults recorded 22 distinct vocalization types and newborns 11 (Mumm and Knörnschild 2014), and each family has its own audio signature. Deep-water hunts can involve synchronous dives and trapping fish against the shore (ADW).

### Sociality and hunting
Family groups of three to eight are typical, occasionally up to 20 when groups feed together. It can hunt alone, in pairs or in groups; shallow-water solo hunts are often the most productive per individual.

### Fighting and aggression
Peaceful within groups but territorial. Whole groups may charge intruders, including boats with people. Fights between groups can be violent, with bites to the snout and genitals, and occasionally lethal. Adults in families have no confirmed serious predators; black and yacare caimans have been reported taking giant otters, jaguars and cougars occasionally kill them (ADW), and adults harass and drive off threats to their young.

### Environmental strengths
- Slow rivers, oxbow lakes, creeks and swamps with clear, shallow water.
- Dense bank vegetation and gently sloping banks for dens and campsites.
- Daylight hunting in clear water, where its eyesight works best.

### Weaknesses
- The bite is its only real weapon.
- No armor, and short fur gives little padding.
- Much less capable on dry land.
- Solitary animals and pups are much more vulnerable than groups.
- Electric eels, stingrays and piranhas can injure it.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** fish of all kinds, small caimans, snakes, and similar-size animals in water.
- **Mixed:** medium carnivores on the riverbank; large snakes; animals of similar mass on land.
- **Bad:** jaguars and cougars on land, large adult caimans and crocodiles, and any large predator that catches it alone.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **River Wolf Pack** — families of three to eight otters defend territory together, charge intruders as a group and can cooperate to take prey too large for one otter, such as young caimans and small anacondas. In a strict one-on-one matchup this advantage is lost.
2. **Whisker Pressure Sense** — highly sensitive whiskers track changes in water pressure and currents, helping it find and follow prey in the water alongside its sharp daytime eyesight.

### Unique traits
1. **Longest Mustelid** — at up to 1.8 m, it is the longest member of the weasel family, although the sea otter may be heavier.
2. **Loudest Otter** — adults use at least 22 distinct call types and newborns 11, and each family has its own recognizable vocal signature.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Slow-moving rivers, creeks, oxbow lakes, flooded forests and swamps in the Amazon, Orinoco and La Plata basins, the Guianas and the Brazilian Pantanal. It prefers clear water, gently sloping banks and overhanging vegetation for dens and campsites. It has vanished from much of its former range; in 2021 conservationists spotted a wild giant otter in Argentina's Bermejo River, where its presence had been uncertain.

### Diet and ecology
Almost entirely fish, especially cichlids, perch, characins (including piranhas) and catfish, supplemented with crabs, snakes, small caimans and anacondas when fish are scarce. Hunting is most successful in shallow water under 0.6 m. As the top predator of its rivers, its status reflects the health of freshwater ecosystems.

### Social structure
Extended family groups of three to eight (up to 20 when groups merge temporarily) centred on a dominant breeding pair. Older siblings help raise pups. Groups mark territory with latrines, scent and calls, and generally avoid conflict with neighbours, but fights can be serious. Some animals live as nomadic transients.

### Reproduction and life history
Pups are born in underground dens near the water, often in the dry season when fish are concentrated. Males and older siblings help raise them. Pups open their eyes in the fourth week, walk in the fifth, swim confidently at 12–14 weeks, are weaned by nine months, and leave the group after two to three years.

### Conservation
Listed as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List since 1999, with wild populations usually estimated below 5,000; it is one of the most endangered mammals in the Neotropics. Threats include decades of poaching for its velvety pelt (1,000–3,000 pelts a year left Amazonian Brazil alone in 1959–1969), habitat loss, logging, mercury in fish from gold mining, and disturbance from poorly managed tourism. A 2024 continental study found its area of occupancy shrinking, and after the 2020 Pantanal mega-fires giant otters showed the steepest decline of any species monitored in one study area (a 2026 Pantanal study).

### Special features
- Wing-like, flattened, muscular tail and webbed feet.
- Pressure-sensitive whiskers.
- Individually unique throat markings.
- Very large vocal repertoire.
- Cooperative family groups with shared roles.

### Human interaction
Heavily hunted for its velvety pelt, with poaching peaking in the 1950s and 1960s until CITES controls from 1973 cut the trade. Groups sometimes surround boats and charge them. Tourism can disturb denning and hunting. Few are kept in zoos, where poor enclosure design has historically caused high pup mortality.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is the longest member of the weasel family, reaching up to 1.8 m.
- In Brazil it is called *onça-d'água*, the "water jaguar".
- Every giant otter has its own unique pattern of pale throat markings.
- Adults use at least 22 different calls, and each family has its own vocal signature.
- It eats about 3 kg of food a day, roughly a tenth of its body weight.
- Family groups sometimes kill small caimans and anacondas.
- Scars on some animals suggest piranhas occasionally bite back.

### Concise site-ready summary
The longest member of the weasel family, the giant otter is a 30 kg river predator that lives in loud, cooperative family groups and hunts fish, snakes and even small caimans. Powerful and agile in water, it relies on a single weapon, its bite, and is much less formidable alone or on dry land.

### Rich narrative profile
The giant otter is the river wolf of South America. A big male weighs about 30 kg and stretches 1.6 m from nose to tail tip, with a thick neck, webbed feet and a flattened, wing-like tail. Families of three to eight patrol their stretch of river each morning, marking campsites and calling constantly; scientists have identified 22 distinct adult call types.

In the water it is fast and precise. It lunges and twists through the shallows, attacks from above or below and swivels at the last moment to catch fish in its jaws, rarely missing. It eats about 3 kg of fish a day and, when fish are scarce, will take crabs, snakes and small caimans. Some prey, such as young caimans and small anacondas, may require the group to work together, and whole families will charge intruders, including boats full of people.

On the absolute scale, a single giant otter is a strong mid-sized predator: heavier and better armed than river otters and sea otters, but relying on its bite alone and lacking the grappling claws of a similarly sized cat. Its group behavior and aquatic skill are real advantages, but in a one-on-one fight on land against a jaguar-class predator, it is outmatched.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Bite:** Wikipedia describes a very powerful bite supported by a sagittal crest, while skull mechanics suggest giant otter jaws favour speed over maximum force. No bite measurement exists, so no PSI is assigned and Weaponry is scored moderately.
- **Mass:** ADW's 24 kg average combines both sexes; the 30 kg canonical is a male.
- **Lifespan:** the 8-year figure is the longest documented in the wild; typical wild lifespan is not known.
- **Predators:** ADW says jaguars and pumas occasionally kill giant otters; Wikipedia treats attacks on adults in groups as rare and partly based on historical reports.
- **Speed and height:** no measurements; both 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Otter (12 kg; A35 D29 Ag91 St76 I78 Sp77):** the giant otter is two and a half times heavier with a larger bite, so it sits above in Attack (46.0) and Defense (34.0). It is slightly less agile overall (84.0) because it is heavier and weaker on land; Stamina is equal.
- **Versus Sea Otter (36 kg; A40 D37 Ag86 St79 I87 Sp89):** similar mass. The giant otter is a more aggressive predator of vertebrates, so it rates higher in Attack, while the sea otter's dense fur and heavier body keep its Defense higher and its tool use keeps its Intelligence and Special higher.
- **Versus Clouded Leopard (22 kg; A49) and Lynx (24 kg; A48):** these lighter cats carry claws for grappling and longer killing canines; the giant otter's bite-only offense keeps it a few points below them in Attack despite its greater mass.
- **Versus Honey Badger (14 kg; A42 D45):** the giant otter is heavier and a stronger biter, so it rates higher in Attack, but the honey badger's loose, tough skin keeps its Defense higher.
- **Anti-inflation check:** group mobbing and cooperative defense are scored in Tactics (82.0) and Special (75.0), not in single-animal Attack or Defense.
