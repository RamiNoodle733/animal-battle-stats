# Sydney Funnel-web Spider (*Atrax robustus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Sydney Funnel-web Spider
- **Scientific name:** *Atrax robustus* O. Pickard-Cambridge, 1877, family Atracidae (mygalomorph spiders)
- **Exact animal represented:** *A. robustus* in the narrow sense. In 2023 the Australian Museum announced that what had been treated as one species is three: *A. robustus* (Sydney), *A. montanus* (southern Sydney funnel-web) and *A. christenseni* (Newcastle funnel-web). This profile uses Sydney *A. robustus* and borrows older whole-group clinical data only where noted.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, about **2.5 cm body length**, estimated **about 2 g** live mass, with fangs about **6 mm** long.
- **Sex choice:** the male is smaller-bodied but longer-legged, and is clearly the more heavily armed sex for battle purposes. Male venom toxicity is consistently **4–6 times** higher than female venom, and in every fatal case where the spider's sex was determined, the biter was a male. Adult males also leave their burrows and wander, so they are the form that meets opponents in the open.
- **Population notes:** body length across the species runs about 1–5 cm, typically 1.5–3.5 cm, with females larger (commonly quoted as up to about 35 mm, against about 25 mm for males). Venom yield and toxicity differ sharply between the sexes, so a female canonical specimen would understate the weapon.

- **Canonical `length_cm`: 2.5**, representative adult male body length.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.** No standardized standing height exists. In the defensive rear the front of the body is lifted high, but no measured value was found.

### Speed
- Wandering males move at night in a zigzag pattern, but no running-speed measurement exists.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- Research on wild spiders reports that both sexes usually mature only in their **fifth year**, and that females can live **more than eight years**. Popular accounts claim females reach up to 20 years and males die about 6–9 months after maturing; these claims are recorded here but not used as canonical values.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 5.0** for the adult male, based on maturity in about the fifth year followed by a short adult wandering season.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Fangs and venom
- Adult male fangs are about **6 mm** long. As in other mygalomorphs, the chelicerae strike downward, parallel to the body axis, which suits stabbing into a target beneath the raised spider.
- **Venom yield (captive study):** milking gave about **0.28 mg** (females) and **0.175 mg** (males); dissection of venom glands gave about **0.25 mg** (females) and **0.81 mg** (males).
- **Lethal component:** a single peptide, δ-hexatoxin-Ar1a (formerly δ-atracotoxin), is responsible for the dangerous effects in humans. It slows sodium-channel inactivation, causing nerves to fire repeatedly. Primates, including humans, are exquisitely sensitive to it.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-pressure measurement exists; the weapon is venom injection through large fangs.

### Defensive structure
- A glossy, heavily sclerotized carapace and robust chelicerae give more structural protection than a thin-bodied araneomorph, but the abdomen is soft and the whole animal weighs only grams.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons
Large downward-striking fangs deliver venom containing δ-hexatoxin-Ar1a. The toxin keeps sodium channels open for longer, so nerves fire uncontrollably. The gene for the lethal toxin has been sequenced and is intronless. In primates this can cause severe envenoming; in other animals the effect is weaker, which makes the weapon partly target-specific.

### Offensive mechanics
When threatened, funnel-webs rear up on the hind legs and show their fangs. When they bite, they hold on tightly and often bite repeatedly. A laboratory behaviour study found that adult females made more fang movements than juveniles in response to a puff of air, so defensive fang use increases with maturity. As prey hunters, they ambush ground-active animals that contact the silk around the burrow entrance.

### Delivered effect on large animals
Thirteen human deaths have been attributed to funnel-web bites, and none since antivenom was introduced in 1981. In a systematic review of 198 potential funnel-web bites (138 definite), 77 produced severe envenoming. For *A. robustus* specifically, **17%** of identified bites were severe, compared with 63–75% for some *Hadronyche* species. Antivenom produced a complete response in **97%** of expertly identified cases. This is a genuinely dangerous weapon against primates, but most bites, even from this species, are not severe.

### Defensive features and durability
A sclerotized carapace, robust chelicerae and the upright fang display make the spider costly to handle. Physical durability is still arthropod-scale: any vertebrate strike that lands can kill it.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Funnel-webs are ground-dwelling burrowers. Adult males are longer-legged and mobile during the breeding season, moving at night and turning up in new locations on about half of daily resightings in a wild tracking study. They are not climbers or sprint specialists, so Agility is moderate.

### Endurance
Males sustain repeated nightly searches during the breeding season, even in bad weather, but they die within that season. Females are sedentary and spend their lives in a single burrow. Low-output persistence is good; high-output endurance is not.

### Senses
Eyesight is poor, as in other mygalomorphs. The spider relies on vibration, touch and chemical cues, including silk at the burrow entrance that carries vibrations from passing prey.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behaviour is largely stereotyped: burrow ambush, defensive rearing and repeated biting, plus the male mate search. The laboratory study found few differences in exploration or tolerance of other spiders across life stages, consistent with limited behavioural flexibility.

### Intraspecific interactions
Courtship is a careful approach in which the male uses a spur on his second pair of legs during mating. Females are not known to cooperate with one another and each keeps her own burrow.

### Aggression
Widely described as highly defensive. It rears up readily, holds on and bites repeatedly once it commits. This supports high Ferocity even though absolute force is tiny.

### Environmental strengths
- Moist forest floors, gardens and rock or log crevices around Sydney.
- Night conditions and damp weather, when males wander.

### Weaknesses
- Tiny absolute mass and negligible raw force.
- Poor eyesight.
- Venom far more effective on primates than on many other animals.
- Males are exposed and short-lived once they leave the burrow.

### Good matchup archetypes
- Insects and other small ground-active animals.
- Primates small enough to be seriously affected before they can crush the spider.

### Bad matchup archetypes
- Birds, reptiles and non-primate mammals that are less sensitive to the venom and much larger.
- Armoured or thick-coated animals that block the fangs.
- Opponents that attack from range or from above.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Primate-Killer Toxin** — δ-hexatoxin-Ar1a slows sodium-channel inactivation so nerves fire uncontrollably. Primates are exquisitely sensitive, which makes this one of the few spider venoms that has killed healthy adult humans, although many non-primates are far less affected.
2. **Rear-and-Grip Strike** — the spider rears onto its hind legs with fangs raised, then drives its downward-striking fangs in, holds on and bites repeatedly. This converts a small venom dose into multiple injections.

### Unique traits
1. **Deadlier Wandering Male** — the smaller male's venom is 4–6 times more toxic than the female's, and every sex-identified fatal bite came from a male. Males leave their burrows for good to search for mates at night.
2. **Silk-Lined Burrow Ambusher** — females spend their whole lives in one silk-lined burrow among logs, rocks or garden soil, using the entrance silk to detect prey and threats.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
*A. robustus* lives in the Sydney region of New South Wales, Australia, in moist forest, bushland and suburban gardens. Burrows are lined with silk and placed in sheltered, damp ground. Wandering males are the ones people meet most often, during the warmer months (November to April).

### Diet and ecology
An ambush predator of ground-active arthropods and other small animals that touch the silk around its burrow entrance. It spends daylight inside the burrow and hunts at night. A species-level diet study was not located in this research pass.

### Social structure
Solitary. Females are highly sedentary and are thought to spend their entire lives in a single burrow. Mature males abandon their burrows permanently to search for females. A wild tracking study found males moved at night, often in zigzag paths, and were in new locations on about half of daily resightings.

### Reproduction and life history
Both sexes usually mature in about their fifth year. Mating typically happens in late summer to early autumn. The male carries a mating spur on his second pair of legs. After the breeding season males die, while females can live for more than eight years and breed again.

### Conservation
No IUCN Red List assessment of *A. robustus* was found in this research pass, so its status is treated as Not Evaluated. It remains common around Sydney. The 2023 split into three species means older records need re-checking before they are applied to *A. robustus* in the narrow sense.

### Special features
- δ-hexatoxin-Ar1a, a sodium-channel toxin to which primates are exquisitely sensitive.
- Large downward-striking mygalomorph fangs, about 6 mm in adult males.
- Male venom 4–6 times more toxic than female venom.
- Silk-lined burrow with entrance silk that transmits vibration.
- Male mating spur on the second pair of legs.

### Human interaction
Thirteen deaths have been attributed to funnel-web bites, all before the 1981 introduction of antivenom, with none since. Antivenom brings a complete response in almost all confirmed cases. δ-hexatoxins are also studied for their action on sodium channels.

### Genuine fun facts
- Male venom is **4–6 times** more toxic than female venom.
- No one has died from a funnel-web bite since antivenom arrived in **1981**.
- In a review of confirmed funnel-web bites, antivenom produced a complete response in **97%** of cases.
- Tracked males moved to a new spot on about **half** of daily checks, walking in zigzags at night.
- The gene for the lethal toxin has **no introns**, a finding that made the title of the study that sequenced it.
- In 2023 scientists split the "Sydney funnel-web" into **three** species.
- Adult males carry a mating spur on their second pair of legs.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Sydney funnel-web is a burrowing Australian spider whose wandering males carry one of the few spider venoms known to kill healthy adult humans. Its δ-hexatoxin makes nerves fire uncontrollably, and it bites repeatedly from a rearing stance. At about 2 g it is physically tiny, and many non-primate animals resist its venom far better than people do.

### Rich narrative profile
The Sydney funnel-web is a spider whose danger is aimed, almost by accident, at primates. Its lethal toxin, δ-hexatoxin-Ar1a, holds sodium channels open so that nerves fire over and over. Humans and other primates are exquisitely sensitive to this; many other animals are much less so. Before antivenom arrived in 1981, thirteen deaths were attributed to funnel-web bites, and in every case where the spider was sexed, it was a male.

That detail shapes the canonical fighter. Adult females are bigger but sedentary, spending years in a silk-lined burrow. Males mature in about their fifth year, leave their burrows for good, and spend the warm months walking at night in search of females, sometimes into gardens and houses. Their venom is several times more toxic than the female's, and their fangs, about 6 mm long, strike downward like picks. When cornered the spider rears up, shows its fangs, and once it bites, it holds on and bites again.

On an absolute roster the funnel-web is still a 2 g animal. It cannot absorb a blow, has poor eyesight and is slow compared with hunting spiders. Most confirmed bites of this species were not severe, and against birds, reptiles or non-primate mammals its venom is far less decisive. The profile therefore pairs a very high Special with low Attack and minimal Defense: a specialist weapon whose lethality depends heavily on who it bites.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** no weighed series found. The 2 g canonical value is a labelled scaling estimate.
- **Lifespan:** research sources support maturity in about the fifth year and female survival beyond eight years; 20-year female claims are popular and unverified.
- **Venom yield:** milking and dissection give different sex rankings (female higher by milking, male higher by dissection). Toxicity per unit, not yield, is what makes males more dangerous.
- **Taxonomy:** older clinical and toxicology data predate the 2023 split and may include *A. montanus* or *A. christenseni*.
- **Bite force:** no PSI exists and none is proposed.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Black Widow (Attack 24.0, Defense 2.0, Special 92.0):** both are tiny spiders whose threat is venom. The funnel-web's larger fangs, repeated biting and a toxin that has killed healthy adults justify a slightly higher Attack (25.0) and Special (95.0). Its sturdier mygalomorph carapace gives a slightly higher Defense (6.0).
- **Versus Goliath Birdeater (Attack 16.0, Defense 12.0, Agility 61.0, Special 90.0):** the Goliath is a far heavier mygalomorph with urticating hairs, so it keeps higher Size, Raw Power and Defense. The funnel-web's venom is much more dangerous to large primates, so it gets the higher Attack and Special.
- **Versus Brazilian Wandering Spider (Attack 27.0, Agility 72.0, Special 93.0):** the wandering spider is heavier, faster and hunts actively, so it keeps higher Attack and Agility. The funnel-web's much higher severe-bite rate in humans (17% for *A. robustus* against about 0.5% for *Phoneutria*) gives it the higher Special.
- **Versus Bullet Ant and Deathstalker Scorpion (Special 96.0):** the funnel-web sits just below them in Special because its lethality is primate-specific and fully reversed by antivenom, and far above them in no physical category.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Raw Power 1.8 and Defense 6.0 keep the vast gap to every vertebrate predator. Venom lethality drives Weaponry and Special only, not Size, Protection or Toughness.
