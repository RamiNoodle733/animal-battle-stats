# Llama

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Roster name:** Llama
- **Scientific name:** *Lama glama* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** domestic llama, the large domesticated South American camelid descended primarily from guanaco ancestry.
- **Status:** living, domesticated. A wild IUCN status should not be presented as though *L. glama* were a natural wild population.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy fully mature **intact male, 150.0 kg**. Adult males are the primary dominance fighters and use biting, neck wrestling, chest contact, kicking and displacement behavior. The mass is near the upper end of ADW's 130-155 kg range without using exceptional 200+ kg domestic individuals.
- **Dimorphism:** ADW describes the sexes as broadly alike in gross body size, so male selection is based chiefly on fighting role and male combat dentition rather than a large size advantage.
- **Population/breed variation:** artificial selection, husbandry, fleece type and local breeding programs produce considerable domestic variation. Zoo and husbandry references span roughly 110-200+ kg. Values in this report are therefore a representative combat-specimen control, not a claim that all llamas converge on one morphology.
- **Domestication:** genomic work confirms llama domestication from guanaco ancestry. Modern llamas are domestic animals rather than a surviving wild species.

## 3. Weapons, defenses and combat mechanics
### Primary weapons
An intact adult male's most specialized close-combat tools are the anterior fighting teeth/canines used during camelid dominance conflict. ADW describes breeding males wrestling rivals down by biting limbs while wrapping the long neck around the opponent. These teeth can lacerate, but the llama is still a 150 kg herbivore without the jaw architecture of a large carnivore.

### Secondary weapons
Llamas can kick, rush, body-check, chest-bump, shove and use their long neck in wrestling. Guard-llama literature documents running at and kicking canid threats. Spitting is a social and defensive deterrent that can disrupt an opponent's face and attention, but it is not treated as serious tissue-damage output.

### Offensive mechanics
The best llama attack sequence is not predatory. A male closes upright, uses body/neck leverage to unbalance a rival, bites exposed limbs or neck, and may kick when positioning permits. Against a smaller canid, size, forward pressure and kicks can be meaningful. Against a similarly sized dedicated predator, however, the llama lacks claws, a crushing bite or horns and is disadvantaged if seized at the throat or hindquarters.

### Defense and durability
A 150 kg body, dense coat, long legs and alert stance provide some passive survivability, but fleece is insulation rather than armor. The neck is long and exposed. Padded feet and long limbs help maintain mobility on broken ground. Defense therefore comes mainly from early detection, distance, intimidation and active kicking rather than damage absorption.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The llama is a mountain-adapted pack camelid with padded two-toed feet and good control on uneven terrain. It is less agile than a wolf-sized predator or cat in rapid direction changes, but more terrain-capable than its tall domestic appearance suggests. No unsupported top-speed figure is used as a substitute for Agility.

### Endurance and stamina
This is one of the llama's strongest conventional attributes. ZooTampa reports loads up to about 60 kg and travel up to 30 km per day; High Park Zoo reports a large llama can carry roughly 25-30% of body mass and walk 25-30 km per day. Peer-reviewed physiology also documents high-altitude hypoxia adaptation. These facts support strong sustained low-to-moderate output, not elite pursuit-running stamina.

### Senses
Side-set eyes provide broad visual coverage, while hearing and herd alarm calls support threat detection. Southwick's Zoo notes panoramic vision and multiple alarm/communication signals. No evidence supports an extreme specialist sensory score.

### Intelligence and tactics
Llamas maintain dominance relationships, use graded social signals, learn husbandry routines and show context-dependent responses to predators. Their most battle-relevant cognition is threat assessment and guard behavior. In the 1998 Applied Animal Behaviour Science study, leadership, alertness and weight correlated with aggressiveness toward a surrogate canid threat.

### Fighting and social behavior
ADW describes polygynous groups led by a male that aggressively defends his breeding position. Male takeover contests include neck wrestling and limb biting until one opponent is forced down/submits. Zoo sources also describe chest ramming and spitting during rank disputes. This makes the intact male substantially more combat-relevant than a stereotypically docile pet llama.

### Predator defense and guarding
Guard llamas have been deliberately used with sheep. A 145-producer Iowa State-associated survey reported sheep/lamb losses falling from 26 head per year before guard llamas to 8 afterward, although that observational result is husbandry evidence, not proof that a llama physically defeats every predator. Experimental behavioral work confirms measurable llama aggression toward dogs and identifies alertness/leadership as useful predictors.

## 4. Environment, strengths, weaknesses and matchup archetypes
**Strong environments:** open uplands, dry grassland, Andean-style broken terrain, pasture and long-distance travel contexts where visibility and footing matter.

**Environmental advantages:** padded feet, high-altitude physiology, tolerance of sparse forage, strong walking endurance and elevated head position for scanning.

**Environmental limitations:** dense cover can reduce early warning and give ambush predators better access. Deep water removes much of the llama's terrestrial footing advantage. Tight spaces can also prevent the long-legged animal from disengaging or using kicks effectively.

**Major weaknesses:** no armor, exposed neck, modest cutting dentition relative to carnivores, no horn/tusk system, limited grappling control once a predator gets inside its kicking envelope, and no evidence for exceptional sprint performance.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** substantially smaller terrestrial opponents that can be deterred by size, rushes and kicks; small canids in open terrain.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized non-specialist herbivores where body mass, balance and male fighting experience matter.
- **Bad:** large cats, wolves in coordinated groups, bears, large suids/bovids with stronger penetrating weapons, and megafauna that overwhelm its mass and reach.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings
- **Attack: 40.0** - a 150 kg intact male can kick, rush, wrestle and cut with fighting teeth, but lacks high-lethality predator jaws, claws, horns or tusks.
- **Defense: 36.0** - size, vigilance and active kicking help, while lack of armor and an exposed neck limit direct survivability.
- **Agility: 60.0** - competent mountain-footed control and evasive movement, not a top-speed proxy.
- **Stamina: 77.0** - strong pack-travel endurance and high-altitude physiology justify a high score, slightly below staged guanaco's more strongly wild/migratory 78.0.
- **Intelligence: 60.0** - social rank, learned handling, alarm communication and predator-directed behavior support competent tactical cognition.
- **Special: 54.0** - high-altitude physiology and guardian alarm/confrontation behavior are meaningful but not rare lethal systems such as venom or electricity.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities
1. **Guardian Rush** - llamas used as livestock guardians can detect, alarm at, approach and sometimes kick or chase canid threats. The ability represents documented predator-directed behavior, not a claim of guaranteed victory over coyotes or wolves.
2. **Altiplano Oxygen Economy** - South American camelid blood and cardiopulmonary physiology support useful function under chronic hypoxia, preserving sustained activity at elevations that impose greater physiological stress on many lowland mammals.

## 8. Exactly two unique traits
1. **Fighting-Tooth Clinch** - intact males combine limb/neck biting with long-neck wrestling during dominance contests, giving a normally herbivorous camelid a genuine close-contact male combat system.
2. **Pack-Mountain Specialist** - centuries of domestic use selected and exploited a body capable of carrying substantial loads for long daily distances over high, uneven Andean terrain.

## 9. Ecology and expanded profile
### Habitat and range
The llama is domesticated and has no natural wild range in the modern sense. Its cultural and biological center is the South American Andes, especially high plateaus and mountain pastoral systems of Peru, Bolivia, Chile and Argentina, and it is now kept worldwide.

### Diet and ecology
Llamas are herbivorous grazers/browsers consuming grasses, shrubs and other vegetation. As domestic stock, diet and ecological impact depend heavily on management. Their padded feet are less mechanically destructive than hard hooves on some substrates.

### Social structure
They are gregarious. ADW describes groups of up to about 20 and a polygynous breeding structure in which a male can defend a group of females. Dominance is dynamic and maintained through postures, spitting, displacement and sometimes physical fights.

### Reproduction and life history
Females are induced ovulators. ADW reports gestation around 360 days, usually one cria, weaning at roughly 3-5 months and sexual maturity around two to three years. Crias are precocial and can run within about an hour after birth.

### Conservation
Because the llama is a domestic form, conventional wild-species conservation categories are not the most informative framing. Conservation relevance lies in domestic genetic diversity, traditional Andean pastoral systems and its relationship to wild guanaco ancestry.

### Adaptations
- high-altitude hypoxia tolerance
- padded two-toed feet for uneven terrain
- dense insulating fleece
- efficient herbivory on coarse forage
- long-distance pack-walking ability
- broad visual field and alarm communication
- male fighting teeth and dominance behavior

### Human interaction
Llamas have been used for transport, fiber, meat, hides and dung fuel for millennia and remain culturally important in Andean communities. Outside South America they are also kept as companion, show, pack and guard animals. Poor socialization can produce dangerous human-directed aggression, particularly in males, so domestication should not be equated with zero combat risk.

### Genuine fun facts
- Genomic evidence confirms that llamas were domesticated primarily from guanaco ancestry.
- A large llama can carry roughly one-quarter to nearly one-third of its own mass for long daily walking distances under pack conditions.
- Females are induced ovulators rather than following a conventional fixed estrous cycle.
- A newborn cria can be mobile very soon after birth.
- Male dominance contests can involve neck wrestling and biting an opponent's limbs until submission.
- Llamas have been deployed as nonlethal livestock guardians, especially against canid threats.
- Their padded feet end in nails rather than conventional hard hooves.

### Concise site summary
The Llama is a 150 kg domestic Andean camelid whose battle profile is built around size, kicks, male fighting teeth, mountain footing and sustained high-altitude work. It can confront smaller canid threats and wrestle rivals, but its lack of armor and dedicated killing weapons keeps Attack and Defense well below comparably sized specialist predators.

### Rich narrative profile
A mature male llama is more formidable than its domestic reputation suggests, but for very specific reasons. It is a tall 150 kg camelid with excellent visibility, padded feet, long-distance pack endurance and a real male dominance system. Rival males do not merely spit. They can close, wrap and lever with the neck, bite limbs and push an opponent down. Against a smaller approaching canid, the same height, vigilance and willingness to rush or kick can make the llama a useful guardian.

That does not turn the llama into a large predator. Its fighting teeth are limited cutting weapons, fleece is not armor, and the long neck creates a vulnerable target. Once a large cat or coordinated canids gain secure contact, the llama has fewer close-range answers than a horned bovid or clawed carnivore. ABS therefore gives it meaningful but moderate Attack and Defense while preserving a much stronger Stamina profile.

Its most distinctive physiology is environmental rather than lethal. Llamas descend from animals adapted to the high Andes, and peer-reviewed work documents mechanisms supporting life under chronic hypoxia. Combined with centuries of pack use, this makes sustained work in thin air a genuine strength. The rating system keeps that advantage in Stamina and Special without double-counting it as raw physical power.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** institutional ranges differ because llamas are domestic and breed/husbandry variation is large. 150 kg is a robust representative male, not a species-wide mean.
- **Length:** sources differ substantially in measurement convention. 160 cm is a conservative head-body canonical value.
- **Speed:** tertiary sources repeat 40-45 km/h, but no strong measured maximum was recovered. `speed_mps` remains 0.0.
- **Bite:** no standardized pressure measurement was recovered. Fighting teeth are documented behaviorally but do not justify PSI.
- **Conservation:** llama is domesticated. Treating it as a normal wild IUCN Least Concern species would be misleading.
- **Guarding:** reduced flock losses and aggression toward dogs support real guardian behavior, but they do not imply that a llama reliably wins direct fights against large predators.

## 12. Cross-animal normalization notes
- **Versus Alpaca:** staged Alpaca is 65 kg. Llama must be substantially higher in Size, Raw Power, Attack and Defense because the canonical llama is more than twice the mass, while both share camelid high-altitude adaptations and male fighting teeth.
- **Versus Guanaco:** staged Guanaco is 120 kg with Attack 38.0 / Defense 35.0 / Agility 63.0 / Stamina 78.0 / Intelligence 58.0 / Special 45.0. Llama at 150 kg receives a small Attack/Defense advantage, slightly lower Agility and nearly equal Stamina. Domestication and guardian use justify somewhat higher tactical/special treatment, but not a huge gap from its wild ancestor.
- **Versus Kudu:** staged Kudu is 230 kg with Attack 58.0. The llama remains far below because kudu's enormous spiral horns provide much greater penetrating reach and damage despite only a moderate mass difference.
- **Versus Kangaroo:** staged 80 kg red kangaroo Attack 52.0. Llama is heavier but has less specialized high-damage striking anatomy, so its Attack remains lower. This prevents size alone from becoming Attack.
- **Stamina:** 77.0 represents pack travel and altitude adaptation, not sprint persistence. It is deliberately strong without approaching transoceanic or extreme-migration roster ceilings.
- **Special:** 54.0 stays moderate because altitude tolerance and guardian behavior are useful but not a rare lethal mechanism.
