# Portuguese Man o' War (*Physalia physalis*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Portuguese man o' war (also man-of-war, bluebottle in some regions)
- **Scientific name:** *Physalia physalis* (Linnaeus, 1758), family Physaliidae, order Siphonophorae, class Hydrozoa, phylum Cnidaria
- **Exact animal represented:** the Atlantic Portuguese man o' war *Physalia physalis*. It is not a true jellyfish: NOAA and Animal Diversity Web describe it as a siphonophore, a colony of genetically identical zooids (polyps and medusoid units) with different jobs that work together as one organism. Pacific "bluebottle" forms sometimes filed as *Physalia utriculus* are not used to set the canonical values.
- **Living/extinct:** Living
- **Canonical combat specimen:** a mature, healthy colony with a float about **20 cm** long and fishing tentacles extended to about **10 m**, the NOAA average. Sex is not used as a selector because it does not change colony size or weapon reach in any documented way.
- **Population notes:** float colour ranges from blue to violet and pink. Colonies come in left-handed and right-handed mirror-image forms that drift at opposite angles to the wind. Individuals are often stranded in large numbers after onshore winds.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- **No reliable measurement.** No published wet mass for whole colonies was found in this pass (a targeted biomass search returned nothing usable).
- **Canonical `weight_kg`: 0.2** — an editorial order-of-magnitude placeholder for the soft tissue of a colony with a float around 20 cm, entered only because the site needs a nonzero mass to place the animal. It is not a measurement and should be replaced if a real value is found.
- **Confidence:** Very low.

### Height
- NOAA: the float rises **up to 6 in (about 15 cm)** above the waterline.
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 15.0**, used as the float-height field because a standing height does not exist for a drifting colony.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Length
- Float: up to about **30 cm (12 in)** long and **13 cm (5 in)** wide (NOAA / Wikipedia summaries; the Nature Scientific Reports morphology paper also describes a float of up to roughly 30 cm).
- Tentacles: NOAA gives an **average of 10 m (30 ft)**, extending to as much as **30 m (100 ft)**. Animal Diversity Web gives typically about 10 m, over 30 m at most. Some popular pages cite 165 ft (about 50 m); this is not supported by the institutional sources and is rejected.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 1000.0**, the average extended fishing-tentacle reach, following the box jellyfish report's convention of recording tentacle reach rather than bell or float size. The float itself is recorded separately (20 cm canonical, 30 cm maximum).
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high for reach; float length is the better "body size" figure.

### Speed
- The colony has no swimming ability. It moves by wind acting on the float as a sail, with the trailing tentacles acting as a drag anchor (Iosilevskii & Weihs, hydrodynamics of sailing).
- Drift studies report a drift speed of about **1.7% of wind speed**, and a drift angle near **40°** from the wind in light winds that narrows toward straight downwind as the wind strengthens; left-handed and right-handed forms drift to opposite sides (Ferrer et al. 2024 lab replicas; Ocean Science 2021 drifting-dynamics study). These are wind-dependent drift values, not the animal's own speed.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no self-powered locomotion to measure).

### Lifespan
- A typical lifespan of about **1 year** appears in tertiary summaries (Wikipedia-style sources); no primary field study of colony longevity was found.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 1.0**, low confidence.

### Bite, tentacles and venom
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** There is no bite; prey is stung and drawn up to feeding polyps (gastrozooids).
- **Weapon system:** the tentacle-bearing zooids (dactylozooids) carry huge numbers of nematocysts, tiny coiled stinging capsules that fire and inject venom on contact.
- **Venom:** physalitoxin, a large glycoprotein making up about **28%** of nematocyst venom protein, is haemolytic and was lethal to mice (PubMed 6111356). A systematic review in International Maritime Health covers human toxicity. Most human stings cause severe burning pain, red welts and sometimes nausea, muscle pain or headache; cardiovascular collapse and death are rare but documented, including a fatal case on the Florida Atlantic coast in 1987 (PubMed 2564268).

### Defensive structure
- No armour of any kind. The float is a thin gas-filled bag and the rest of the colony is soft tissue. Protection comes from the stinging tentacles and from the ability to deflate the float and briefly submerge when attacked at the surface.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary and secondary weapons
The only weapon is the tentacle curtain. Fishing tentacles averaging 10 m hang below the float and are loaded with nematocysts that fire on contact. After a strike, the tentacles contract and lift prey up to the gastrozooids, which digest it. There is no secondary weapon: no jaws, no beak, no spines.

### Offensive mechanics
Offense is entirely passive. The colony cannot approach, chase or aim. Anything that swims into or brushes the tentacles is stung, often across a long stretch of skin, because a single tentacle can wrap along a limb or body. Small fish are paralysed and killed. Large animals, including people, suffer intense pain and welts; systemic effects are uncommon and deaths are rare.

### Defensive features and durability
Soft and fragile, but contact with any part of the colony is costly for a predator without special protection. It can deflate its float and sink briefly to escape a surface attack. Its colonial build means the loss of some tentacles or zooids does not end the organism, yet it has no structural protection against a bite or crush.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It does not swim. Wind pushes the float like a sail and the tentacles act as a drag anchor, so the colony drifts slowly at an angle to the wind. Handedness decides which side of the wind it drifts toward. It cannot steer in any meaningful combat sense.

### Endurance
Because drifting is powered by wind, the colony can travel across ocean basins with little muscular effort. Its stinging supply is spread across thousands of nematocysts, so repeated contacts keep stinging. Neither counts as high-output stamina.

### Senses
No eyes and no brain. Nematocysts fire in response to contact and chemical cues, and the colony coordinates through a nerve net. It does not detect or track an opponent at a distance.

### Intelligence and tactics
No evidence of learning or tactical choice. Its "strategy" is a fixed one: drift with a wide stinging curtain and eat what gets caught.

### Sociality and hunting
Each colony is a single organism made of many zooids. Colonies are often found in large numbers together because the same winds collect them, and NOAA notes groups of 1,000 or more. There is no cooperative hunting between colonies.

### Fighting and aggression
None in the active sense. It never initiates a fight; it stings whatever touches it.

### Environmental strengths
- Open ocean surface in warm and temperate waters, where prey swims into its long curtain.
- Any matchup where the opponent must bite, grab or swim through it.
- Crowded surf zones where many colonies strand at once.

### Weaknesses
- No mobility of its own; it cannot pursue or escape except by sinking briefly.
- Soft body with no armour.
- Venom is rarely lethal to large animals.
- Several predators ignore or exploit the stings: loggerhead turtles (skin too thick for the stings), ocean sunfish, the blue sea slug *Glaucus atlanticus* and blanket octopuses.
- Useless on land; stranded colonies can still sting but cannot act.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small fish and other small swimmers; predators with thin skin that bite or grab first.
- **Mixed:** medium marine animals that brush the tentacles but can retreat before a heavy dose.
- **Bad:** thick-skinned or sting-tolerant predators (loggerhead turtles, ocean sunfish), specialist predators such as *Glaucus* and blanket octopuses, anything that attacks from above or out of the water, and every land-based fight.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Ten-Meter Sting Curtain** — fishing tentacles averaging 10 m (up to 30 m) carry vast numbers of nematocysts that fire venom on contact, then contract to haul prey up to the feeding polyps. Any opponent that swims into, bites or grabs the colony takes stings along a long stretch of skin.
2. **Deflate and Dive** — the gas-filled float can be deflated so the colony sinks briefly below the surface, a simple escape from attacks at the surface.

### Unique traits
1. **Colony, Not a Jellyfish** — each man o' war is a siphonophore colony of genetically identical zooids with separate jobs (float, stinging tentacles, feeding polyps, reproductive units) that function together as one organism.
2. **Living Sailboat** — the float acts as a sail and the tentacles as a drag anchor, so the colony travels by wind alone; left-handed and right-handed forms drift to opposite sides of the wind, and the float gas can contain up to about 13% carbon monoxide produced by a gas gland.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
It lives at the surface of the open ocean, mainly in the Atlantic Ocean and the Indian Ocean in warm and temperate waters, and is regularly blown onto coasts. It has also appeared in the Mediterranean, where scientists have asked whether it is an occasional visitor or an established presence. Strandings are strongly driven by wind direction and strength.

### Diet and ecology
It feeds mainly on small fish and fish larvae, plus other small drifting animals caught by the tentacles. It is itself eaten by loggerhead sea turtles, ocean sunfish, the blue sea slug *Glaucus atlanticus* (which stores the unfired stinging cells in its own body for defense) and blanket octopuses (which tear off tentacles and carry them as weapons). The man-of-war fish *Nomeus gronovii* is partly resistant to the stings and lives among the tentacles.

### Social structure
Not social in the usual sense. Each colony is one organism made of many zooids. Large numbers can drift together because wind gathers them, sometimes 1,000 or more according to NOAA.

### Reproduction and life history
Colonies develop from a single fertilised egg; new zooids are budded off during growth, as described in detail in the Nature Scientific Reports study of its morphology and development. Specialised reproductive zooids release gametes into the water.

### Conservation
Not Evaluated: no IUCN Red List assessment was located in this pass. The species is widespread and abundant, with frequent mass strandings.

### Special features
- Gas-filled float (pneumatophore) up to about 30 cm long that works as a sail.
- Gas gland that produces carbon monoxide inside the float.
- Fishing tentacles averaging 10 m, up to 30 m.
- Nematocyst venom with physalitoxin as its main haemolytic toxin.
- Left-handed and right-handed mirror-image forms.
- Ability to deflate the float and sink briefly.

### Human interaction
Stings are a common beach hazard in warm Atlantic waters and cause severe burning pain and red welts; systemic symptoms occur in some cases, and rare deaths have been reported. Stranded colonies can still sting. Medical sources note that dilute acetic acid (vinegar), proteolytic meat tenderiser or baking soda have been used to inactivate the venom, and outbreaks of stings have been documented on the coasts of Brazil and France.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is not one animal in the usual sense but a colony of genetically identical zooids working as one.
- Its fishing tentacles average about 10 m and can reach 30 m.
- It has no engine: the float is a sail, and the tentacles act as a drag anchor.
- Colonies come in left-handed and right-handed forms that drift to opposite sides of the wind.
- The float gas can contain up to about 13% carbon monoxide, made by the animal itself.
- The blue sea slug *Glaucus atlanticus* eats it and reuses its unfired stinging cells for its own defense.
- Blanket octopuses rip off its tentacles and wield them as weapons.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Portuguese man o' war is not a jellyfish but a floating colony of specialised zooids that sails on the wind under a gas-filled float. Its stinging tentacles average about 10 m and can reach 30 m, making contact extremely painful and occasionally dangerous. It cannot swim, see or chase, so it wins only when an opponent blunders into its curtain.

### Rich narrative profile
The Portuguese man o' war is one of the ocean's strangest fighters. What looks like a single jellyfish is a colony of genetically identical zooids, each with a job: one forms the float, some carry stinging tentacles, others digest prey or reproduce. Together they behave as one organism, drifting on the sea surface under a translucent blue or violet sail.

Its weapon is reach. Fishing tentacles average about 10 m and can trail as far as 30 m, and each carries huge numbers of nematocysts, tiny capsules that fire venom on contact. Small fish that swim into the curtain are paralysed and hauled up to the feeding polyps. For larger animals, including people, a brush with the tentacles means severe burning pain and welts; deaths have been recorded but are rare. On the absolute battle scale, that makes it a formidable passive trap rather than a killer of large animals.

Everything else is a weakness. It cannot swim, steer, see or think, and its body is soft. Its only escape trick is to deflate the float and sink for a while. Loggerhead turtles eat it through thick skin, ocean sunfish take it, blanket octopuses tear off its tentacles to use as weapons, and a small blue sea slug even recycles its stinging cells. The man o' war scores very high in Special and Weaponry, but in Agility and Intelligence it sits at the bottom of the roster.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** no measurement exists in the sources found; 0.2 kg is a placeholder, not data.
- **Tentacle length:** 10 m average and 30 m maximum are consistent across NOAA and ADW; popular 50 m (165 ft) claims are rejected.
- **Lifespan:** the "about one year" figure is tertiary and should be treated as provisional.
- **Drift speed:** the 1.7% figure comes from a search summary of drift studies and depends on wind and colony shape.
- **Venom lethality:** deaths are documented but rare; most stings are painful rather than dangerous.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Box Jellyfish (A 56.0, D 10.0, Ag 55.0, St 45.0, I 24.0, Sp 98.0):** both are passive-contact cnidarian venom specialists. The man o' war has far longer reach (10 m versus about 3 m), but *Chironex* venom is much more often lethal to people, and the box jellyfish swims actively and steers with 24 eyes. So the man o' war is lower in Attack (38.0), Agility (6.0), Intelligence (5.0) and Special (90.0), and slightly higher in Defense (13.0) because the colony survives losing parts and its sting curtain is larger.
- **Versus Stonefish (A 27.0, D 50.0, Sp 95.0):** both hurt what touches them. The man o' war's tentacles actively capture prey across a huge zone, so its Attack is higher; the stonefish's squat body and spine-syringe venom make it a far better defender, so its Defense stays much higher.
- **Versus Octopus / Blue-Ringed Octopus:** octopuses have brains, eyes, camouflage and active hunting; the man o' war must stay far below them in Agility, Intelligence and Tactics. Blanket octopuses are a direct bad matchup because they use man o' war tentacles as weapons.
- **Versus Bullet Ant (Sp 96.0) and Black Widow (Sp 92.0):** the man o' war's Special of 90.0 sits just below them because its venom is less often dangerous per contact, even though its delivery zone is far larger.
- **Anti-inflation check:** tentacle length is recorded as reach, not body size; Size stays at 10.0 and Raw Power at 1.0. Weaponry and Special carry the venom; Attack stays moderate because delivery is passive.
