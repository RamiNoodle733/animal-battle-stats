## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
The roster animal is the ring-tailed lemur, ***Lemur catta***, a terrestrial-leaning lemurid endemic to Madagascar. Use a healthy prime **adult male** for ABS. Older summaries often call the sexes alike in size, but a large comparative growth analysis found *L. catta* to be significantly male-biased in adult body mass, with males about 10.7% larger in that dataset. Wild averages in the Wisconsin Primate Info Net are nevertheless nearly identical at about 2.21 kg, showing that the dimorphism is modest and population/sample dependent.

The canonical male is set at **2.5 kg**, **43 cm head-body length**, and about **103 cm total length including a representative 60 cm tail**. This is a representative fighting-sex animal, not a zoo-obese or record specimen.

## 2. Physical measurements and variation
### Mass
- Animal Diversity Web: average about **2.2 kg**.
- Wisconsin Primate Info Net: wild males/females about **2.207-2.213 kg** on average; captive males about 2.705 kg and females 2.678 kg.
- Tennenhouse et al. (2015): significant male-biased adult body-mass dimorphism, approximately **10.7%** in its sample.
- **Canonical `weight_kg`: 2.5.** This represents a robust prime male while remaining close to measured wild scale and below typical captive inflation.

### Length and height
ADW gives **39-46 cm head-body** and **56-63 cm tail**. The tail is longer than the torso and non-prehensile. **Canonical `length_cm`: 103.0 total**, derived from representative 43 cm body + 60 cm tail. No defensible standardized shoulder-height dataset was found, so **`height_cm`: 0.0** rather than inventing a standing or seated height.

### Speed and locomotion
No reliable species-specific instrumented maximum running speed was found. Ring-tailed lemurs are the most terrestrial living lemurs, regularly walking and running quadrupedally, but also climb and jump. High-speed/force-plate research includes *L. catta* in comparative primate jumping biomechanics, supporting coordinated jumping rather than a defensible maximum travel speed. **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** pending direct measurement.

### Lifespan
Wisconsin reports wild females rarely exceeding 16 years, a known wild female around 18-20 years, males recorded to at least 15, and captivity reaching 27 years. National Geographic gives wild lifespan up to about 18 years. **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 16.0**, representing normal wild adult longevity rather than captive maximum.

### Bite and dentition
No defensible standardized PSI measurement exists. A comparative primate jaw-mechanics paper estimated **56.51 N posterior bite force** for *L. catta* from musculoskeletal geometry, but force in newtons cannot be converted to PSI without defensible contact area. A 2026 morphology paper also characterizes *L. catta* as relatively gracile and links its unfused mandibular symphysis with low bite-force production. **`bite_force_psi`: 0.0**.

The species has long upper canines, slightly larger in males, and both sexes use them in combat. The procumbent lower incisors/canines form a toothcomb primarily associated with grooming and food processing, not a giant piercing weapon.

### Hands, feet, tail and scent structures
Hands and feet provide grasping control, with an opposable hallux aiding climbing. The long tail is **not prehensile**; it contributes to balance, visual signaling and group cohesion. Males have specialized antebrachial/wrist and brachial/chest scent structures used in marking and ritualized competition. These are signaling systems, not venom glands.

## 3. Combat biology
### Weapons and offensive mechanics
The practical weapons are the jaws/canines, grasping hands and feet, and whole-body grappling. Upper canines can slash during serious fights. At only ~2.5 kg, however, absolute damage is low across a roster containing large carnivores and megafauna. Male contests often stop at threat, scent display or chasing rather than requiring damaging contact.

### Defense and toughness
Defense comes from mobility, grasping escape routes, alert group living and dense fur, not armor. There is no shell, thick hide or major skeletal protection. Against a similarly sized attacker, climbing and rapid repositioning can prevent clean contact; once caught by a materially larger predator, the lemur has little structural protection.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Ring-tailed lemurs are unusual among lemurs for extensive ground use. They walk/run quadrupedally, climb well and retain useful jumping capacity. The combination of terrestrial competence, arboreal escape and grasping feet produces high maneuverability for a small mammal. It should not be confused with proven high top speed.

### Endurance and recovery
Smithsonian reports groups may travel up to **6 km per day** while foraging. This supports useful daily locomotor endurance, but it is not evidence for extreme sustained running. Heat, dehydration and the animal's small absolute energy reserve constrain prolonged high-output fighting.

### Senses
Olfaction is unusually important. Individuals use scent marks for identity, reproductive and territorial/social information. Vision supports diurnal locomotion and social signaling, while hearing and vocal communication help maintain group awareness. Senses are strong in a matchup context but not comparable to specialist raptors or macrosmatic pursuit carnivores at long range.

### Intelligence and tactics
Ring-tailed lemurs live in complex multi-male/multi-female groups. Experimental work found them outperforming less-social mongoose lemurs on transitive-inference tasks before corrective training, consistent with sophisticated processing of ordinal/social relationships. They use threat stares, submission displays, scent signals, vocalizations and flexible escalation. This supports strong primate tactics, but not great-ape/corvid-level tool innovation.

### Fighting and social competition
Females dominate males socially, but adult males compete intensely during the breeding season. Wisconsin documents **jump-fighting**, which can escalate to serious injury, and ritualized male **stink fights** lasting from minutes to roughly an hour. In a stink fight a male rubs scent onto his tail, arches it forward and waves the scented tail toward a rival. This can make the rival counter-display, physically attack or flee.

### Predation and predator defense
The species relies heavily on vigilance, alarm/social information, escape to complex terrain and group cohesion. A single adult can bite and grapple when cornered, but evasion is usually more valuable than standing against a larger predator.

### Environment, strengths and weaknesses
**Best environments:** mixed ground/arboreal terrain with trunks, branches and vertical escape options.  
**Weak environments:** open flat ground against faster, larger predators; water-heavy matchups; confined spaces that remove climbing escape.

**Good matchup archetypes:** similarly sized poorly maneuvering animals, small opponents vulnerable to grasp-and-bite tactics.  
**Bad matchup archetypes:** small carnivores with stronger jaws, raptors with clean aerial access, venomous specialists, and virtually all medium/large predators.

## 6. Exactly two special abilities
1. **Scent-Duel Display** — Adult males deliberately load the tail with wrist/chest gland secretions and wave it toward rivals. It can cause counter-display, withdrawal or escalation, giving a genuine conspecific contest effect without pretending the odor is toxic.
2. **Mixed-Terrain Escape** — Grasping feet, opposable hallux, quadrupedal ground competence, climbing and coordinated jumping let the lemur switch between terrestrial and arboreal escape routes rapidly.

## 7. Exactly two unique traits
1. **Female-Dominant Society** — Females dominate males within troops, an unusual primate social system that shapes access, conflict and reproductive competition.
2. **Signal Tail** — The conspicuous non-prehensile ringed tail functions in balance, group cohesion and visual/olfactory signaling rather than as a grasping fifth limb.

## 8. Ecology and expanded profile
### Habitat and range
Endemic to southern and southwestern Madagascar, the species occupies dry deciduous forest, gallery forest, spiny bush/scrub, rocky areas and some montane/humid habitats. IUCN notes a broad but fragmented range with often-low densities.

### Diet
Opportunistic omnivorous/frugivorous feeding includes fruit, leaves, flowers, herbs, insects and occasional small vertebrates. Seasonal availability strongly changes diet.

### Social structure and life history
Troops commonly contain multiple males and females and may range from about 3 to 25 individuals. Females remain in natal groups while males disperse around sexual maturity and can change groups repeatedly. Smithsonian places male group change around maturity at roughly three years. Reproduction is seasonal, producing young after a roughly five-month gestation in common accounts.

### Conservation and human interaction
The species is **Endangered**. IUCN's 2020 lemur assessment reported a suspected decline of at least 50% over three generations, driven by habitat destruction/fragmentation and hunting pressure. It is common in captivity but that does not offset loss of wild populations.

### Adaptations
The long tail supports balance and signaling; grasping extremities support climbing; terrestrial quadrupedalism broadens foraging options; scent glands encode social information; dense fur buffers variable conditions; and behavioral flexibility allows exploitation of diverse southern-Madagascar habitats.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is the most terrestrial of the living lemurs despite retaining strong climbing ability.
- The striped tail is longer than the head-body length and is not prehensile.
- Males can hold ritualized stink fights in which the tail becomes a scent-delivery display structure.
- Troops may travel as much as about 6 km in a day while searching for food.
- Experimental ring-tailed lemurs showed stronger spontaneous transitive-inference performance than less-social mongoose lemurs.
- Females dominate males socially, even though a modern growth analysis found modest male-biased adult mass in its sample.

### Concise site summary
A highly social, ground-using Madagascar primate whose real battle strengths are agility, grasping mobility, social cognition and extraordinary scent communication. Its canines can injure similarly sized rivals, but at roughly 2.5 kg its absolute attack and durability remain low across the full ABS roster.

### Narrative profile
The ring-tailed lemur is not a miniature ape bruiser. Its combat biology is built around avoiding bad contact, reading social situations and controlling position. On the ground it moves confidently on all fours with its striped tail held conspicuously; in complex terrain it can climb, grasp and jump to turn a flat pursuit into a three-dimensional escape problem. When conflict is unavoidable, canines and grappling provide a real but small-scale weapon set.

Its most distinctive competitive system is chemical. Male scent structures are integrated into ritualized contests in which secretions are applied to the tail and presented toward rivals. This can settle competition without a damaging fight, although jump-fighting and biting show that escalation is possible. Cognitively, experimental transitive reasoning and complex troop hierarchies justify a high Intelligence rating relative to most similarly sized mammals. None of these adaptations erase the central ABS fact: a 2.5 kg lemur remains physically fragile beside foxes, large monkeys, carnivores and megafauna.

### Future field proposals
- Obtain instrumented maximum ground-running speed for healthy wild adults rather than extrapolating from generic lemur claims.
- Measure sex-specific in-vivo canine bite force and tooth contact area; keep PSI unset until both exist.
- Quantify jump distance/acceleration in wild adults across ground-to-tree transitions.
- Compare male contest injury rates and escalation probability by rank/season to refine Ferocity.

### Source conflicts and uncertainty
Older institutional syntheses often describe the sexes as alike in size, while Tennenhouse et al. found statistically significant male-biased adult mass. ABS therefore acknowledges modest male bias but does not exaggerate it. Lifespan sources mix wild typical values with captive maxima; 16 years is used as a representative wild figure. No reliable maximum speed or PSI is promoted.
