# Crowned Eagle (*Stephanoaetus coronatus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Crowned Eagle (also African crowned eagle or crowned hawk-eagle)
- **Scientific name:** *Stephanoaetus coronatus* (Linnaeus, 1766)
- **Exact animal represented:** the living African species *Stephanoaetus coronatus*, the only living member of its genus, found in forests and wooded habitats of sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Status:** Living; Near Threatened.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **4.0 kg**, **90.0 cm** total length, about **1.7 m** wingspan. She is the larger and heavier sex and therefore the stronger fighting form.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** females weigh about 3.2–4.7 kg and are roughly 10–15% larger than the smaller sex, which weighs about 2.55–4.12 kg. Plumage is broadly similar.
- **Population notes:** in southern Africa the species is limited to eastern areas. Diet varies strongly by habitat: rainforest birds take mostly monkeys, while birds in savanna woodland, forest islands and suburbs take more hyraxes and small antelope.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The feet are the main weapon: thick legs, heavy toes and wide, strongly curved talons that crush and pierce. The hooked bill is used after the prey is controlled. Throughout its range, the main prey are monkeys, small antelope such as duikers and chevrotains, rock hyraxes, rodents and viverrids. Mammals make up most of the diet.

Evidence of weapon power comes from long-term prey studies. In a 37-month study in Kibale National Park, Uganda, prey remains collected under the nests of two pairs were mostly primates. The eagles took adult and juvenile monkeys roughly in proportion to their abundance and took males more often than females, even though the males are the larger, better-armed sex. A related taphonomic study documented the damage the eagles leave on monkey skulls and skeletons. Popular and institutional summaries describe kills of antelope weighing several times the eagle's own mass, with bushbuck up to about 30 kg as an exceptional upper record. These are extremes, not the typical kill.

### Offensive mechanics
The crowned eagle attacks inside closed forest and wooded habitat, where monkeys, duikers and hyraxes live. It strikes with its heavy feet and holds on with its massive talons until the prey is subdued. Its short, broad, rounded wings and long tail suit short, controlled attacks in tight spaces better than open-country pursuit. No quantified description of attack sequences (perch height, strike distance, success rate) was located.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Broad, rounded wings and a long tail favor tight turns and controlled movement through forest over sustained high-speed flight. Agility is scored as elite for forest maneuvering, not for top speed.

### Endurance
No evidence of long pursuit flights or long-distance soaring hunts was found, and the short wings are not built for them. GPS telemetry has been used on urban breeding pairs in South Africa, but distance figures were not retrieved. Stamina is scored as moderate.

### Senses
Like other forest eagles it hunts by sight from cover in cluttered habitat. No species-specific sensory measurement was located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Kibale data show selective predation: some monkey species were taken more often than expected by chance and others less often. Urban South African eagles switch prey depending on what is available, taking rock hyraxes where they are present and more vervet monkeys where hyraxes are absent. This supports solid raptor-level tactical flexibility.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Pairs are territorial and invest heavily in one young every two years. The species commits to dangerous prey, including adult male monkeys that live in groups and can fight back. Ferocity is scored high because of this routine commitment to risky prey.

### Environmental strengths
- Closed-canopy forest, riverine woodland and wooded gorges that allow hidden perches and steep attacks.
- Prey living in trees or on the forest floor below perches.
- Wooded suburban greenspaces where hyraxes and monkeys are plentiful.

### Weaknesses
- Only about 4 kg in absolute mass.
- No armor; light avian skeleton.
- Short wings reduce open-country pursuit and soaring efficiency.
- Loses its advantage if grounded or caught by a large terrestrial grappler.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** monkeys, hyraxes, small antelope, medium arboreal or forest-floor mammals, opponents surprised from above in cover.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized raptors and medium carnivores where first contact decides the fight.
- **Bad:** armored animals, large carnivores, great apes, large ungulates and any grappler heavy enough to overpower the eagle once contact is made.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Broad-Wing Forest Strike** — short, broad, rounded wings and a long tail let the eagle attack inside closed forest, where monkeys and duikers live, instead of needing open sky. This turns forest cover into a first-strike advantage.
2. **Crushing Talon Grip** — thick legs and wide, heavy talons clamp and pierce prey until it stops moving, letting a 4 kg eagle kill monkeys and small antelope well above its own weight.

### Unique traits
1. **Primate Hunter of Africa** — in Ugandan rainforest, primates made up the great majority of prey remains, and eagles took adult males more often than expected.
2. **Great Stick Nest** — pairs reuse and enlarge the same nest for many years; nests can reach about 2.4 m across and 3 m deep and may pass to a new pair.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The crowned eagle occurs across sub-Saharan Africa in closed forest, riverine and gallery forest, dense woodland and wooded gorges. In southern Africa it is limited to eastern regions. A breeding population also lives in the wooded suburbs and greenspaces of Durban and Pietermaritzburg in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa.

### Diet and ecology
It is a mammal-focused apex predator. Main prey across the range are monkeys, small antelope (duikers, chevrotains, young bushbuck), rock hyraxes, rodents and viverrids. In equatorial rainforest, estimates put monkeys at roughly 25–90% of the diet; in savanna and forest-island habitats primates are taken less often and hyraxes and duikers more often. In urban KwaZulu-Natal, rock hyrax was the main prey, and vervet monkeys were more important where hyraxes were absent.

### Social structure
Adults live in territorial pairs, and no cooperative hunting was documented in the sources reviewed. The young depend on the parents for a long time after fledging.

### Reproduction and life history
Pairs usually raise one chick every two years, with a breeding cycle of about 500 days, because the young stay dependent for so long. The stick nest is lined with fresh green branches and reused for years; building one from scratch can take about five months.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Near Threatened** on the IUCN Red List, with a decreasing population estimated at roughly 5,000–50,000 mature individuals. The main threats are loss and fragmentation of African forest and the bushmeat trade. Urban populations face additional human-caused threats and conflicts, reviewed in a 2021 study of urban crowned eagles.

### Special features
- Thick, heavy legs and wide, massive talons.
- Short, broad, rounded wings and long tail for forest attacks.
- A two-year breeding cycle with a long period of dependence for the young.
- Large, long-used stick nests.
- Prey choice that shifts with habitat.

### Human interaction
Crowned eagles sometimes live near people in wooded suburbs. South African researchers placed time-lapse cameras at 11 urban nests to check how often prey with human associations was taken, and use GPS tracking to understand how urban pairs use space, with the aim of reducing conflict.

### Genuine fun facts
- In a 37-month Kibale study, primates made up the great majority of prey remains under two nests.
- The eagles took male monkeys more often than females, even though males are larger and more combative.
- Nests can grow to about 2.4 m across and 3 m deep after years of reuse.
- A single breeding cycle lasts roughly 500 days, so a pair raises at most one young every two years.
- A 4 kg female can kill antelope several times her weight; bushbuck up to about 30 kg are an exceptional record.
- Urban eagles in KwaZulu-Natal live mostly on rock hyraxes.
- Building a new nest from scratch can take a pair about five months.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Crowned Eagle is Africa's most powerful forest eagle. The canonical female weighs about 4 kg but has thick legs and massive talons that regularly kill monkeys and small antelope far heavier than herself. It attacks inside closed forest with short, broad wings, but its small absolute mass and lack of armor keep it far below large mammals in raw power and defense.

### Rich narrative profile
The crowned eagle packs an unusually heavy weapon system into a medium-sized raptor. At about 4 kg for the canonical female it is lighter than a golden eagle, yet its legs are thick, its toes heavy and its talons wide and strongly curved. It is built for one decisive move: a short forest attack onto a monkey or small antelope, followed by a crushing grip.

Field data support the reputation. Under two Ugandan nests over three years, primate remains dominated, and the eagles took adult male monkeys more often than expected, not just easy juveniles. Elsewhere the same bird lives on rock hyraxes, duikers and, in South African suburbs, the hyraxes and vervets of garden greenspace. That flexibility, together with selective choice of monkey prey, makes it a thoughtful predator for a raptor.

In an absolute matchup its limits are the limits of a 4 kg bird. It has no armor, a light skeleton and short wings built for forest attacks, not long chases. It wins by choosing the moment of contact inside cover. On open ground, or against a heavy grappler that survives the first strike, its advantages fade quickly.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 2.5–4.7 kg overall; canonical 4.0 kg female is representative, not the maximum.
- **Talons:** museum hallux claws of 4.9–6.2 cm are accepted; 10 cm captive claims are rejected as unconfirmed.
- **Maximum prey:** "six times its own mass" and 20–30 kg antelope are exceptional upper claims from summaries, not the typical kill. Weaponry reflects routine kills of adult monkeys.
- **Lifespan:** 14–15 years comes from institutional averages; no ringing-based wild longevity study was retrieved.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Harpy Eagle (8.0 kg; A44.0 Ag91.0 Sp79.0):** very similar forest-ambush approach, but the harpy has twice the mass and larger talons, so it stays above on Attack, Weaponry, Size and Special.
- **Versus Philippine Eagle (6.0 kg; A42.0):** the Philippine Eagle is heavier with a larger bill; the crowned eagle has proportionally heavier feet. Attack 41.0 sits just below.
- **Versus Golden Eagle (5.0 kg; A40.0 St79.0):** the crowned eagle is lighter but routinely kills group-living primates and small antelope in cover, so Attack is set 1 point higher and Ferocity higher. The golden eagle keeps much higher Stamina from its soaring and long-range hunting.
- **Versus Caracal (17 kg; A44.0 D25.0):** the caracal, four times heavier with feline grappling, stays above the eagle on Attack and Defense despite the eagle's superior strike geometry.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "kills prey six times its weight" is treated as an exceptional feat and expressed through Weaponry, Ferocity and Special, not through Raw Power or Size.
