# Yellow-naped Amazon (*Amazona auropalliata*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Yellow-naped Amazon (also yellow-naped parrot)
- **Scientific name:** *Amazona auropalliata* (Lesson, 1842); sometimes treated as a subspecies of the yellow-crowned amazon (*A. ochrocephala*).
- **Exact animal represented:** the living Central American amazon parrot *Amazona auropalliata*, in its wild form on the Pacific slope from southern Mexico to north-western Costa Rica and in north-eastern Honduras and Nicaragua. Pet yellow-naped amazons are the same species and are covered where relevant.
- **Status:** Living; Critically Endangered (IUCN).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **0.5 kg**, **35.0 cm** total length. The sexes look alike; males are used because captive males are the more nest-protective, biting sex in the breeding season (Wikipedia), and pair duets show clear sex-specific roles.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** none in plumage. No reliable sex-specific mass data were located.
- **Population notes:** three subspecies: *A. a. auropalliata* (Pacific slope, Oaxaca to Costa Rica), *A. a. parvipes* (Mosquito Coast of Honduras and Nicaragua, with more yellow and red at the bend of the wing) and *A. a. caribaea* (Bay Islands, Honduras, where birds eat pine cones). Costa Rica holds the stronghold population.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The bill is the only weapon. A calculated bite of about 94 N is strong for a 0.5 kg bird; the same study notes that a 451 g blue-fronted amazon bites with about 123 N while a Cooper's hawk of the same mass bites with only about 3 N. Captive yellow-naped amazons are known for nest-protective biting, especially males in the breeding season, and signal stress by fanning the tail and "pinning" (rapidly narrowing and widening) the pupils (Wikipedia). It is not a predator; it eats seeds, fruit, figs and nuts.

### Offensive mechanics
Bites are delivered at close range from a perch or while climbing. Wild pairs defend small nest territories mainly with coordinated duets; neighboring pairs counter-duet, and these contests sometimes become so heated that physical fights break out at territory boundaries, though this is rare (Wikipedia).

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A stocky, short-tailed amazon that flies strongly between communal night roosts and feeding areas and climbs well with feet and bill. It is less acrobatic than long-tailed macaws and cockatoos.

### Endurance
Birds commute daily between communal roosts and feeding areas across dry forest, mangroves and farmland. Translocated adults in Costa Rica returned to their original capture sites within 120 days (Salinas-Melgoza and Wright 2012), showing strong homing over the landscape. No daily distance data were located.

### Senses
Normal parrot vision and hearing. Acute hearing and vocal memory underpin its dialect and duet system. No special sense is documented.

### Intelligence and tactics
The vocal behavior of the yellow-naped amazon has been studied more than that of any other wild parrot (Wikipedia):
- Costa Rican populations have distinct vocal dialects, each with its own contact call, and birds respond less to calls from other dialects. Dialects were stable from 1994 to 2005 (Wright et al. 2008) but shifted between 2005 and 2016 as populations collapsed, with more bilingual sites and new call types (Dahlin et al. 2024).
- A juvenile moved across a dialect boundary changed its contact call to match the local dialect within six weeks (Salinas-Melgoza and Wright 2012).
- Mated pairs sing coordinated duets that follow syntax rules for note order; pairs took longer to approach playback of duets with broken syntax, suggesting they notice the difference (Dahlin and Wright 2012).
- In captivity it is one of the best mimics of human speech.
This is strong evidence of vocal learning and social communication, but less direct evidence of physical problem solving than exists for cockatoos, kea and corvids.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Pairs are monogamous in the breeding season and defend small territories around the nest by duetting. Counter-duets between neighbors can escalate to fights. Birds gather in pairs and flocks with larger groups at communal roosts.

### Predator defense
Flocking, communal roosting, vigilance, loud calls and flight. Most nest failures are caused by people taking chicks for the pet trade (Wikipedia).

### Environmental strengths
- Dry deciduous forest, savanna woodland, gallery forest and mangroves.
- Farmland with scattered large trees for nesting.

### Weaknesses
- About 0.5 kg in absolute mass.
- No armor.
- Bill is its only weapon; bites are close range.
- Heavy poaching of chicks; wild numbers are very low.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small animals and birds that can be bitten and cannot follow it into the air.
- **Mixed:** other amazons and parrots of similar size.
- **Bad:** hawks, falcons, tree-climbing snakes, cats and anything larger.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Clamp-Jaw Bite** — a calculated bite of about 94 N, roughly 30 times the measured bite of a Cooper's hawk of similar weight, makes a defending yellow-naped amazon a serious close-range biter for its size.
2. **Duet Border Patrol** — mated pairs defend their nest territory with precisely timed duets that follow rules of note order, and counter-duets with neighbors can escalate into fights.

### Unique traits
1. **Parrot Dialects** — Costa Rican yellow-naped amazons speak in regional dialects with distinct contact calls, and young birds that move learn the local dialect within weeks.
2. **Master Mimic** — one of the parrots best known for copying human speech, which, with its playful nature, made it a top target of the pet trade.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The yellow-naped amazon lives along the Pacific slope from Oaxaca in southern Mexico to north-western Costa Rica, on the Mosquito Coast of Honduras and Nicaragua, and on the Bay Islands of Honduras. It uses deciduous and evergreen forest, clearings, savanna woodland, pine woodland on ridges, gallery forest and semi-arid woodland, and less often thorn forest, mangroves and farmland with scattered trees (WPT).

### Diet and ecology
It eats seeds of *Cochlospermum* and *Curatella*, figs and ripening *Terminalia* fruit (WPT), plus other nuts, berries and fruit. Bay Island birds feed on Caribbean pine cones.

### Social structure
Birds live in pairs or flocks and gather in larger groups at communal night roosts and feeding areas. Pairs are monogamous in the breeding season and defend small territories by duetting.

### Reproduction and life history
Nests are in large natural tree cavities; the birds chew the entrance but do not dig new holes, and dead coyol palms are a preferred site. Clutches are 2–4 eggs, laid mainly in February–March (WPT). Most nests fail because chicks are taken for the pet trade (Wikipedia).

### Conservation
The yellow-naped amazon is listed as **Critically Endangered** by the IUCN, after two uplistings from Vulnerable (Dahlin et al. 2024). WPT gives about 1,000–2,500 mature birds and decreasing. The population is estimated to have fallen by more than 92% over three generations, mainly from poaching of chicks for the pet trade and loss of dry forest to farming. Populations in Mexico, Guatemala and Honduras are now very small, and Costa Rica is the stronghold. The Mesoamerican Parrot Census Network, supported by WPT, monitors the species with volunteers.

### Special features
- Yellow band across the nape on otherwise green plumage.
- Regional vocal dialects that young birds learn.
- Syntax-based pair duets.
- Excellent human-speech mimicry in captivity.

### Human interaction
Yellow-naped amazons have long been prized pets for their talking ability and playful personality. They bond strongly with one person and can bite, especially males protecting a perceived nest in the breeding season. The pet trade is the main reason the species collapsed in the wild. A rare turquoise colour mutation exists.

### Genuine fun facts
- Yellow-naped amazons in Costa Rica speak in regional dialects, much like human accents.
- A young bird moved to another dialect area switched to the local call within six weeks.
- Pairs sing duets that follow rules about which notes come in which order.
- Neighboring pairs sometimes "counter-duet" until the argument turns into a fight.
- Its calculated bite is about 30 times the measured bite of a Cooper's hawk of similar weight.
- The wild population has fallen by more than 92% in three generations.
- One captive yellow-naped amazon reportedly lived 49 years.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Yellow-naped Amazon is a 0.5 kg green parrot from the Pacific side of Central America, famous as a talker and for wild vocal dialects and pair duets. Its calculated 94 N bite is strong for its size, but it is a light, unarmored bird. Poaching for the pet trade has made it Critically Endangered.

### Rich narrative profile
The yellow-naped amazon is a stocky green parrot with a yellow band across the back of its neck, living in the dry forests, mangroves and farm country of Central America's Pacific slope. It is one of the best-studied wild parrots because of its voice. In Costa Rica, birds from different regions use different contact calls, forming dialects with sharp borders. Young birds that move learn the local dialect within weeks, while adults tend to stay loyal to home, and over 22 years of study these dialects began to blur as populations crashed.

Pairs defend their nest territory by duetting, trading male and female notes in a fixed order. Neighbors reply with duets of their own, and occasionally these vocal contests turn into physical fights at the border. In captivity the same vocal talent makes it one of the best mimics of human speech, which is exactly why poachers take its chicks: most wild nests fail because the young are stolen, and the population has fallen by more than 92% in three generations.

In an absolute matchup it is a 0.5 kg bird with a strong bite for its size and no armor. It can hurt a hand or a small animal, but against a raptor or a carnivore it has only flight and alertness to rely on.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 432 g (AnAge) to 550 g (WPT); canonical 0.5 kg.
- **Bite:** a calculated value from a single specimen; the method was validated across parrots but precision for this species is low.
- **Lifespan:** captive records only.
- **Taxonomy:** sometimes lumped with the yellow-crowned amazon; the report follows WPT and IUCN in treating it as a species.
- **Speed:** no measured flight speed; field left at 0.0.
