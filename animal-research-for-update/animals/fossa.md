# Fossa (*Cryptoprocta ferox*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Fossa (also spelled fosa)
- **Scientific name:** *Cryptoprocta ferox* Bennett, 1833
- **Exact animal represented:** the living fossa of Madagascar, the island's largest native carnivore. Only the living species is represented.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Status:** Living; endemic to Madagascar.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male of about **8.0 kg**, head-body about **75 cm** with a tail of similar length. This sits near the top of the normal male range without using exceptional heavy individuals.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger and heavier than females. Published ranges give males about 6.2–8.6 kg and females about 5.5–6.8 kg. Males are also the sex that competes at mating trees and that has been seen forming hunting associations.
- **Population variation:** body size varies with region and condition, and a few unusually heavy males have been reported, but those are not used as the representative baseline. Juvenile females show a temporary male-like external genital appearance that fades with age, which can confuse field sexing of young animals.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The fossa is a compact, muscular carnivore built to kill primates about its own size. It seizes prey with its forelimbs and sharp semi-retractile claws, pins it, and kills with a bite to the head or neck. Its prey list is dominated by lemurs: diet studies in western Madagascar found vertebrates make up the great majority of the diet, with lemurs the largest share, alongside tenrecs, rodents, reptiles and birds. It is the only native predator able to kill the largest living lemurs, such as sifakas.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The fossa is equally at home on the ground and in the canopy. It walks flat-footed, and its highly flexible ankles can rotate so it can descend trunks head-first, a feature shared with few other carnivores. A long tail acts as a counterbalance when it leaps between branches. This lets it chase lemurs through trees instead of waiting for them to come down, which is its main matchup advantage.

### Endurance
Fossa range widely: GPS and radio-tracking studies in Kirindy Forest show males using larger home ranges than females and covering substantial distances. Activity is spread across day and night. This supports good travel endurance, but its hunting style is short, intense pursuit and grappling rather than long chases.

### Senses
Fossa hunt by sight, hearing and smell, in dense forest and at night. No quantitative sensory data were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Usually solitary, but not rigidly so. Simultaneous GPS tracking in Kirindy showed that some adult males form lasting associations, and three males were observed hunting a sifaka together for about 45 minutes. This flexible cooperation is unusual for a solitary carnivore and supports a solid tactics score, without primate-level problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Adults live alone for most of the year. Males may associate in pairs or trios. Hunting is opportunistic across ground and canopy, and a single fossa can take a sifaka of several kilograms.

### Fighting and aggression
Mating takes place on traditional mating trees where a female stays for extended periods and several males compete for access; this brings males into direct contests. Fossa defend themselves fiercely when cornered, and villagers often regard them as dangerous poultry and livestock raiders.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense forest with continuous canopy, where it can move in three dimensions.
- Mixed ground and tree terrain where prey must commit to one or the other.
- Night or low-light conditions.

### Weaknesses
- Only about 8 kg; lacks the mass to overpower medium or large predators.
- No armor or thick hide.
- Bite and claw offense requires close contact.
- Much less effective in open ground with no trees to use.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** primates and other climbing animals up to roughly its own size; small carnivores that cannot follow it into trees.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized cats such as the ocelot, where grappling skill and footing decide the fight.
- **Bad:** larger predators, large herbivores, armored animals and anything that can absorb its bite while using mass.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Head-First Descent** — highly flexible ankles rotate so the fossa can run down tree trunks head-first and leap between branches, letting it chase prey through the canopy and down to the ground without breaking contact.
2. **Canopy Ambush Grapple** — it seizes lemurs with sharp semi-retractile claws and grasping forelimbs, pins them and kills with a head or neck bite; it is the only native predator able to take the largest living lemurs.

### Unique traits
1. **Madagascar's Top Carnivore** — the largest native carnivore on Madagascar and the main predator of lemurs, with lemurs making up the largest share of its diet.
2. **Mating-Tree Gatherings** — females mate on traditional mating trees where several males gather, and some males form lasting associations that can include hunting together.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The fossa lives only on Madagascar. It occurs across the island's forest types, from humid eastern rainforest to dry deciduous forest in the west and spiny forest in the south, and from sea level into the mountains. It also moves through degraded forest near villages, as tracking in Ankarafantsika National Park has shown.

### Diet and ecology
A carnivore that eats mostly vertebrates. Lemurs are the largest part of the diet at studied sites, with tenrecs, rodents, reptiles, birds and occasionally domestic poultry also taken. Field estimates from western Madagascar suggest fossa predation removes a notable share of local sifaka and mouse lemur populations each year, making it an important pressure on lemur communities.

### Social structure
Mostly solitary. Males use larger home ranges than females, and their ranges overlap more with other males. Some males form stable associations with one or two other males, and cooperative hunting by males has been observed.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding centres on traditional mating trees, where a female remains for an extended period and mates repeatedly with several males. Litters are small, and young stay with the mother for many months. Juvenile females temporarily show male-like external genitalia and coat secretions, which fade as they mature; this transient masculinization was first described in this species.

### Conservation
Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, with a decreasing population trend. Total population estimates are uncertain and vary widely between studies; regional density estimates are low. Main threats are forest loss and fragmentation, hunting and persecution in retaliation for poultry losses, and disease risks from domestic carnivores.

### Special features
- Flexible, rotating ankles that allow head-first descent.
- Sharp semi-retractile claws on all feet.
- Long tail used as a balance pole in trees.
- Flat-footed walking gait on the ground.
- Transient masculinization of juvenile females.

### Human interaction
Fossa are feared and persecuted in parts of rural Madagascar because they take poultry and are the subject of local folklore. Because they are rarely seen in the wild, most people outside Madagascar know them from zoos, where they are kept in a small number of breeding programs.

### Genuine fun facts
- The fossa is Madagascar's largest native carnivore.
- It can climb down trees head-first thanks to ankles that rotate.
- Lemurs make up the largest share of its diet in field studies.
- Three males were once watched hunting a single sifaka together for about 45 minutes.
- Young females temporarily look male before changing as they mature.
- Females mate on traditional "mating trees" where several males gather.
- Its tail is about as long as its head and body combined.

### Concise site-ready summary
The fossa is Madagascar's top predator, a sleek, muscular 8 kg carnivore that hunts lemurs on the ground and high in the canopy. Rotating ankles let it run down trunks head-first, and sharp claws and a strong bite make it deadly to prey its own size, but its small mass limits it against larger fighters.

### Rich narrative profile
The fossa looks like a cross between a small cougar and a mongoose, with a long, low body, short dense coat, big forward-facing eyes and a tail as long as the rest of it. A mature male weighs about 8 kg. On Madagascar, where there are no native cats or dogs, it fills the role of the island's main predator, and its favourite prey is the lemur.

What sets the fossa apart in a fight is where it can fight. Its ankles rotate so it can run down a trunk head-first, it leaps between branches using the tail for balance, and it grips with sharp semi-retractile claws. That lets it follow a sifaka through the canopy, seize it with its forelimbs and finish it with a bite. Some males go further and hunt in small teams, a rare behavior for a mostly solitary carnivore.

On the absolute roster scale, though, the fossa is still a small animal. It has no armor, limited mass, and its weapons all require close contact. It rates near the top for agility and canopy control, alongside the ocelot and just below the clouded leopard, while its Attack and Defense stay in the small-carnivore band well below leopards and other big cats.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** some secondary pages mention males approaching 12 kg; this was not verified and is not used as the baseline.
- **Population:** total population estimates vary widely and depend on method and site; no single figure is adopted.
- **Lifespan:** only zoo longevity was found. The 15-year canonical value is low confidence.
- **Bite:** no fossa bite-force value could be verified; the field stays at 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Ocelot (13 kg; A38 D27 Ag88):** the ocelot is heavier, so the fossa sits just below it in Attack (37.0) and Raw Power (27.0 vs 29.0). Agility is set one point higher (89.0 vs 88.0) because the fossa's head-first descent and canopy pursuit exceed the ocelot's mainly terrestrial hunting.
- **Versus Clouded Leopard (22 kg; A49 Ag94 Sp80):** the clouded leopard keeps higher Attack, Agility and Special because it is nearly three times heavier, has much longer canines and remains the roster's canopy-control extreme. The fossa is the closest match in arboreal style.
- **Versus Bobcat (10 kg; A44) and Caracal (17 kg; A44):** both keep higher Attack as heavier cats with a stronger pouncing kill; the fossa is ahead in arboreal agility and Special.
- **Versus Wolverine:** the live wolverine values are legacy placeholders (for example Attack 1.6, Agility 29.2), not research values, so they are not used as anchors. A researched 18 kg wolverine should end up above the fossa in Attack, Defense and Stamina and below it in Agility.
- **Anti-inflation check:** being Madagascar's top predator does not move the fossa into big-cat territory; at 8 kg it stays far below the leopard (A60) in Attack.
