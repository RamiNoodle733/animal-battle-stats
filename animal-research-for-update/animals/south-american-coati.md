# South American Coati (*Nasua nasua*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** South American coati (also ring-tailed coati, brown-nosed coati)
- **Scientific name:** *Nasua nasua* (Linnaeus, 1766)
- **Exact animal represented:** the living South American coati of tropical and subtropical South America, a member of the raccoon family (Procyonidae). It is not the white-nosed coati of Central and North America or the mountain coatis.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Status:** Living; Least Concern on the IUCN Red List.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male of about **5.5 kg**, head-body about **55 cm**, with a ringed tail of similar length. This sits in the upper half of the published adult range without using the heaviest animals.
- **Sex choice:** adult males usually live alone and are the sex that fights over access to groups of females; solitary males follow groups in the mating season and fight the resident male. The sources used do not split body mass by sex, so the canonical mass is an upper-mid adult value applied to a male.
- **Population variation:** coat colour varies widely, from almost black to orange-red. Populations range from lowland forest to about 2,500 m east of the Andes, and an introduced population lives on Mallorca.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The coati's weapons are long, sharp canines and strong front claws. It is an omnivore that mostly eats fruit and invertebrates, plus small vertebrates and bird eggs, digging prey out of leaf litter, soil and rotten logs with its claws and long snout. Adult males fight each other during the mating season, and a coati that attacked two children in a Brazilian home caused deep scratches and bites, showing that its claws and teeth can wound people when it is cornered or provoked.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Equally at home on the ground and in trees. Its double-jointed ankles rotate about 180 degrees, letting it climb down trunks head-first. It climbs out to the end of a branch and jumps to the next, uses its long, semi-prehensile tail for balance, and swims well. A study of its forelimb muscles found a shoulder girdle built for stability in climbing and digging and strong forepaw movements.

### Endurance
Female bands forage through much of the day over large, overlapping home ranges, moving between fruit trees and ground foraging sites. No daily-distance measurements were verified in this pass, so Stamina is scored moderately.

### Senses
Its long, flexible, pig-like snout and strong sense of smell locate beetles, grubs, termites and other hidden prey under leaf litter and in crevices. No quantitative sensory data were found.

### Intelligence and tactics
Coati bands have structured social relationships: mother-offspring bonds and female-female ties shape grooming networks, and individuals give each other support in food disputes. Alarm calls send the band up into trees, after which they drop to the ground and scatter. Coatis also learn to exploit tourists and hotel grounds where people feed them.

### Sociality and hunting
Females and young live in bands of about 15–30. Most adult males live alone, but the South American coati is the only coati species whose groups keep an adult male year-round; that resident male sired 67–91% of the young in his group, although at least half of litters included a young sired by an outside male.

### Fighting and aggression
Solitary adult males follow groups in the mating season and fight the resident male for access to females. Within bands, aggression mostly occurs during feeding competition. Coatis generally flee larger predators by climbing, but they bite and scratch hard when cornered.

### Environmental strengths
- Forest with both ground cover and trees, where it can switch between foraging on the ground and escaping upward.
- Daylight, when it is active.
- Group settings, where many eyes and alarm calls help detect danger.

### Weaknesses
- Only about 5.5 kg with no armor.
- Canines and claws are effective only at close range and against small opponents.
- Preyed on by jaguars, ocelots, jaguarundis, foxes, boas and large birds of prey.
- Much less effective alone in open ground.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** invertebrates, small reptiles and rodents; similar-sized animals that cannot climb.
- **Mixed:** raccoons, capuchins and other small, agile mammals, where canines and claws weigh against dexterity and cleverness.
- **Bad:** cats from ocelot size up, large birds of prey, big snakes and any larger predator.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Reverse-Ankle Scramble** — double-jointed ankles rotate about 180 degrees so it can run down trunks head-first, and it crosses the canopy by jumping from branch ends, using its tail for balance.
2. **Band Alarm Scatter** — when a band member gives an alarm call, the group climbs trees, then drops to the ground and scatters in many directions, making it hard for a predator to single one out.

### Unique traits
1. **Snorkel Snout** — a long, flexible, pig-like snout noses through leaf litter and crevices while strong front claws rip open logs and dig out hidden prey.
2. **Resident Band Male** — it is the only coati species whose bands include an adult male all year; genetic tests showed resident males fathered 67–91% of their group's young.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Tropical and subtropical South America east of the Andes, from Colombia and the Guianas south to Uruguay and northern Argentina, mainly in lowland forest but up to about 2,500 m. It lives mainly in forest, including deciduous, evergreen and cloud forest, and its occupancy is higher where forest cover is high and lower at higher elevations. It has been introduced to Mallorca, where it is considered invasive.

### Diet and ecology
An omnivore that eats mainly fruit and invertebrates such as beetles, grubs, ants, termites, spiders, scorpions and centipedes, plus frogs, lizards, small rodents and bird eggs. It is likely an important seed disperser and is prey for jaguars, ocelots, jaguarundis, foxes, boas and birds of prey.

### Social structure
Females and young form bands of about 15–30 individuals. Most adult males live alone and join bands during the breeding season, but in this species a resident adult male often stays with a group all year. Bands do not defend exclusive territories, and home ranges overlap.

### Reproduction and life history
All females in a band come into heat within the same one- to two-week period. Females leave the band to give birth in a tree nest after a gestation of about 74–77 days, raising 3–7 young, and rejoin the band with their offspring 5–6 weeks later. Young can climb at 26 days and eat solid food at about four months. Females mature at about two years; males usually leave their birth group at about three years.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. It is widespread, but it depends on forest cover and is hunted by people in parts of its range. Because coatis often live close to people in parks and cities, they share parasites and pathogens with domestic animals, and many veterinary surveys focus on them.

### Special features
- Long, flexible, pig-like snout.
- Ankles that rotate about 180 degrees for head-first descent.
- Long, ringed, semi-prehensile tail held upright when walking.
- Strong, curved front claws.
- Long, sharp canines.

### Human interaction
Coatis are a familiar sight at tourist sites such as Iguazú Falls, and bands may forage through hotel grounds and visitor areas. Where coatis and people interact closely, attacks can occur; a 2010 case report described a coati attacking two children in their home in Brazil, causing deep scratches and bites. People also hunt coatis in parts of their range.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its ankles can turn around so it can climb down trees head-first.
- Solitary males were once thought to be a separate species and were called "coatimundis".
- It is the only coati species whose bands keep an adult male all year.
- All the females in a band come into heat within the same week or two.
- Mothers give birth alone in a tree nest, then bring their young back to the band.
- Coatis have been introduced to the Mediterranean island of Mallorca.

### Concise site-ready summary
The South American coati is a 5.5 kg ring-tailed forager of South American forests that climbs down trees head-first and noses out prey with a long, mobile snout. It lives in bands that raise the alarm and scatter at danger, and it bites and claws hard when cornered, but it is far too small to stand up to real predators.

### Rich narrative profile
The South American coati is a slim, long-snouted relative of the raccoon, with a banded tail that it often carries straight up. A mature male weighs around 5.5 kg. Its coat varies from dark brown to orange-red, and its most noticeable feature is a long, flexible, pig-like nose that it pushes into leaf litter, soil and crevices in search of beetles, grubs and other small prey.

Coatis are built to use both the ground and the trees. Their ankles rotate so they can climb down trunks head-first, their strong front claws rip open rotten logs, and their tails help them balance as they jump from branch to branch. Females and young travel in noisy bands of up to about 30, sleeping in trees and foraging by day; at an alarm call the whole band shoots up the trees and then drops and scatters.

In battle terms the coati is a quick, well-armed small mammal rather than a fighter. Long canines and claws let males fight rivals and let any cornered coati inflict painful wounds, but it weighs little more than a large house cat and is regular prey for jaguars, ocelots and big birds of prey. It sits just below the raccoon in Attack, above it in Agility, and in the upper band for Intelligence thanks to its complex band life.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 2–7.2 kg (Wikipedia) versus 3–6 kg (ADW); San Diego Zoo's 1–1.5 kg refers to a multi-species page and is not used.
- **Sex differences:** the sources used do not give separate male and female masses, so the canonical male mass is an upper-mid adult value.
- **Height and speed:** no reliable values; both fields are 0.0.
- **Bite:** no measurement or estimate exists.
- **Dispersal:** Wikipedia says males usually leave their birth group at about three years, while ADW describes males as philopatric and females as dispersing; the report follows the more recent summary but flags the conflict.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Raccoon (6.5 kg; A30 D29 Ag74 St59 I76 Sp68):** the raccoon is heavier and a stronger problem solver, so it keeps slightly higher Attack, Defense, Intelligence and Special; the coati is the better climber and descends head-first, so its Agility is higher.
- **Versus Capuchin Monkey (4 kg; A23 D17 Ag84 I92):** the coati is heavier with longer canines and claws, so it has higher Attack and Defense; the capuchin keeps higher Agility and far higher Intelligence.
- **Versus Red Panda (5 kg; A24 D24 Ag77):** similar mass; the coati is a more active forager with sharper canines and a stronger social system, so it rates slightly higher in Attack, Agility, Stamina and Intelligence with similar Defense.
- **Versus Ocelot (13 kg; A38):** a major predator of coatis, and more than twice as heavy with feline grappling, so it stays well above the coati in Attack.
- **Anti-inflation check:** bold coatis at tourist sites do not raise its ratings; a 5.5 kg omnivore remains in the low bands for Attack and Defense.
