# Arapaima (*Arapaima gigas*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Arapaima (pirarucu in Brazil, paiche in Peru)
- **Scientific name:** *Arapaima gigas* (Schinz, 1822), family Arapaimidae (bonytongues, often placed in Osteoglossidae)
- **Exact animal represented:** the giant air-breathing arapaima of the Amazon basin, using *A. gigas* as the roster species. Stewart's 2013 revision showed that the genus holds several species, including the slender *A. leptosoma* from the Solimões River, so older and management literature labelled "*A. gigas*" can include other *Arapaima* species. Values here are drawn from sources on the large Amazonian form.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature large adult, **100.0 kg** and **210.0 cm total length**. Sex is left unspecified because no consistent size difference between the sexes was found; males differ mainly in breeding colour and in providing most parental care.
- **Population variation:** fishing has removed most of the largest fish. FishBase gives a common length of about 2 m, and a management summary notes that fish over 2 m weighing more than 125 kg are still found, while fish at the published maximums are now rare.

### Bite and mouth
- The arapaima feeds mainly by suction: the mouth cavity expands suddenly and water and prey rush in. Its "bony tongue" and the roof of the mouth carry tooth-bearing plates used to grip and crush prey inside the mouth.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite measurement exists, and its feeding does not rely on a bite in the usual sense.

### Scale armor
Large, overlapping scales form the arapaima's main defence. Meyers, Yang and colleagues showed that each scale has a highly mineralized outer layer, about twice as hard as the inner layer, over a flexible foundation of collagen fibres stacked in alternating directions like plywood. In mechanical tests, piranha teeth could not penetrate the scales; one test tooth broke before it got through. The calculated bite of a 1 kg piranha was about 20 N.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The arapaima is a suction-feeding ambush predator. It hovers near the surface or among flooded vegetation, then opens its large mouth suddenly so that prey is sucked in within a fraction of a second. Prey is gripped and crushed against the toothed tongue and palate. Most prey is fish, with crustaceans and sometimes birds, frogs or small mammals taken near the surface. Against large opponents this system offers little damage: it has no large teeth, claws or venom. Its most dangerous physical asset beyond feeding is the thrust of a 100 kg muscular body, which males use to clear nest hollows with their tails.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
A long, heavily muscled body with rear-set dorsal and anal fins gives strong acceleration for surface strikes and short bursts. It moves through flooded forest and lakes but is not an open-water pursuit swimmer. Maneuverability is moderate for its size.

### Endurance and respiration
The arapaima is an obligate air breather: it must surface roughly every 10–20 minutes to gulp air, and it drowns if denied access to the surface. This lets it live in warm, oxygen-poor floodplain lakes where many fishes cannot, but it also makes it predictable and easy for fishers to find and count.

### Senses
It detects prey near the surface visually and through water movement. No specialized sense such as electroreception is known.

### Intelligence and tactics
Hunting is ambush-based. The most complex behavior is reproductive: males build nests, guard eggs and young, and lead schools of fry, communicating with them through a secretion from glands on the head. Fishers can predict and count individual fish at their breathing rises, which shows how regular its behavior is.

### Sociality and hunting
Adults are mostly solitary outside breeding. Pairs form during the rainy season, and the male stays with the brood for up to about three months while the young follow him into flooded forest.

### Fighting and aggression
Guarding males defend nests and young. The species is not known to attack large animals, and it is not aggressive toward people outside nest defence.

### Environmental strengths
- Warm, oxygen-poor floodplain lakes where water-breathing fish struggle.
- Flooded forest and surface waters where it can ambush prey from below.
- Contests with small, biting fish that cannot penetrate its scales.

### Weaknesses
- Must surface to breathe every 10–20 minutes.
- Weak offensive weapons against large or armored opponents.
- Scale armor resists cutting teeth but not crushing crocodilian bites.
- Large, predictable fish that is easy to locate at the surface.
- Poor performance outside slow, warm fresh water.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small fish and other animals it can engulf; piranha-style biters whose teeth cannot get through its scales.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized fish and reptiles in murky floodplain water.
- **Bad:** caimans and other crocodilians, large cats at the surface, and anything that can pin it away from air or crush through its scales.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Piranha-Proof Scales** — each scale pairs a hard mineralized outer layer with a flexible, plywood-like collagen base. In lab tests, piranha teeth could not puncture the scales. In a battle this blunts attacks from cutting and puncturing teeth.
2. **Gulp-Air Survival** — an obligate air breather that rises every 10–20 minutes to gulp air, letting it hunt and live in warm, oxygen-poor lakes that drive other large fish away.

### Unique traits
1. **Giant of the Bonytongues** — one of the largest freshwater fishes in the world, with published maximums near 3 m and 200 kg, and a toothed "bony tongue" that crushes prey inside the mouth.
2. **Devoted Father** — males build nests, guard eggs and lead schools of fry for up to about three months, communicating with the young through secretions from glands on the head.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Arapaima live in the Amazon basin in Brazil, Peru and neighbouring countries, in floodplain lakes, slow rivers, flooded forest and swamps. Populations also occur in Guyana, and introduced populations exist outside South America, including in Indonesia where the species is considered invasive.

### Diet and ecology
Adults are mainly piscivorous, taking other fish, and also eat crustaceans and occasionally birds, frogs, small mammals and plant matter. As one of the largest predators in floodplain lakes, the arapaima is itself hunted mainly by people; caimans also live in the same lakes.

### Social structure
Adults are usually solitary. During breeding a male and female pair, and the male guards the brood. Young fish school tightly around the guarding parent as rising water carries them into flooded forest or savanna.

### Reproduction and life history
Spawning takes place in the rainy season. Males, which develop red coloration when breeding, dig nests about 50–60 cm across and 15–20 cm deep in shallow water, and a female lays thousands of eggs. Males guard the young for up to about three months. Both sexes carry gland-like tissue on the head that releases a secretion, and a proteome study found that this cephalic fluid changes during parental care; it is used for communication with the young and may partly feed the fry. Fish reach maturity at 4–5 years and about 1.6–1.85 m.

### Conservation
*Arapaima gigas* is listed as **Data Deficient** on the IUCN Red List. Overfishing has caused severe local declines in parts of the Brazilian Amazon, and a 2020 review called the species endangered in its native range in Brazil while invasive in Indonesia. Community-based management has produced striking recoveries: at the Mamirauá reserve, fishers counting arapaima at their breathing rises helped the population grow about nine-fold, from roughly 2,200 to 20,650 fish, between 1999 and 2006. Similar recoveries have been reported on the Juruá and Purus rivers.

### Special features
- Obligate air breathing through a modified, lung-like swim bladder.
- Large scales with a hard mineralized surface over a flexible collagen base.
- Toothed "bony tongue" and palate for crushing prey.
- Suction feeding that pulls prey in within a fraction of a second.
- Head glands that release a secretion used in parental care.
- Red breeding coloration in males.

### Human interaction
Arapaima are among the most valuable food fishes of the Amazon and are farmed and exported. Their need to surface makes them easy targets for fishers, which drove declines, but it also makes them easy to count, the basis of successful community management with fishing quotas.

### Genuine fun facts
- In lab tests, a piranha tooth broke before it could get through an arapaima scale.
- Arapaima must breathe air and surface about every 10–20 minutes.
- Fishers at Mamirauá count arapaima by watching them rise to breathe, and the managed population grew about **nine-fold** in eight years.
- Male arapaima guard their young for up to about three months.
- Glands on the parents' heads release a fluid used to communicate with the fry.
- Until 2013 all arapaima were treated as one species; a revision showed there are several.

### Concise site-ready summary
The arapaima is a giant air-breathing fish of Amazon floodplain lakes, reaching over 2 m and 100 kg. It ambushes fish with a sudden vacuum-like gulp and crushes them on a bony, toothed tongue. Its scales are so tough that piranha teeth fail against them, but it must surface to breathe every 10–20 minutes and has little offensive power against large animals.

### Rich narrative profile
In a warm, still Amazon lake at low water, the oxygen can run so low that most large fish cannot cope. The arapaima can. Every ten to twenty minutes it rises, gulps air with an audible splash and sinks again. Between breaths it hangs near the surface or among flooded trees, waiting for smaller fish to come close. Then its huge mouth opens so fast that water and prey rush in, and the prey is crushed against the toothed "bony tongue" and palate.

The arapaima's scales are one of the best-studied natural armors. Engineers testing them found a hard mineralized outer layer over a flexible base of collagen fibres stacked like plywood, and a piranha tooth driven into a scale broke before it could get through. Those scales let a large fish share its lakes with shoals of piranhas. They are not proof against everything: caimans share the same lakes, and a crushing crocodilian bite or a fisher's spear is a different problem from a piranha's cutting teeth.

In ABS terms the arapaima is a large, well-armored fish with modest weapons. Its Defense is solid because its armor is scientifically demonstrated, its Attack is low for its size because suction feeding does little to big opponents, and its Special reflects air breathing and remarkable parental care. Its story also includes one of conservation's success stories, as fishing communities that count the fish at their breathing rises have helped several populations recover.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Maximum size:** FishBase's 450 cm maximum and 3 m-plus claims are historical; modern large fish are about 2–3 m. Canonical 210 cm follows the common length.
- **Mass:** no measured length-weight relationship was recovered; 100 kg is a judgment within the published range.
- **Taxonomy:** after the 2013 revision, some "*A. gigas*" data may refer to other *Arapaima* species.
- **Lifespan:** only tertiary captive figures were found.
- **Conservation:** IUCN lists Data Deficient, while regional reviews describe severe local declines and strong managed recoveries.
- **Surfacing interval:** sources give 5–20 minutes; 10–20 minutes is used here.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Piranha (1.2 kg; 37.0 / 20.0 / 82.0 / 55.0 / 59.0 / 67.0):** the arapaima is about 80 times heavier and its scales defeat piranha teeth in testing, so its Defense is far higher. - **Versus Electric Eel (15 kg; 49.0 / 29.0 / 66.0 / 58.0 / 63.0 / 98.0):** both are air-breathing Amazon fish, but the eel's high-voltage attack gives it higher Attack and a far higher Special. The arapaima's mass and armor give it much higher Defense.
- **Versus Bull Shark (111 kg; Attack 72.0, Defense 52.0):** similar mass, but the shark's cutting teeth make it far more dangerous; the arapaima's scale armor gives it slightly higher Defense.
- **Versus Black Caiman (300 kg; Attack 87.0):** the caiman shares its lakes and can crush through scale armor; the arapaima is well below it in every force category.
- **Anti-inflation check:** fame as a "giant fish" does not raise Attack. Scale armor is counted in Defense and Protection, not again in Special.
