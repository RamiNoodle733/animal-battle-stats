# Gaur (*Bos gaurus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Gaur (also called Indian bison, although it is a wild ox, not a bison)
- **Scientific name:** *Bos gaurus* C. H. Smith, 1827
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild gaur of South and Southeast Asia, not the domestic gayal or mithun (*Bos frontalis*), which was bred from gaur stock and is sometimes filed under the same accounts.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male (bull) of about **1,000 kg** and **190 cm** shoulder height, with fully grown horns and dorsal ridge. This is a representative prime bull, not a record animal.
- **Sex choice:** males are roughly a quarter larger and heavier than females, carry thicker horns and a much taller dorsal ridge and dewlap, and are the sex that fights for breeding access. Females typically weigh around 600–700 kg.
- **Population variation:** Indian gaur are the best-studied and hold most of the world population; Southeast Asian and Malayan gaur populations are smaller and more fragmented. Reported maximum masses up to 1,500 kg should not be used as the representative baseline.

- **Canonical:** **1,000.0 kg** mature bull, the top of the handbook species range and the bottom of the reported male range.
- **Confidence:** Moderate; published ranges differ in whether they describe typical bulls or maxima.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The gaur's weapon system is a tonne of muscle driving a pair of thick, upcurved horns. Bulls hook sideways and upward with the horns rather than meeting head-on like a Cape buffalo; the heavy frontal ridge between the horns braces the strike. Mass, shoving and trampling add damage. The combination is lethal to people: at a tertiary hospital in Manipal, Karnataka, gaur caused more significant and life-threatening injuries than any other wild mammal in the surrounding region.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Gaur live in forested hills and move well on steep, broken ground and through undergrowth. They can charge quickly, but there is no reliable top-speed figure, and a tonne of body mass limits sharp turning. Agility is modest on the full roster.

### Endurance
Gaur graze and browse across forest, bamboo and grassland mosaics and range between feeding areas and water. They are most active in the cooler parts of the day and rest in shade, and in disturbed areas they shift toward night activity. Heat limits sustained exertion.

### Senses
Gaur rely strongly on smell and hearing in dense forest and are vigilant; herds react quickly to alarm. No quantitative sensory data were located.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior is typical of a wild bovid: vigilance, retreat into cover, herd bunching and, when cornered or surprised, a direct charge. Bulls settle rank largely through lateral display, turning side-on to show their height and bulk, which avoids many damaging fights.

### Sociality and predator defense
Herds of about 2 to 40 animals form around adult females and young, usually with one breeding bull; other bulls move between herds or live alone. The tiger is the only regular predator of adults. In Thailand's Huai Kha Khaeng, a study of 24 collared tigers recovered mandibles from 82 gaur at kill sites; the mean weight of gaur killed was about **398 kg**, showing tigers take mostly young and female animals but also adults. Tigers are also occasionally killed by gaur and water buffalo.

### Fighting and aggression
Bull contests escalate from lateral displays to horn clashes and shoving. Gaur are generally shy and retreat from people and predators when they can, but they charge when surprised at close range. Human-gaur conflict is rising in southern India: records from Tamil Nadu and the Nilgiris show crop damage, dozens of human injuries and several deaths over multi-year periods.

### Environmental strengths
- Forested hills, bamboo thickets and grassland edges.
- Steep, broken terrain that slows heavier-footed opponents.
- Dense cover that suits a close-range surprise charge.

### Weaknesses
- No true armor; flanks, belly and hindquarters are soft targets.
- Heat limits prolonged exertion.
- Mostly avoids a fight, giving an ambush predator the first move.
- A tonne of mass limits sharp turning.
- Tigers do kill adult gaur.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** medium and large predators forced into frontal contact; similar-sized herbivores with weaker horns.
- **Mixed:** big cats that can seize the neck from the side; other tonne-class bovids.
- **Bad:** elephants and rhinoceroses with a large mass advantage; aquatic or aerial opponents it cannot reach.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Hooking Horn Sweep** — bulls strike sideways and upward with thick, upcurved horns braced by a broad frontal ridge, backed by a tonne of body mass.
2. **Lateral Size Display** — bulls turn side-on to show their full height and ridge, settling many contests and deterring threats before contact.

### Unique traits
1. **Largest Wild Cattle** — the gaur is the biggest wild bovid alive, with bulls around a tonne and up to 2.2 m at the shoulder.
2. **White-Stockinged Giant** — a glossy dark coat, pale lower legs, a tall dorsal ridge over the shoulders and a hanging dewlap give bulls a distinctive silhouette.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Gaur live in South and Southeast Asia, from India and Nepal through Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Vietnam and Peninsular Malaysia. They use evergreen and deciduous forest, bamboo, forest glades and nearby grasslands, often in hilly country. About 85% of the population is in India.

### Diet and ecology
Gaur graze on grasses and browse leaves, shoots, bamboo, bark and fruit. As large herbivores they shape forest-edge vegetation and are major prey for tigers, which rely heavily on them in some reserves.

### Social structure
Herds of about 2 to 40 animals centre on females and young, typically with one breeding bull; bulls may move between herds during the breeding season or live alone. Bulls establish rank mainly by lateral display rather than all-out fights.

### Reproduction and life history
Females usually bear a single calf. Calves are protected within the herd and are the age class most often taken by predators. Bulls take several years to reach full body size, horn thickness and ridge height.

### Conservation
Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, a status held since 1986. The 2016 assessment estimated roughly 6,000–21,000 mature individuals, with declines of more than 70% in parts of the range over three generations. Populations are stable or recovering in well-protected Indian reserves but face poaching, habitat loss, disease from domestic cattle, uncontrolled fire, invasive plants and disturbance elsewhere.

### Special features
- Largest living wild bovid.
- Thick, upcurved horns in both sexes, 60–115 cm long.
- Tall dorsal ridge over the shoulders and a prominent dewlap in bulls.
- Pale "stockings" on the lower legs.
- Lateral display used to settle rank.

### Human interaction
Gaur are shy but increasingly enter farmland and hill towns in southern India, where encounters cause crop losses, injuries and deaths. The domestic gayal or mithun of northeast India and Myanmar was bred from gaur stock.

### Genuine fun facts
- Gaur are the largest wild cattle species in the world.
- Despite the nickname "Indian bison", gaur are wild oxen and not bison.
- Both bulls and cows carry horns up to about 115 cm long.
- A Thai study of kills by 24 collared tigers found the average gaur killed weighed about 398 kg, well under half a prime bull's mass.
- Gaur have been recorded killing tigers, one of very few prey animals to do so.
- Near Manipal in Karnataka, gaur caused more serious injuries to people than any other wild mammal.
- Bulls often size each other up side-on instead of fighting straight away.

### Concise site-ready summary
The gaur is the largest wild cattle species on Earth, a tonne-class forest bull armed with thick upcurved horns and wrapped in heavy muscle beneath a towering dorsal ridge. Usually shy, it becomes a formidable, horn-hooking brawler when surprised or cornered, strong enough to kill a tiger on occasion but still vulnerable to one.

### Rich narrative profile
A mature gaur bull is an imposing animal: close to two metres at the shoulder, around a tonne in weight, glossy near-black with white socks, and topped by a high muscular ridge that runs from the shoulders into the back. Its horns, present in both sexes, sweep up and inward from a broad, pale forehead. Everything about the silhouette advertises mass, and bulls use that silhouette deliberately, turning side-on to rivals so their full size is visible.

Most of the time gaur avoid trouble. They graze and browse in forest glades and hillside grasslands, retreat into cover at the first sign of danger, and in disturbed areas shift their activity into the night. When a threat is too close to escape, the response is a fast, heavy charge ending in hooking horn strikes. That reaction is why gaur injure more people than any other wild mammal around one Karnataka hospital, and why tigers usually target calves, cows and weakened animals rather than prime bulls.

For ABS calibration the gaur sits at the top of the wild-cattle group: heavier than the Cape Buffalo and Bison, with slightly higher Raw Power and Attack, but with a less aggressive temperament than the Cape Buffalo and no fused horn boss. It remains well below rhinoceros and elephant scale in size, power and defense, and it has no special mechanism beyond size, horns and display.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** sources range from 650–1,000 kg (species) to 1,000–1,500 kg (males). The canonical 1,000 kg sits where they meet.
- **Speed:** 56 km/h figures come from popular pages without method; rejected.
- **Lifespan:** summaries conflict (20–25 vs 25–30 years); 25 years is a compromise at low-moderate confidence.
- **ADW account:** the ADW page is filed under *Bos frontalis*, which covers the domestic gayal; wild-gaur figures from it are treated cautiously.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Cape Buffalo (750 kg):** the gaur is about a third heavier, so Raw Power (81.0 vs 74.0), Size and Attack (78.0 vs 76.0) sit slightly higher. The Cape Buffalo keeps higher Weaponry (74.0 vs 72.0) and Ferocity (82.0 vs 70.0) for its fused boss and readier counter-attack.
- **Versus Bison (900 kg):** similar body scale; gaur gets slightly more Raw Power and Attack for its extra mass and larger horns, while Bison keeps higher Agility (64.0 vs 56.0) and Stamina (74.0 vs 69.0) on its documented pivoting and cold-plains endurance.
- **Versus Yak:** the yak has no research report yet; its live values are legacy placeholders and are not used as anchors. A wild yak bull is broadly similar in mass, so the two should end up close in Attack and Defense, with the yak ahead on high-altitude Stamina.
- **Versus Rhinoceros and Black Rhinoceros:** both remain above the gaur in Attack and Defense (86.0/82.0 and 82.0/76.0) because of greater mass and horn-first body plans.
- **Versus Siberian Tiger (Attack 84.0):** the tiger keeps higher Attack as a dedicated killer; the gaur's higher Defense (72.0 vs 61.0) reflects that prime bulls are hard to kill and occasionally kill tigers.
