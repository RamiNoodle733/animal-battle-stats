# Nile Crocodile (*Crocodylus niloticus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Nile Crocodile
- **Scientific name:** *Crocodylus niloticus* Laurenti, 1768, family Crocodylidae
- **Exact animal represented:** the Nile crocodile of eastern and southern Africa and Madagascar. West and Central African populations now treated as the separate West African crocodile (*Crocodylus suchus*) are not used to set this profile; the San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance fact sheet still groups both names, so older sources can mix them.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **400.0 kg** and **450.0 cm total length**, representing a large fighting-age bull rather than a record giant.
- **Sex choice:** size is strongly sexually dimorphic. Species accounts put adult males at roughly 3.5–5 m and 225–750 kg, with females usually about 30% smaller. SeaWorld gives adult females an average near 3.5 m and 50–350 kg. Males are the larger, territorial fighting sex.
- **Population variation:** body size varies with region, age and food supply. A 322-animal morphometric study at Lake St Lucia and Ndumo (KwaZulu-Natal, the southern range limit) found male-biased adult sex ratios and clear sexual size dimorphism, and noted that the length-to-mass relationship is logarithmic, so mass climbs steeply in the longest males. Exceptional males above 6 m and near 1,000 kg exist but are not canonical.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Species accounts and the IUCN Crocodile Specialist Group (CSG) summary: large adult males about **225–750 kg**; exceptional old males approach **1,000 kg**.
- SeaWorld: adult males typically **400–500 kg**, up to about 900 kg.
- San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance library: body weight **70–100 kg** with adults up to 1,000 kg reported. This low figure appears to describe smaller or pooled animals and conflicts with every male-specific source, so it is not used for the canonical male.
- **Canonical:** **400.0 kg**. This sits in the upper-middle of the adult-male range, below SeaWorld's upper figure and well below record animals, and deliberately below the 500 kg saltwater crocodile canonical because salties are the larger species on average.
- **Confidence:** Moderate. Mass rises steeply with length, so any single number carries wide uncertainty.

### Length
- Species accounts / CSG: adult males commonly **3.5–5.0 m**; females roughly 2.4–3.8 m.
- SeaWorld: adult males average about 5 m and can reach 6.1 m.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 450.0 cm** total length (snout to tail tip), consistent with a 400 kg male.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Height
No standardized standing-height convention exists for crocodilians, and posture changes between belly-crawl and high walk. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0** (unresolved/inapplicable), matching the saltwater crocodile and alligator reports.

### Speed
- SeaWorld reports short bursts of about **14 km/h** on land during surprise attacks and swimming at **30–35 km/h** "for extended periods". The sustained swimming claim is not supported by any measured study found in this run and is at odds with crocodilian burst physiology.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (unresolved). No instrumented maximum was located, and the roster's other crocodilians also leave this field unresolved rather than adopting popular figures.

### Lifespan
- SeaWorld: believed average lifespan **70–100 years**. Wild ageing of large crocodiles is difficult, and this figure is a zoological summary rather than a demographic estimate.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 70.0**, the low end of the only cited range and the same value used for the saltwater crocodile.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Bite and dentition
- Erickson et al. (2012) directly measured bite forces in mature adults of all living crocodilian species, including Nile crocodiles. The highest value in that study, **16,414 N**, came from a 4.59 m saltwater crocodile. A Nile-specific maximum force was not recovered in this run's search results.
- Popular claims of a "5,000 psi" Nile crocodile bite and articles stating that a Nile crocodile bite equals "3,700 psi" mix up the saltwater crocodile record, force and pressure. They are not adopted.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Force is not pressure; no Nile-specific pressure measurement with a stated contact area was found.
- Teeth are conical gripping teeth built to seize and hold, not chew. Large prey is torn apart by head shaking and body rolling.

### Defensive structure
Bony plates (osteoderms) reinforce the skin along the back, the skull is massive, and the body is low and heavy. The belly, eyes, limb joints and throat are less protected. This is strong partial armor, not a shell.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The jaws are the main weapon. A Nile crocodile lies almost fully submerged with only its eyes, ears and nostrils showing, closes the distance with a short tail-driven lunge, seizes with conical teeth and then drags the victim into deeper water to drown it. Large prey is broken up by jerking the head from side to side or by rolling the whole body. Species accounts list zebra, buffalo, warthog, wildebeest, impala, baboon and hyena as adult prey, with the largest males occasionally taking giraffe, young hippos or lions. Fish, especially catfish and barbel, still make up most of the diet in many areas.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
In water the muscular tail provides powerful propulsion and the animal can change depth and attack angle with strong whole-body control. On land it can high-walk and burst forward over short distances, but it is not a pursuit animal. Maneuverability is therefore moderate: effective at the shoreline, limited in open terrain.

### Endurance
Like other crocodilians, the Nile crocodile runs on a low-cost ectothermic budget. It can wait submerged for long periods and go long intervals between large meals, but maximal effort is short-lived. Sustained high-output fighting is a weakness.

### Senses
SeaWorld describes excellent eyesight by day and night. Leitch and Catania showed that crocodilian integumentary sensory organs, studied in Nile crocodiles and spectacled caimans, respond to touch, pressure and vibration with sensitivity that National Geographic summarized as greater than human fingertips. Nile crocodiles carry these organs over the whole body. The result is strong detection of surface ripples and prey movement even in murky water or darkness.

### Intelligence and tactics
The Nile crocodile shows more coordinated behavior than its solitary image suggests. At Lake St Lucia, groups have been reported lining up across a narrow channel to intercept migrating fish, and several crocodiles may hold a large carcass so others can tear pieces off. Dinets reviewed such reports as apparent coordination in cooperatively hunting crocodilians. At the Mara River crossings, crocodiles wait for wildebeest to commit to the water. This supports moderate tactical flexibility, not mammal-level problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Adults tolerate one another at productive sites and form size-based dominance hierarchies. Big males defend space and access to females. Group feeding occurs around large carcasses and fish runs. The PNAS Mara River study estimated that crocodiles in the main drowning reach eat about 150 wildebeest over the migration period, only about 2% of the carcasses entering the river, showing that much of their intake at crossings is scavenged or opportunistic.

### Fighting and aggression
Intraspecific aggression is common. A zoo study of an all-male group found that agonism was not reduced by visual barriers, showing how persistent male-male conflict can be. In the wild, large males fight with jaw strikes, bites and body shoves, and dominance tracks size. The species is widely reported as a frequent attacker of people across Africa.

### Environmental strengths
- Rivers, lakes, swamps and floodplains with murky water.
- Drinking points and river crossings where prey must enter the water.
- Deep water next to the bank, where a seized animal can be pulled under.
- Warm climates that keep ectothermic performance high.

### Weaknesses
- Softer belly, eyes, throat and limb joints.
- Short limbs and poor dry-land pursuit.
- Offense depends on landing the first bite.
- Limited sustained high-output effort.
- A failed first strike against a large horned or tusked animal exposes it to heavy counterattack.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** medium and large terrestrial animals that must approach water; opponents with poor swimming ability; soft-skinned prey vulnerable to a seize-and-drown attack.
- **Mixed:** heavily built horned or tusked herbivores when contact happens in shallow water; large predators that engage at the bank.
- **Bad:** much larger aquatic megafauna such as adult hippos, fast opponents that stay away from water, and fights on open dry ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Crossing-Point Ambush** — waits almost fully submerged at drinking points and river crossings, lunges, seizes and drags prey into deep water to drown it. This turns the shoreline into a trap for large land animals, as seen at the Mara River wildebeest crossings.
2. **Fish-Dam Cooperation** — groups have been reported lining up across narrow channels to intercept migrating fish and holding large carcasses so others can tear off pieces. In a battle this mainly adds tactical options near water rather than raw force.

### Unique traits
1. **Africa's Heavyweight Reptile** — the largest crocodilian in Africa, with big males reaching roughly 225–750 kg and exceptional animals approaching a tonne.
2. **Fingertip-Grade Jaw Touch** — dome-shaped sensory organs in the skin, studied directly in Nile crocodiles, detect pressure and vibration with sensitivity greater than human fingertips, helping the animal find and handle prey in murky water.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Nile crocodiles live in rivers, lakes, swamps, marshes and floodplains across much of sub-Saharan Africa, especially eastern and southern Africa, and in Madagascar. They also enter brackish estuaries and coastal lakes such as Lake St Lucia, helped by salt glands on the tongue. West African populations are now usually treated as a separate species.

### Diet and ecology
Hatchlings eat insects, crustaceans, amphibians and small fish. Prey size increases with growth. Adults eat mostly fish, especially catfish and barbel, plus birds, reptiles and mammals ranging from impala and warthog to wildebeest, zebra and buffalo. Carrion is taken readily. Because their metabolism is slow, big crocodiles can gorge during events such as migration crossings and then go a long time between large meals.

### Social structure
Adults are not territorial in every setting, but large males defend space and mating access, and dominance follows size. Crocodiles gather in numbers where food is concentrated, such as fish runs and river crossings, and can feed together on large carcasses.

### Reproduction and life history
Females dig nests in sandy banks and guard them. Sex is set by nest temperature: at Lake St Lucia males develop at about 31.7–34.5 °C, and females at temperatures below and above that range. Hatchling and juvenile mortality is high. Males take longer than females to reach large adult size.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List because it remains widespread and abundant across African wetlands. One widely repeated estimate puts the global population at about 50,000–70,000 mature individuals. Local populations still face habitat loss, pollution, persecution and conflict with people, and a Zimbabwe review found status varies considerably between water systems.

### Special features
- Eyes, ears and nostrils set high on the head for near-total submersion.
- Dorsal osteoderms and a massive skull.
- Powerful laterally flattened tail for swimming and lunging.
- Whole-body integumentary sensory organs for touch and vibration.
- Salt glands on the tongue for brackish water.
- Conical replacement teeth for seizing and holding.

### Human interaction
Nile crocodiles are widely reported to cause more attacks on people than most other crocodilians, mainly where people fish, wash or collect water at the shoreline. Management in many countries combines protection, regulated harvest and farming, and removal of problem animals.

### Genuine fun facts
- A study of the Mara River estimated that crocodiles in the main drowning reach eat about **150 wildebeest** during the migration, roughly **2%** of the carcasses entering the river.
- Research on Nile crocodiles found their skin sensory organs are more sensitive to touch than human fingertips.
- Nest temperature decides a hatchling's sex; at Lake St Lucia males come from a narrow middle temperature band.
- Groups of crocodiles have been reported forming a living barrier across a 550 m channel at Lake St Lucia to catch migrating fish.
- Crocodiles in a zoo all-male group kept fighting even when visual barriers blocked their view of each other.
- The tongue carries salt glands, letting the species use brackish water despite being mainly a freshwater animal.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Nile crocodile is Africa's largest reptile and a shoreline ambush specialist. A 400 kg adult male combines a crushing crocodilian bite, gripping teeth, armored back, explosive tail-driven lunges and the seize-drag-drown tactic that makes river crossings deadly. It dominates at the water's edge but tires quickly and is far less dangerous on open dry ground.

### Rich narrative profile
A big male Nile crocodile fights on its own terms. It spends most of its time nearly invisible, only the eyes, ears and nostrils breaking the surface, while sensory organs across its skin pick up ripples and footsteps at the water's edge. When an antelope, zebra or wildebeest lowers its head to drink or commits to a crossing, the crocodile converts tail thrust and 400 kg of body into a short, violent lunge. Once the jaws close, the fight moves into the water, where the crocodile holds, drags, rolls and waits for the victim to drown.

This species is also more social than its reputation suggests. Crocodiles gather wherever food concentrates, jostle by size, and have been reported working together: lining up across a channel to catch fish, or anchoring a carcass so others can tear it apart. Those behaviors give it tactical options that a purely solitary ambusher would lack, though they do not approach the planning of social mammals.

In ABS terms the Nile crocodile is an asymmetric specialist. It earns elite Attack and very strong Defense from its bite, mass and armor, sitting just below the heavier saltwater crocodile and well above the American alligator. Its Agility and Stamina stay moderate because ectothermic muscles are built for bursts, not long fights, and because it cannot chase or maneuver well on land. Keep it near water and it is among the most dangerous animals on the roster; pull the fight onto open ground and much of that danger disappears.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** male-specific ranges (225–750 kg) and SeaWorld's 400–500 kg typical male disagree with the SDZWA sheet's 70–100 kg figure. Canonical 400 kg follows the male-specific sources.
- **Speed:** SeaWorld's 30–35 km/h sustained swimming claim is implausible for a burst-built ectotherm and is not adopted; the field stays 0.0.
- **Bite:** no Nile-specific maximum force was recovered in this run, and popular PSI figures confuse force with pressure. `0.0` means unresolved, not weak.
- **Lifespan:** 70–100 years is a zoo summary estimate; wild ages are hard to verify.
- **Cooperative hunting:** reports of fish barriers and shared carcass handling are real observations but partly anecdotal; they support moderate tactics, not high intelligence.
- **Taxonomy:** older sources that include West African populations may blend in the smaller *C. suchus*.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Saltwater Crocodile (500 kg; 92.0 / 83.0 / 52.0 / 46.0 / 58.0 / 80.0):** the Nile crocodile uses the same weapon system on a smaller canonical body, so Attack, Defense, Raw Power and Weaponry sit a few points lower. Intelligence is slightly higher because group fish barriers and shared carcass handling are documented, while the saltie keeps higher Special for salt tolerance and current-assisted ocean travel.
- **Versus Alligator (200 kg; 84.0 / 77.0 / 49.0 / 40.0 / 51.0 / 72.0):** twice the canonical mass and a larger bite system put the Nile crocodile clearly above the alligator in Attack, Defense and Raw Power, with similar locomotor limits.
- **Versus Hippopotamus (1,800 kg; Attack 90.0, Defense 84.0):** the hippo keeps slightly higher Attack and Defense because of its far greater mass and tusks. The crocodile's Weaponry and Special are higher because its bite-and-drown system and sensory skin are more specialized.
- **Versus Green Anaconda (50 kg):** both are water-edge ambushers, but the crocodile's mass, armor and bite place it far above the anaconda in Attack and Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Attack 89.0 is not maximum-tier; it stays below the saltwater crocodile and hippo. Stamina 44.0 reflects burst physiology rather than fame. Ferocity is high because of documented male aggression and attacks on people, not because the species is feared.
