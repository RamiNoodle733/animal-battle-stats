# Okapi (*Okapia johnstoni*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Roster label:** Okapi
- **Exact taxon:** *Okapia johnstoni* (Sclater, 1901), family Giraffidae. Living species endemic to the Democratic Republic of the Congo.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime mature **male**, **275.0 kg**, about **150.0 cm shoulder height** and **250.0 cm body length**. Males are selected despite females being larger because males are the territorial/fighting sex and alone bear developed ossicones.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance gives males **200-300 kg** and females **225-350 kg**. ADW independently identifies females as larger. Males possess short skin-covered ossicones; females generally lack developed ossicones. Chester Zoo gives male ossicones about **10-15 cm**.
- **Population uncertainty:** wild okapi are difficult to measure because of dense forest habitat and security constraints. Institutional ranges are therefore more useful than pretending a precise wild population mean is known.

## 2. Physical measurements and morphology

### Mass, height and length
San Diego Zoo reports adults about **1.5 m tall**, males **200-300 kg** and females **225-350 kg**. ADW reports an overall **210-350 kg** mass range. National Geographic gives about **8 ft (2.44 m)** length and **5 ft (1.52 m)** height. The canonical 275 kg male is near the upper part of the ordinary male range but not a record.

### Speed
No primary or high-quality species-specific instrumented maximum running speed was recovered. Popular web claims near 37 mph are not accepted as a physiological measurement. **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0 pending defensible direct evidence.** This does not mean the animal cannot run quickly.

### Lifespan
San Diego Zoo gives **17 years average**. Chester Zoo gives **15-20 years**, with about 30 possible in human care, while Sacramento Zoo notes a captive record of 33. **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 17.0**, representing ordinary adult life history rather than a captive maximum.

### Ossicones, hooves and other weapons
Males bear short skin-covered bony ossicones, generally **10-15 cm**. EAZA husbandry guidance specifically describes aggressive **headbanging** and warns of serious injury to male ossicones during introductions. The legs and hooves provide the larger mechanical weapon system. As a 275 kg giraffid, an okapi can kick and shove effectively, but no direct okapi kick-force measurement was found, so no force is invented.

### Bite
The okapi is a browser with no specialized predatory bite. No defensible species-specific bite pressure measurement was found. **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.**

### Defenses
A robust ungulate body, long legs, hooves, alert hearing, oily water-shedding coat, forest camouflage and avoidance behavior are the main defenses. The skin is not armor. National Geographic identifies leopard as the principal natural predator.

## 4. Combat biology

### Offensive mechanics
The okapi is not a dedicated fighter like a bovid with large horns. Its practical offense comes from a heavy ungulate body, long legs and hooves, close-range shoving, and male head strikes involving the skull and short ossicones. EAZA guidance confirms headbanging as real aggressive behavior during introductions. Ossicones add a hard contact point but are short and less dangerous than long horns or antlers.

### Defensive adaptations and durability
At 275 kg, the male has meaningful mass and skeletal robustness, but lacks thick armor, massive horns, tusks or a protective shell. Dense-forest camouflage and early detection are more important than absorbing damage. Leopard predation demonstrates that adult-sized okapi are not beyond the reach of large predators.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Long legs permit efficient terrestrial movement through forest trails. Okapis routinely use established paths and must negotiate dense understory, supporting good balance and obstacle navigation. Their build is less specialized for open-ground acceleration and turning than small cursorial predators, and no unverified top-speed claim is allowed to inflate Agility.

### Endurance and activity
San Diego Zoo reports adult males can travel up to **4 km/day**, with home ranges spanning about **10 km**. Okapis consume roughly **18-29 kg of browse daily** and are active particularly from afternoon into evening. This supports solid sustained low-to-moderate activity, not elite pursuit endurance.

### Senses
The large mobile ears can rotate independently, allowing useful directional hearing in dense forest. Vision and smell are also important, but the strongest documented sensory specialization is hearing. Interdigital scent glands on all four feet contribute to chemical communication.

### Intelligence and tactics
Okapis navigate stable forest trail networks, maintain territories/home ranges and use scent communication. EAZA husbandry material documents individual behavioral flexibility during introductions. Evidence does not justify primate- or cetacean-level problem solving, so Intelligence remains moderate.

### Fighting and social behavior
Adults are mostly solitary. San Diego Zoo reports males maintain large ranges and attempt to exclude other males while tolerating females. EAZA guidelines document aggressive headbanging during introductions and dominance changes after separation/reintroduction. Combat is therefore primarily intrasexual territorial conflict, not cooperative group fighting.

### Predation and predator defense
The okapi is herbivorous and does not hunt. Against predators it benefits from concealment, acute hearing, forest familiarity, flight, body mass and potentially dangerous hooves. National Geographic identifies leopards as the chief natural predator.

### Environmental strengths
Dense rainforest favors its camouflage, large ears, trail knowledge and maneuvering familiarity. Wet conditions suit the oily coat. Vegetation can reduce a pursuing predator's clean approach.

### Environmental limitations
Open terrain removes much of the camouflage advantage. Short ossicones have limited reach. The long legs can be vulnerable to destabilization or limb injury, and the animal lacks heavy frontal armor.

### Major weaknesses
No long-reach horn/tusk system, no armor, no predatory bite, solitary combat style, and comparatively limited offensive specialization for a 275 kg ungulate.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Strong against:** much smaller terrestrial animals unable to absorb kicks or exploit the okapi's long-legged geometry.
- **Competitive against:** similarly sized lightly armed herbivores, especially in dense terrain where movement familiarity matters.
- **Weak against:** large cats that secure a neck/body attack, heavily horned bovids/antelopes, bears, great apes at close grappling range, and megafauna with much greater mass.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Rainforest Radar** - very large independently mobile ears improve directional detection in visually obstructed forest, helping the okapi detect threats before contact.
2. **Ossicone Headbang** - mature males use the head in aggressive encounters; short bony skin-covered ossicones add a hard contact structure to close-range territorial clashes.

### Unique traits
1. **Forest Giraffid Frame** - a compact giraffid body combines long legs, elevated browsing reach and dense-forest mobility without the extreme height of a giraffe.
2. **Striped Understory Camouflage** - contrasting leg and rump striping breaks up the body outline in dappled rainforest vegetation, supporting concealment rather than armor.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and geographic range
Okapis are endemic to central and northeastern Democratic Republic of the Congo. IUCN's conservation strategy identifies them as a tropical-rainforest endemic; zoo and specialist sources emphasize dense lowland/closed-canopy forest.

### Diet and ecology
A selective browser eating leaves, twigs, buds, fruits, ferns and fungi. San Diego Zoo reports about **18-29 kg of food per day** and notes clay consumption, likely for minerals. The long dark prehensile tongue strips foliage and also assists grooming.

### Social structure
Predominantly solitary. Females associate with dependent calves. Adult males maintain larger ranges and attempt to exclude other males, while allowing females to pass through.

### Reproduction and life history
Gestation is roughly **14-16 months** and usually produces one calf. Calves use a hiding strategy early in life and can delay defecation for weeks, reducing scent cues near hiding sites. Sexual maturity occurs around two to three years in zoo summaries, while full adult size can take longer.

### Conservation
The okapi is **Endangered**. IUCN and major zoo conservation programs identify habitat loss, poaching/bushmeat hunting, illegal mining, human settlement and armed insecurity in the DRC as major threats. Exact wild abundance remains difficult to estimate.

### Major adaptations
Large independently mobile ears, cryptic dark coat and striped limbs, oily water-shedding fur, long prehensile tongue, long legs, male ossicones and interdigital scent glands.

### Human interaction
Okapis are conservation flagships for Congo Basin rainforest. Their restricted range overlaps regions affected by armed conflict, mining and hunting, complicating both protection and field research. Captive populations are managed cooperatively by major zoological institutions.

### Genuine fun facts
- The okapi is a giraffid, not a zebra relative, despite its striped legs.
- Females are generally larger than males, while males alone normally develop prominent ossicones.
- The ears can rotate independently.
- The tongue is prehensile and can be used to groom the face and ears.
- Calves can delay their first defecation for weeks, apparently reducing scent near hiding sites.
- Adult males can use home ranges spanning roughly 10 km and may travel about 4 km in a day.

### Concise site-ready summary
The okapi is a 275 kg forest giraffid whose combat value comes from long-legged kicking power, a sturdy body, acute hearing and male territorial head strikes. It is less heavily armed than elk or large bovids, but dense-rainforest camouflage and exceptional mobile ears make it difficult to surprise in its home terrain.

### Rich narrative profile
The okapi is built for survival in closed forest rather than open-ground dominance. Its dark body disappears into shadow while striped limbs fragment the outline among shafts of light and vegetation. Huge mobile ears continuously sample a habitat where visibility is short, and established trail networks let a solitary animal move efficiently through dense cover.

A mature male is still a formidable physical object. At roughly 275 kg it can shove, kick and use its head in territorial clashes. EAZA husbandry guidance is particularly useful because it confirms headbanging as genuine aggressive behavior rather than an inferred giraffid analogy. Yet the short ossicones do not provide the reach or penetrating geometry of an antelope's long horns, and the okapi lacks armor. Its battle profile therefore stays below a similarly sized elk in Attack while preserving meaningful absolute power.

The species' best defense is to detect and avoid danger before a clean engagement. Leopards remain natural predators, demonstrating the limits of body mass and concealment once a large cat achieves good contact. ABS ratings consequently separate a strong sensory/forest toolkit from raw durability and weaponry.

### Future field proposals
- Collect standardized wild adult male/female mass, shoulder-height and body-length distributions.
- Obtain instrumented sprint measurements before assigning nonzero `speed_mps`.
- Quantify hoof strike force and head-impact mechanics in mature males.
- Measure ossicone dimensions by age and sex from museum/zoo datasets.
- Compare leopard-avoidance behavior and escape success across forest structure.
- Quantify hearing thresholds/localization accuracy rather than relying only on ear morphology.

### Confidence and source conflicts
- **Mass/height:** high confidence; multiple institutions agree closely. Female-biased size is explicit, but male fighting morphology/territoriality drives specimen choice.

- **Lifespan:** high for managed populations, lower for wild maximum; 17 years is used as representative rather than 30-33 year captive extremes.
- **Combat force:** low direct quantitative evidence. Headbanging is documented, but kick/head impact forces are not, so no invented Newton or PSI values appear.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes
- **Versus Elk:** the researched 320 kg bull elk has Attack 62.0. Okapi Attack **53.0** stays materially lower because elk carries large antlers optimized for male combat, while okapi ossicones are short and its primary offense is hoof/body contact.
- **Versus Moose:** the 550 kg bull moose at Attack 69.0 and Defense 50.0 remains substantially above okapi in mass, reach and weapon scale.

- **Versus Ocelot:** okapi greatly exceeds the 13 kg cat in absolute size, raw power and Defense, while Ocelot remains much more agile and tactically predatory.
