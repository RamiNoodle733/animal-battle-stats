# Oceanic Whitetip Shark (*Carcharhinus longimanus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Oceanic whitetip shark
- **Scientific name:** *Carcharhinus longimanus* (Poey, 1861)
- **Exact animal represented:** the single living oceanic whitetip species, a large requiem shark of the open tropical and warm-temperate ocean, recognised by its long, rounded, paddle-like pectoral fins with mottled white tips.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **270 cm** total length and **150 kg**, matching FishBase's common length and the "usually up to 3 m and 150 kg" size given in secondary summaries, rather than the 4 m maximum.
- **Sex choice:** both sexes mature at similar lengths (about 1.7–1.9 m per the Florida Museum; FishBase gives a length at first maturity of about 196 cm), and Animal Diversity Web describes females as generally the larger sex. Females are therefore the representative large adult.
- **Population notes:** body size has fallen where fishing pressure is heavy. In the Gulf of Mexico the mean weight of caught whitetips dropped from about 86 kg in the 1950s to about 56 kg in the 1990s, so modern catches are often smaller than a full-grown adult.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Secondary summaries give "usually up to 3 m and 150 kg" (Wikipedia, tertiary).
- IGFA all-tackle record **167.4 kg** (Florida Museum; FishBase "published weight").

- Gulf of Mexico catch means: 86.4 kg (1950s) and 56.1 kg (1990s), reflecting fished, partly immature samples.
- **Canonical:** **150.0 kg** for a 270 cm adult female.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Length
- FishBase: maximum **400 cm** TL, common length **270 cm** TL. Florida Museum: some individuals reach 3.5–4 m, but most are under 3 m.
- Maturity at about 170–196 cm, around 4–9 years depending on region (Florida Museum, FishBase, NOAA).
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 270.0** total length.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Height
- Standing height is not meaningful for a fish. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- Papastamatiou et al. (2018, *Scientific Reports*) fitted four sharks with speed and motion loggers: they cruised in constant yo-yo dives at **0.6–0.7 m/s**, with occasional high-pitch bursts up to **4.6 m/s**. One shark breached from **160 m** depth, rising vertically at about **4 m/s** before clearing the surface.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 4.6**, the highest directly measured burst.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high (instrumented, but only four animals).

### Lifespan
- NOAA Fisheries: lives **up to 25 years**; maximum age estimated at **25–36 years**.
- FishBase reported age 22 years; Animal Diversity Web gives typical wild lifespan up to 15 years and a longest known of 22.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 25.0**, following NOAA.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Bite and dentition
- Upper teeth are broad, triangular and fully serrated; lower teeth are narrower with serrated tips; about 13–15 teeth on each side of each jaw (Florida Museum, Wikipedia). The Florida Museum describes them as effective at holding and tearing prey and easily confused with bull shark teeth.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No measured or modelled bite force for this species was found.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Protection comes from denticle-covered skin, a stocky body and mass. Immature whitetips are taken by larger sharks (Florida Museum).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The oceanic whitetip's weapon is a classic requiem-shark bite: broad, fully serrated upper teeth that saw through flesh and narrower, pointed lower teeth that pin prey. The Florida Museum describes the teeth as effective for holding and tearing, and they are easily confused with bull shark teeth. It eats oceanic bony fishes, threadfins, stingrays, sea turtles, seabirds, squid, crustaceans and whale carrion (FishBase), and has been seen biting into schools of yellowfin and bigeye tuna. Sucker scars on one individual filmed off Hawaii suggest it may tackle large squid at depth.

What sets it apart is behavior. It is bold, persistent and opportunistic, investigating anything that might be food, and dominates the silky sharks it competes with at feeding events (Animal Diversity Web). No bite-force figure exists, so its offense is rated from tooth form, body mass and demonstrated prey range.

### Defense and durability
A 150 kg stocky body with tough denticle skin gives moderate absolute durability. There is no armor, and the gills, eyes and long pectoral fins are exposed. Immature whitetips are prey for larger sharks.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Whitetips usually cruise slowly with their huge pectoral fins spread wide, which Animal Diversity Web and the Florida Museum both note. Logger data show a low-cost yo-yo cruise at 0.6–0.7 m/s, but also bursts to 4.6 m/s and vertical breaches from as deep as 160 m. The long fins give lift and fine low-speed control; the stocky body turns less sharply than a mako or hammerhead.

### Endurance
Endurance is a strength built on economy. Papastamatiou et al. (2018) showed that whitetips swim close to the speed that minimises the cost of transport, keep speed constant through their dives and could meet their daily energy needs with about 1–1.5 kg of prey (roughly 1.5% of body mass). Tagged sharks from Cat Island, Bahamas, dispersed 290–1,940 km over 30–245 days (Howey-Jordan et al. 2013), and Pacific sharks moved from 140 to more than 4,000 km.

### Senses
Full shark sensory suite: smell, lateral line, vision and electroreception (Animal Diversity Web notes electroreceptors that pick up distressed or injured prey). Whitetips are known to trail pilot whales, which find squid by echolocation, and to follow boats.

### Intelligence and tactics
Whitetips show real behavioral range: slow energy-saving search, sudden bursts, surface breaches, following pilot whales to squid, trailing tuna boats and competing aggressively in mixed-species feeding frenzies. The mesopelagic dives appear to be foraging surveys (Howey et al. 2016). This is flexible opportunism rather than complex planning.

### Hunting behavior
Mostly a solitary searcher in the upper 200 m of the open ocean, spending 99.7% of its time there, with short dives (mean about 13 minutes) to as deep as 1,082 m, mostly at night (Howey-Jordan et al. 2013). It gathers with other sharks where food is concentrated.

### Intraspecific fighting and predator defense
Aggressive displays are used in competition at food. Adults have few predators beyond people and possibly the largest sharks and orcas; immature animals are more exposed.

### Aggression
Among the boldest sharks. The Florida Museum lists it as one of the more dangerous sharks to humans and advises extreme caution around divers. Recorded attacks number 21 between 1955 and 2020, five fatal (Wikipedia summary of the International Shark Attack File), and it is blamed for deaths among shipwreck survivors in open water.

### Environmental strengths
- Open, deep, warm ocean far from shelter.
- Long searches where prey is scarce and energy must be saved.
- Situations that reward persistence and repeated investigation.

### Weaknesses
- No armor; gills and eyes exposed.
- Slow routine cruise; bursts are short.
- Mass is moderate next to the largest sharks and toothed whales.
- Ectothermic muscles, unlike warm-bodied makos and white sharks.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** mid-sized fish, turtles, seabirds and weakened animals that cannot escape its persistence.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized requiem sharks and warm-bodied pelagic sharks of similar mass.
- **Bad:** larger sharks, orcas and heavily armored opponents.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Breach From the Deep** — logger data recorded a whitetip accelerating vertically from 160 m at about 4 m/s and clearing the surface, one of several breaches. It can launch a surprise strike from far below an opponent at the surface.
2. **Pilot Whale Shadowing** — whitetips trail pilot whales, which find squid by echolocation, and follow tuna boats. By riding along with better finders of food, it turns empty ocean into feeding opportunities.

### Unique traits
1. **Paddle-Fin Glider** — its very long, rounded pectoral fins spread wide while cruising, supporting a slow, low-cost search at 0.6–0.7 m/s.
2. **Open-Ocean Persistence** — a bold, hypercompetitive forager that outcompetes silky sharks at feeding frenzies and keeps investigating possible food far from land.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The oceanic whitetip lives in tropical and warm-temperate oceans worldwide, well offshore over deep water. It spends almost all its time in the upper 200 m, preferring water warmer than about 20 °C, but makes short dives into the mesopelagic zone to at least 1,082 m. It is occasionally seen near oceanic islands such as those of the Red Sea and the Bahamas.

### Diet and ecology
An opportunistic top predator. Prey includes oceanic bony fishes such as tunas and dolphinfish, threadfins, stingrays, sea turtles, seabirds, squid, gastropods, crustaceans and the carcasses of whales, and it will eat garbage thrown from ships. Pilot fish, remoras and dolphinfish often travel with it.

### Social structure
Mostly solitary, but it gathers with other sharks at concentrated food and takes part in feeding frenzies, where it dominates silky sharks.

### Reproduction and life history
Gives birth to live young. After a gestation of 10–12 months females give birth, usually every second year, to litters of 1–14 pups (average about 6), with larger females having more young (NOAA Fisheries). Pups are born at about 45–75 cm depending on region, and maturity comes at 4–9 years.

### Conservation
Listed as **Critically Endangered** on the IUCN Red List (2019), with estimated declines of more than 80% over three generations and possibly around 98% in some regions. NOAA Fisheries listed it as threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act in 2018. It is listed on CITES Appendix II. Main threats are longline, purse-seine and gillnet bycatch and demand for its large fins.

### Special features
- Very long, rounded, paddle-like pectoral fins with white tips.
- Broad, fully serrated upper teeth.
- Energy-minimising yo-yo swimming at near-optimal speed.
- Deep mesopelagic dives to at least 1,082 m.
- Electroreceptors used to find distressed or injured prey.

### Human interaction
Rarely met near shore, but divers in places such as the Red Sea and the Bahamas see it regularly. It is bold around people and boats and should be treated with caution; it has been involved in fatal incidents with swimmers and shipwreck survivors. Retention is prohibited in U.S. commercial and recreational fisheries.

### Genuine fun facts
- In 1969 two scientists wrote that it was perhaps the most abundant large animal on Earth; it is now Critically Endangered.
- One tagged whitetip breached from 160 m below the surface, rising at about 4 m/s before leaping clear.
- A 40-second breach costs about as much energy as 50 minutes of normal swimming.
- An adult may need only about 1–1.5 kg of food a day.
- Whitetips follow pilot whales to find squid.
- Its long white-tipped pectoral fins give it the Latin name *longimanus*, "long hands".
- Tagged sharks have dived to 1,082 m into water as cold as 7.75 °C.

### Concise site-ready summary
The oceanic whitetip is a bold, 150 kg open-ocean shark with long paddle-like fins and fully serrated upper teeth. It cruises slowly to save energy, then bursts or breaches from deep water, and its persistence around anything that might be food makes it one of the most dangerous sharks in blue water.

### Rich narrative profile
The oceanic whitetip lives where food is scarce and far between. A mature female of about 2.7 m and 150 kg spends almost all her time in the warm upper 200 m of the open ocean, gliding slowly on huge, rounded pectoral fins. Motion loggers show that this leisurely style is precise economy: she swims at close to the speed that costs the least energy, holds that speed through long yo-yo dives, and can live on a kilogram or so of prey a day.

That economy hides a real predator. When something promising appears, the whitetip investigates without hesitation, and it can switch from a 0.7 m/s cruise to bursts of 4.6 m/s or a vertical rush from 160 m that carries it clear of the surface. Its broad serrated upper teeth tear flesh from tunas, turtles, seabirds and whale carcasses, and it follows pilot whales and fishing boats to find food. At feeding frenzies it pushes aside silky sharks.

In a fight, the whitetip brings persistence and a good cutting bite, but no armor and only moderate mass. Similar-sized sharks are fair contests, while larger sharks and orcas outclass it. Once so common that scientists called it perhaps the most abundant large animal on Earth, it has been reduced by fin demand and bycatch to Critically Endangered status.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 150 kg follows the FishBase length-weight relationship and "usual" size summaries; the IGFA record of 167.4 kg is reported for an unusually short 2.2 m fish, so individual variation is large.
- **Lifespan:** NOAA's 25 years sits between ADW's 22-year maximum and NOAA's upper estimate of 36.
- **Bite force:** not measured; Weaponry is rated from tooth form.
- **Speed:** the 4.6 m/s burst comes from four sharks only.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Bull Shark (111 kg; A72 D52 Ag73 St69 I58 Sp82):** similar serrated dentition and similar mass. The whitetip is heavier and more persistent, but the bull shark's unusually forceful jaws keep its Attack a point higher (72.0 vs 71.0). The whitetip's energy-saving ocean travel gives it much higher Stamina (80.0 vs 69.0); the bull shark's euryhalinity keeps its Special higher (82.0 vs 76.0).
- **Versus Mako Shark (275 kg; A75 D53 Ag85 St88 I64 Sp86):** the mako is heavier, warm-bodied and far quicker (Agility 85.0 vs 70.0), so it stays above in Attack, Stamina and Special. Defense is equal (53.0) because neither has armor and both are mid-sized.
- **Versus Tiger Shark (legacy, not yet researched; A87.9 D63.6 Ag85.1 St89.5):** the tiger shark's values are legacy placeholders, not research anchors. A researched tiger shark (400–600 kg with can-opener teeth) should still sit well above the whitetip in Attack and Defense; the legacy Agility of 85.1 looks high for a heavy tiger shark and was not used.
- **Versus Great White Shark (900 kg; A88 D68 Ag73 St91 I70 Sp87):** six times heavier with larger serrated teeth and regional endothermy; stays above in every rating except that Agility is close (73.0 vs 70.0).
- **Versus Hammerhead Shark (230 kg; A74 D55 Ag84 St82):** heavier and more maneuverable; Attack and Agility stay higher. Stamina is close (82.0 vs 80.0).
- **Anti-inflation check:** Ferocity is 82.0 because boldness is well documented, but Attack stays at 71.0 because boldness does not add cutting power. Speed 70.0 reflects measured bursts, while Agility is held to 70.0 because routine swimming is slow.
