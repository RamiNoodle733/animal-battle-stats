# Japanese Spider Crab (*Macrocheira kaempferi*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Japanese spider crab (also Japanese giant spider crab; *taka-ashi-gani*, "tall-legged crab", in Japanese)
- **Scientific name:** *Macrocheira kaempferi* (Temminck, 1836)
- **Exact animal represented:** the Japanese spider crab, the only living member of its genus and the arthropod with the greatest leg span known (McClain et al. 2015; Wikipedia).
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy hard-shelled adult **male**, **12.0 kg**, **30.0 cm** carapace width and about **3.0 m** claw-to-claw span.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger than females and have much longer claws; in adult males the claw-bearing legs are far longer than the walking legs, while in females they are shorter than the walking legs (Georgia Aquarium; ADW). The male is the more heavily armed sex.
- **Population notes:** found mainly off the Pacific coast of Japan, with outlying records from Taiwan (Wikipedia; McClain et al. 2015). The carapace stays about the same size through adulthood while the legs keep lengthening with age (ADW; Monterey Bay Aquarium), so leg span is a poor guide to mass.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- ADW: **16–20 kg**.
- Georgia Aquarium: can weigh **up to 44 lb (20 kg)**.
- Wikipedia summary: up to **19 kg**.
- McClain et al. (2015): actual size measurements are rare; the 19 kg figure cannot be confirmed; a display specimen in The Hague with a **3.7 m** leg span weighed **more than 13.6 kg**.
- **Canonical:** **12.0 kg**, an editorial large-adult value placed below the only documented weight for a near-record-span animal.
- **Confidence:** Low. The quoted 16–20 kg figures are unconfirmed maxima.

### Carapace and leg span
- Monterey Bay Aquarium: carapace **up to 12 inches (30 cm)** across; legs span up to **12 feet (3.7–3.8 m)**.
- ADW and Georgia Aquarium: carapace about **37 cm**; span nearly 4 m / up to 3.7 m.
- McClain et al. (2015): a mature female from Taiwan measured only 24.2 cm carapace length.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 30.0**, recorded as **carapace width**, matching the carapace-based convention used for the king crab on this roster. Leg span is recorded separately at about 300 cm for a large adult, below the 3.7–3.8 m maxima.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Height
- No standing-height measurement found. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
- ADW: slow-moving, roams the seafloor and **cannot swim**. No speed measurement exists.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0**, meaning no measurement, not stationary.

### Lifespan
- ADW notes it is often reported to live **100 years**, but this is not backed by a published age study.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 0.0**, meaning no reliable measurement. The 100-year figure is recorded as an unverified claim only.

### Claws
- Males have long, equal right and left claws (ADW). ADW describes the claws as **fairly weak**, used against smaller predators and to tear up objects.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No pinch-force measurement exists; none is invented.

### Defensive structure
- A well-calcified, bumpy carapace covered in tubercles and spines (ADW), used for protection and camouflage (Wikipedia). The long walking legs are thin and are often lost to predators and nets (ADW).
- Most vulnerable right after molting, before the new shell hardens (Monterey Bay Aquarium). A captive molt takes about 100 minutes during which the crab cannot move (Wikipedia, citing Okamoto's captive observations).

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The Japanese spider crab's weapons are a pair of long, slender claws, far longer in males than in females. ADW describes them as fairly weak: good for tearing up sponges or algae, prying open mollusks and warding off smaller predators, but not for crushing hard prey the way a king crab or lobster can. Its long reach is the real asset; a big male can touch an opponent from well over a metre away.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
It walks slowly across the seafloor on eight very long, thin walking legs and cannot swim (ADW). The legs give a wide, stable stance but make quick turns or bursts difficult. It moves between depths seasonally, with adults coming up to about 50 m to spawn (Monterey Bay Aquarium).

### Endurance
Low-output, steady activity suited to scavenging over the seafloor at 50–600 m (Georgia Aquarium). At an experimental whale carcass at 500 m in Sagami Bay, Japanese spider crabs were the second most abundant visitor, with 1,002 individuals counted, and showed a strong day-night rhythm of activity (Aguzzi et al. 2019).

### Senses
ADW notes that because the species is not an active hunter and has few predators, its sensory systems are not especially advanced. It finds carrion on the seafloor, as shown by its numbers at the whale carcass (Aguzzi et al. 2019).

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior is simple. Juveniles decorate their shells with sponges, kelp and other material to disguise themselves, following a set routine of tearing, shaping and attaching objects, but most adults do not bother because their size deters predators (ADW; Wikipedia).

### Sociality and hunting
It is an opportunistic scavenger and omnivore, eating dead or decaying fish, invertebrates and algae, and sometimes prying open mollusks or catching small invertebrates (Monterey Bay Aquarium). It is not an active predator (ADW).

### Fighting and aggression
The species is described as having a gentle disposition despite its fearsome appearance (Wikipedia). Large adults rely on size and claws to ward off predators (Monterey Bay Aquarium).

### Environmental strengths
- Deep, cold seafloor habitat where few large predators hunt.
- Encounters with small animals that cannot get past its reach and shell.

### Weaknesses
- Weak claws and very low striking power.
- Thin legs that are easily lost.
- Very slow and unable to swim.
- Soft and helpless during and just after molting.
- Low aggression.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small, soft animals that cannot breach its shell or get inside its reach.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized crustaceans, where its reach competes with their stronger claws.
- **Bad:** octopuses, fish and mammals with strong bites, and any fast opponent that can pick off its legs.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Record Reach** — a big male's claw-to-claw span can pass 3 m, the widest of any arthropod, letting it touch and fend off an opponent long before that opponent reaches its body.
2. **Living Disguise** — young crabs tear off sponges, kelp and other material and attach them to hooked spines on the shell, hiding their outline on the seafloor.

### Unique traits
1. **Legs That Keep Growing** — the shell stops growing much in adulthood, but the legs keep lengthening with age, and lost legs can regrow at later molts.
2. **Deep-Water Giant** — it lives at 50–600 m off Japan and gathers in large numbers at food falls; over a thousand were counted at one whale carcass at 500 m.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Japanese spider crabs live on sandy and rocky bottoms of the continental shelf and slope, mainly off the Pacific coast of Honshu from Tokyo Bay to Kagoshima, with outlying records from Iwate Prefecture and Taiwan (Wikipedia; ADW). They are found at 50–600 m, most often around 150–300 m (Georgia Aquarium; ADW). In Suruga Bay they are regular at 300 m, where the water is about 10 °C (Wikipedia).

### Diet and ecology
They are omnivorous scavengers, eating dead or decaying fish, invertebrates and algae, and sometimes prying open mollusks or catching small invertebrates (Monterey Bay Aquarium). At an experimental sperm whale carcass at 500 m they were the second most abundant animal and showed day-night activity cycles (Aguzzi et al. 2019).

### Social structure
They are not social in any organized way, but gather in numbers at food sources (Aguzzi et al. 2019) and move to shallower water to spawn (Monterey Bay Aquarium).

### Reproduction and life history
Mating takes place in early spring. Females carry fertilized eggs on the abdomen, up to about 1.5 million per season, which hatch in about 10 days (Wikipedia; ADW). Larvae pass through a brief prezoeal stage that lasts only minutes (Okamoto 1995), then zoeal and megalopa stages whose survival and length depend strongly on temperature (Okamoto 1993). Young crabs live in shallower water and move deeper as they grow (Monterey Bay Aquarium).

### Conservation
The species has not been assessed by the IUCN in any source read for this report. The catch has declined considerably over the last 40 years, and fishing is restricted during the spring breeding season to protect it (ADW; Monterey Bay Aquarium). Warming seas may reduce larval survival and degrade seaweed beds (Monterey Bay Aquarium).

### Special features
- The widest leg span of any arthropod, over 3.5 m in the largest animals.
- Well-calcified, spiny, bumpy carapace.
- Long claws in males, short in females.
- Legs that keep lengthening through adulthood and regrow after loss.
- Decorating behavior in juveniles.

### Human interaction
Japanese spider crabs are a seasonal delicacy in Japan, though their depth makes them hard to catch (Georgia Aquarium). They are popular in public aquariums worldwide; a study across six aquariums set baseline blood values and linked shell lesions to changes in blood cells (Griffioen et al. 2022). Because the legs are so long, researchers often use their tendons as a research material (ADW).

### Genuine fun facts
- It has the widest leg span of any known arthropod, up to about 3.7 m.
- Its Japanese name, *taka-ashi-gani*, means "tall-legged crab".
- Its shell stops growing much in adulthood, but its legs keep getting longer.
- Young spider crabs dress themselves in sponges and seaweed for camouflage.
- More than a thousand were counted at a single whale carcass on the seafloor at 500 m.
- A female can carry up to about 1.5 million eggs in a season.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Japanese spider crab has the widest leg span of any arthropod, over 3 m in big males, but its long claws are weak and it is slow, gentle and unable to swim. Its spiny shell gives decent protection, yet its thin legs and weak pinch make it a poor fighter against anything with a strong bite.

### Rich narrative profile
The Japanese spider crab looks like a monster from the deep: a spiny, orange body the size of a dinner plate, perched on legs that can stretch more than three metres from claw to claw. It lives on the seafloor off Japan at depths of 50 to 600 m, and it keeps growing in an unusual way: the shell reaches its adult size, but the legs go on lengthening for the rest of the crab's life.

Despite the look, it is a scavenger rather than a hunter. It picks over dead fish, algae and small invertebrates, and gathers in large numbers where food falls to the bottom; researchers counted more than a thousand around one whale carcass. It is reported to have a gentle disposition, and its long claws are relatively weak, better at tearing sponges off rocks than crushing shells.

In a battle, that makes it much less dangerous than it looks. Its reach can keep a small opponent at a distance and its well-calcified, spiny carapace protects the body, but the legs are thin and easily broken, it cannot swim away, and during a molt it is helpless. It rates low in Attack and Agility, moderate in Defense, and its Special score rests on reach and camouflage rather than any real weapon.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 16–20 kg figures repeat across sources but, as McClain et al. (2015) point out, none is confirmed. The canonical 12.0 kg is an editorial value below the only documented weight of a near-record-span specimen.
- **Carapace:** 30 cm (Monterey Bay Aquarium) versus about 37 cm (ADW, Georgia Aquarium). `length_cm` uses 30 cm carapace width.
- **Lifespan:** the popular 100-year figure has no published basis found in this run; `lifespan_years` is left at 0.0 (unknown).
- **Claw strength:** described as weak by ADW; no force measurement exists.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus King Crab (3.1 kg; A30 D44 Ag26 St55 I31 Sp49):** the spider crab is about four times heavier with a far greater reach, but its claws are weak and slender while the king crab's are crushing. It therefore sits below the king crab in Attack (22.0), Defense (40.0), Agility (14.0), Stamina, Intelligence and Special, and above it only in Size.
- **Versus Lobster (1.0 kg; A28 D36 Ag35 St57 I48 Sp60):** the lobster's differentiated crushing and cutting claws, tail escape and dominance behavior give it higher Attack, Agility and Intelligence despite a twelfth of the mass. The spider crab's larger, spinier shell gives it higher Defense.
- **Versus Coconut Crab (3 kg; A39 D28):** the coconut crab's powerful pinch makes it a far better attacker; the spider crab's greater size and carapace give higher Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the record leg span is credited in Size and Special only, not as a weapon, because the claws at the end of it are weak. No pinch force or lifespan is invented.
