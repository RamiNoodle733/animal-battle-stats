# Impala research report

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Roster name:** Impala
- **Scientific name:** *Aepyceros melampus* (Lichtenstein, 1812)
- **Exact animal represented:** common impala, with the species-level profile centered on *A. m. melampus* rather than the geographically restricted black-faced form *A. m. petersi*.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy fully mature territorial male, **65.0 kg**, approximately **90.0 cm shoulder height**, **145.0 cm head-body length**. Males are the larger, horn-bearing and primary intraspecific fighting sex.
- **Dimorphism:** only males carry the long lyre-shaped horns. ADW reports a species mass range of 45-60 kg in its summary, while institutional/ungulate references broaden the adult range to roughly 40-80 kg; SeaWorld gives males 45.5-79.5 kg. The 65 kg canonical male is intentionally representative, not a maximum 80 kg individual.
- **Population variation:** the black-faced impala of Namibia/Angola is a recognized geographically restricted form and should not silently define the common roster animal. Conservation and phenotype notes therefore distinguish it from the widespread common form.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
Credible institutional references span roughly 40-80 kg for the species, with males reaching the upper portion of that range. SeaWorld gives adult males 45.5-79.5 kg. **Recommended canonical mass: 65.0 kg** for a mature territorial male. Confidence: high for the range, moderate-high for the representative point.

### Height
National Geographic gives 33-39 in (83.8-99.1 cm) at the shoulder; Ultimate Ungulate gives 75-95 cm. **Recommended canonical shoulder height: 90.0 cm.**

### Length
Ultimate Ungulate gives body length **120-160 cm**, plus a 30-45 cm tail. **Recommended canonical `length_cm`: 145.0 cm head-body**, keeping tail length separate rather than silently combining measurement conventions.

### Speed
Planete Sauvage gives a top running speed of **80 km/h**, equivalent to **22.2 m/s**, and describes leaps up to 4 m high and 10 m long. This is an institutional zoo value rather than a controlled maximum-speed experiment, so confidence is moderate. It is still sufficiently explicit and species-specific to use as the proposed canonical top-speed field with the caveat preserved. **Recommended `speed_mps`: 22.2.**

### Lifespan
SeaWorld gives **12-17 years**. Use **15.0 years** as a representative canonical lifespan rather than a captive record. Confidence: moderate-high.

### Bite force
No defensible species-specific bite-pressure measurement was recovered. Impala are grazing/browsing ruminants whose combat weapon is the male's horns, not a specialized damaging bite. **Recommended `bite_force_psi`: 0.0** rather than fabricating PSI.

### Horns
ADW reports male horns **45-91.7 cm** long, heavily ridged and S/lyre shaped. Safari West independently gives approximately **45-92 cm**. Mature males use these horns directly against rivals. Horn length is therefore a high-confidence combat measurement.

### Defensive structure
There is no armor, shell or unusually thick hide. Defense comes primarily from early detection, explosive acceleration, rapid directional changes, jumping, herd vigilance and escape. The body is comparatively gracile and optimized for locomotion rather than absorbing trauma.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offensive mechanics
The adult male's primary weapons are the paired ridged horns, potentially approaching 0.9 m in length. Rutting males defend access to females and engage rivals directly. Peer-reviewed observations of rutting males document fights and intense horn-stabbing of the ground, with broken horns observed in some males. In a one-on-one matchup the impala's offense is therefore a forward horn thrust, hook, clash or shove delivered from a mobile stance. Hooves can contribute incidental kicks, but they are secondary to the horns.

### Defense and durability
Impala are escape specialists rather than tanks. Their slender limbs and relatively light frame make them vulnerable if a large predator secures contact. They lack meaningful armor and should not receive high Protection simply because they are difficult to catch. Their survival strategy is to avoid the damaging exchange in the first place.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
This is the species' strongest conventional battle dimension. A credible zoo source reports 80 km/h and leaps up to 4 m high and 10 m long. Rapid bounding, obstacle clearance and abrupt changes in heading make an impala difficult to intercept in open or broken savanna. Top speed and maneuverability are scored separately: speed is very high, but Agility reflects the combination of acceleration, bounding, balance and evasive control.

### Endurance and recovery
Impala can sustain routine movement and remain active across day/night periods, but they are not specialized persistence runners on the scale of canids or endurance-migration specialists. A classic Kruger study found relatively small daily home ranges (~0.98 km²/24 h) and short daily movements (~3 km/24 h), which argues against converting their spectacular sprint escape into elite long-duration Stamina. Recovery is good for an athletic ungulate but not extraordinary.

### Senses
Large laterally placed eyes and mobile ears support vigilance in predator-rich habitats. Herd living adds many observers, but this report does not multiply one individual's sensory score by herd size. Senses are strong for early predator detection and tracking nearby rivals.

### Tactics and intelligence
The species uses flexible social organization: territorial males, bachelor groups and female/breeding herds. Rutting males defend females and space, and can escalate from display/vocalization to direct horn combat. This supports moderate tactical competence, but not primate/corvid/carnivore-level problem solving.

### Fighting and predation behavior
Impala are herbivores and do not hunt. Male-male fighting is the relevant offensive behavior. Peer-reviewed rut observations document males defending females against rivals, fights, ground-stabbing with horns and broken horns. The species' loud rut calls and mobile larynx function in reproductive competition rather than as a damaging sonic weapon.

### Predator defense
The core sequence is detection, alarm/flight, high-speed evasive running and bounding over or around obstacles. Grouping can dilute individual risk and improve detection, but a solitary ABS specimen receives only the individual capabilities it can bring into the arena.

### Environmental advantages
- Open savanna and woodland edge: room to accelerate, turn and bound.
- Broken scrub: obstacle-clearing ability can complicate pursuit.
- Familiar dry African habitats: feeding flexibility and heat tolerance support routine function.

### Environmental disadvantages
- Confined terrain removes the spacing needed for evasive speed.
- Deep mud, steep slick surfaces or dense barriers reduce the value of long-legged bounding.
- Aquatic matchups strongly disadvantage a terrestrial antelope.

### Weaknesses
- Limited protection once seized.
- Horn offense is male-specific and most effective forward-facing.
- Gracile limbs can be compromised by heavy impact or grappling.
- High sprint performance does not equal extreme sustained endurance.
- Herbivore combat repertoire is narrower than that of similarly sized dedicated predators.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favorable:** smaller terrestrial opponents that cannot withstand a horn strike or match its reach and mobility; slow opponents in open ground.

**Unfavorable:** large ambush/grappling predators that can survive the initial horn threat and secure the torso; armored opponents resistant to puncture; aquatic specialists; very large ungulates whose mass overwhelms the impala's absolute power.

## 6. Proposed headline ratings

- **Attack: 43.0.** Long horns give the mature male legitimate puncture/hooking capability, but absolute damage remains below comparably sized carnivores with multi-surface biting/clawing systems and far below large bovids.
- **Defense: 31.0.** Evasion helps avoid damage, but physical protection is limited. Defense is intentionally much lower than Agility.
- **Agility: 91.0.** Elite terrestrial acceleration, bounding and obstacle clearance, with top speed treated as supporting evidence rather than a substitute for agility.
- **Stamina: 64.0.** Strong athletic capacity, but not inflated to elite endurance because field movement data show modest routine travel distances.
- **Intelligence: 48.0.** Social recognition, territorial behavior and flexible herd organization support moderate behavioral sophistication without evidence for high-order problem solving.
- **Special: 62.0.** Exceptional escape bounding and specialized male horns are meaningful matchup tools, but neither is a rare biochemical or sensory super-system.

## 7. Abilities and traits

### Special abilities
1. **Savanna Vault** - explosive bounding can clear major horizontal and vertical obstacles while maintaining escape momentum, making interception difficult in broken open terrain.
2. **Lyre-Horn Counter** - mature males can thrust, hook and clash with long ridged horns during frontal engagements, punishing opponents that enter the head-and-neck arc.

### Unique traits
1. **Metatarsal Signal Patches** - distinctive dark hair/gland regions above the rear hooves are part of the impala's characteristic signaling anatomy.
2. **Rutting Resonator** - males possess a highly mobile laryngeal system used to produce unusually impressive rutting calls during reproductive competition.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Impala occupy savannas, grasslands, light woodland and woodland-savanna edges across eastern and southern Africa, commonly associated with access to water and suitable browse/grass. The common form remains widespread and abundant.

### Diet and ecology
Impala are mixed-feeding herbivores, shifting between grazing and browsing according to season and food availability. This flexibility helps them persist across seasonal savanna systems. They are also an important prey base for multiple African carnivores.

### Social structure
Three broad organizations are commonly recognized: territorial adult males, bachelor groups and breeding/female herds. During the rut, males compete for access to females and defend space or female groups. Large seasonal aggregations can form when resources are abundant.

### Life history
Gestation is roughly 6-7 months and females usually produce one offspring. Young males eventually leave female groups and enter bachelor groups. The recommended lifespan field is 15 years within the institutional 12-17 year range.

### Conservation
The species is assessed as **Least Concern** and the common impala population is estimated at roughly two million, with substantial numbers in protected areas and on private land. The black-faced form has a much smaller distribution and has required targeted conservation attention. The common form's secure status should not be generalized to every regional population.

### Adaptations
The impala combines long cursorial limbs, extreme bounding, rapid acceleration, high-speed escape, large vigilant eyes/ears, seasonal mixed feeding and male horn weaponry. These adaptations solve two different problems: avoiding predators and competing with conspecific males.

### Human interaction
Impala are prominent in protected-area tourism and are also hunted for meat and trophies in parts of southern Africa. Habitat alteration and management can affect local populations even though the species as a whole remains common.

### Genuine fun facts
- Male horns can approach **92 cm**, roughly comparable to the canonical animal's shoulder height.
- Peer-reviewed rut observations recorded males stabbing the ground with their horns and noted broken horns in some individuals.
- A classic Kruger study estimated only about **3 km of daily movement** despite the species' spectacular sprint capability.
- Impala can switch between grazing and browsing as seasonal food conditions change.
- Their black markings include distinctive patches above the rear hooves, linked with metatarsal glands.
- Rutting males use a mobile larynx to produce resonant calls during competition.

### Concise site summary
The impala is a fast, evasive African antelope built to survive by not being caught. A mature ram combines elite bounding and sprint maneuverability with long lyre-shaped horns used in serious male combat. Its weakness is the other side of that specialization: once a large predator secures contact, the impala has little armor and limited close-range options beyond its forward horn arc.

### Rich narrative profile
An impala ram is not a miniature buffalo. Its build is light, spring-loaded and optimized around spacing. Long limbs generate explosive escape, while the body remains narrow enough for rapid changes of direction. In open savanna that geometry is a major defensive advantage because a pursuer must repeatedly solve a moving interception problem rather than simply match straight-line speed.

The male's horns transform an otherwise escape-focused herbivore into a credible frontal fighter. During the rut, males do not merely display: direct fighting occurs, and field observations document horn impacts severe enough that broken horns are seen. Even so, ABS scaling must preserve absolute force. A 65 kg ram cannot be scored as though horn length erases the mass gap to an ibex, large cat, hyena, buffalo or megafaunal opponent.

Its best battle plan is therefore positional. Keep distance, force a slower opponent to turn, use acceleration and bounds to reset the angle, and present the horns when contact becomes unavoidable. In cramped terrain the strategy collapses. A grappling predator that gets past the horn tips reaches a lightly protected torso and slender limbs. This makes the impala an unusually clear example of why Agility, Defense and Stamina should remain separate ratings.

## 11. Confidence and source conflicts

- **Mass:** high confidence in broad adult male range, moderate-high in 65 kg representative canonical choice. Sources differ because some summarize both sexes while others give male-specific maxima.
- **Height/length:** high confidence in general ranges; canonical values are representative editorial selections within them.
- **Speed/jump:** moderate confidence. The 80 km/h and 4 m/10 m jump values are institutional but were not recovered as controlled biomechanical maxima. They should not be presented as laboratory measurements.
- **Bite PSI:** intentionally absent. No credible species-specific PSI measurement found.
- **Lifespan:** moderate-high confidence for 12-17 y institutional range; longer captive maxima should not replace a representative field-oriented value.
- **Conservation:** high confidence for common impala Least Concern/abundant status; black-faced form must be treated separately.

## 12. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Ibex:** impala is lighter and less powerfully built, so Attack 43.0 and Raw Power 34.0 remain below Alpine ibex (Attack 51.0). Impala is much faster and more evasive, so Agility 91.0 exceeds ibex 71.0.
- **Versus Hyena:** spotted hyena remains substantially higher in Attack, Defense, Stamina and Intelligence because it is a robust bone-processing predator with greater close-contact lethality. Impala wins decisively in Agility, not in durability.
- **Versus Iguana:** impala's absolute mass, horns and locomotor performance justify much higher Attack and conventional Agility, while iguana retains more unusual Special mechanisms such as autotomy/regrowth.
- **Against megafauna:** 65 kg mass and horn reach do not justify compressed ratings near hippo/elephant tiers. The large roster-wide physical gap is preserved.
- **No proportional-strength leakage:** spectacular jumps and speed raise Speed/Maneuverability/Agility, not Raw Power.
- **No speed-agility conflation:** Agility is high because of evasive bounding and control in addition to sprint speed; Stamina is kept moderate-strong because routine field movement does not support elite persistence-runner status.
