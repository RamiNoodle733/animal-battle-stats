# Wombat (*Vombatus ursinus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Wombat; specifically the common wombat, also called the bare-nosed wombat
- **Scientific name:** *Vombatus ursinus* (Shaw, 1800)
- **Exact animal represented:** the common wombat, the species shown in the site's live photo (an adult photographed at Tidal River, Wilsons Promontory National Park, Victoria, Australia, within the mainland subspecies' range). The two hairy-nosed wombats (*Lasiorhinus*) are separate species and are not represented.
- **Status:** Living. Least Concern.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male of the mainland subspecies, **28.0 kg** and **100.0 cm** head-body length.
- **Sex choice:** the sexes are similar in size in the sources checked, so a male is used by default rather than because males are clearly larger.
- **Population variation:** three subspecies differ in size. The mainland form is the largest, averaging about 26–27 kg and about 1 m long, with large animals reaching 1.2 m and 35 kg (Wikipedia); the Tasmanian form averages about 20 kg and 85 cm; the Flinders Island form is smallest at about 75 cm.

### Speed
- Australian Museum: wombats are strong and "can move fast at speeds over 40 km/h over short distances."
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 11.1 m/s** (40 km/h), a museum-reported short-sprint figure, not an instrumented trial.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Lifespan
- Wikipedia: average **15 years** in the wild and 20 in captivity.
- ADW: typically **12–15 years**; about 30 years maximum in captivity.
- Australian Museum: up to **11 years** in the wild.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 12.0**.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-low because wild figures conflict.

### Teeth, bite and claws
- Wombats have a single pair of upper and lower incisors, unique among marsupials, and all teeth grow continuously; enamel structure keeps a self-sharpening ridge on the molars (Wikipedia, Sharp & Trusler).
- The masseter and medial pterygoid muscles are greatly enlarged compared with other marsupials, and the broad, flat skull is "well suited to generate a very high bite force" (Sharp & Trusler 2015). Finite element modelling found the wombat skull under low stress with high mechanical efficiency (Sharp 2015).
- Bites on people are documented; one naturalist received a bite 2 cm deep through a rubber boot, trousers and thick socks (Wikipedia).
- Claws on short, powerful legs are long and suited to digging.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No measured bite force was found; modelling supports a strong bite without giving a verified number.

### Defensive structure
- The rump is the main shield: a toughened rear hide over a posterior made largely of cartilage, with almost no tail to grab (Wikipedia). The wombat dives into a burrow and blocks the tunnel with its rump.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The wombat is a herbivore, so its offense is defensive. Chisel-like, ever-growing incisors driven by very large jaw muscles give a strong bite, the long digging claws can puncture skin, and a startled wombat will charge and bowl a person over, risking broken bones from the fall (Wikipedia). A 2010 report described a Victorian man needing hospital treatment for cuts and bite wounds after a wombat attack. None of these tools is a dedicated killing weapon.

### Defense and durability
The rump is a real specialization. With most of the posterior made of cartilage under toughened hide, and no meaningful tail, a predator that follows a wombat into its tunnel faces a hard, blunt wall that is difficult to bite. ADW and the Australian Museum describe wombats backing into an attacker in the burrow and crushing its head against the roof or wall with the rump and strong hind legs; Wikipedia calls the skull-crushing story an urban legend. The rump-blocking defense is well supported; the crushing kill is treated here as unconfirmed.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Short, powerful legs give a low center of gravity and great digging force. Over short distances wombats reach over 40 km/h, but they are built for sprinting to a burrow, not for sustained running or agile dodging.

### Endurance
Wombats spend about two-thirds of their lives in burrows, emerging after sunset to graze for several hours (Australian Museum). Home ranges are small, 0.024–0.083 km² in ADW. Digging long tunnel systems takes sustained strength, but daily travel and aerobic stamina are modest.

### Senses
Mainly nocturnal and crepuscular. The sources read give no measured sensory performance; smell and hearing are used for foraging and detecting threats in the dark.

### Intelligence and tactics
The wombat's tactics are simple and effective: stay near one of several burrows, flee to it, and plug it with the rump. Individuals keep a number of burrows and switch where they sleep every 1–9 days. Complex problem solving is not documented.

### Social structure and fighting
Generally solitary, with overlapping ranges and occasional shared burrows. Mating involves the male biting the female's rump and rolling her onto her side.

### Predator defense
Main native predators are dingoes and Tasmanian devils; quolls and eagles take young. Foxes and wild dogs are introduced threats (Australian Museum). Young, immature and old or weakened wombats are the most likely prey.

### Environmental strengths
- Its own burrow network, where the rump shield and narrow tunnels favor it.
- Forest, heath and grassland with soft, diggable soil.
- Nighttime and dusk activity.

### Weaknesses
- Slow over distance; far from a burrow it must rely on a short sprint and a bite.
- Low absolute mass and no dedicated killing weapons.
- Head, neck and belly are exposed if flipped or caught in the open.
- Sarcoptic mange weakens many animals.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small to medium predators that try to follow it into a burrow; opponents that must attack from behind; similar-size animals that cannot bite through the rump.
- **Mixed:** foxes, lone dingoes and Tasmanian devils in open ground.
- **Bad:** large carnivores, heavy ungulates, raptors against juveniles, and any fight far from cover.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Rump Shield Plug** — the wombat dives into its tunnel and blocks it with a cartilage-backed rump under toughened hide, leaving a pursuer facing a hard, almost tailless wall that is difficult to bite. Crushing an attacker against the tunnel roof is reported by some sources but not confirmed.
2. **Bulldozer Burrower** — powerful claws and short, strong limbs dig tunnel systems 2–20 m long with several entrances and side tunnels, giving the wombat a network of refuges across its range.

### Unique traits
1. **Cube-Shaped Scat** — the common wombat produces cube-shaped droppings; dissections show the cubes form in the last 17% of the intestine, where varying intestinal wall stiffness shapes the corners.
2. **Ever-Growing Teeth** — the only marsupial whose teeth grow continuously, with a single pair of upper and lower incisors and self-sharpening molars.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Temperate forests, woodlands, heath and grassy areas of southeastern Australia with soil suitable for burrowing. At European settlement it ranged from southeastern Queensland along the Great Dividing Range of New South Wales to most of Victoria (Australian Museum); it also lives in Tasmania and on Flinders Island, with an introduced population on Maria Island. Wombats often live in riparian areas because they prefer to dig burrows there (ADW).

### Diet and ecology
A grazer of mainly native grasses. Its efficient digestion lets it spend much of its time in burrows. Common wombats are ecosystem engineers: their burrows turn over soil and shelter many other species. A camera-trap study in southeastern Australia found small mammals and birds more active at burrow sites, and those associations persisted or strengthened in areas burned by the 2019–20 Black Summer fires.

### Social structure
Solitary, with overlapping home ranges and occasional burrow sharing. Wombats use several burrows and switch sleeping burrows every 1–9 days.

### Reproduction and life history
Wombats breed about every two years and produce a single joey. Joeys leave the pouch weighing 3.5–6.5 kg. Sexual maturity comes at about two years.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. Sarcoptic mange is the most serious problem, along with roadkill and habitat loss. Spotlight surveys estimated about 71,800 wombats on Flinders Island in 2020, with densities of 0.42 per hectare there and 0.11 per hectare on mainland Tasmania; both populations grew over the 19-year survey period. A Victorian bounty paid from 1926 to 1966 treated wombats as agricultural pests.

### Special features
- Cartilage-backed, toughened rump used to block burrows.
- Single pair of upper and lower incisors; all teeth grow continuously.
- Greatly enlarged jaw-closing muscles on a broad, flat skull.
- Powerful digging claws and short, strong legs.
- Cube-shaped droppings formed in the final part of the intestine.

### Human interaction
Wombats dig under rabbit fences and are sometimes treated as pests. Most encounters are harmless, but startled or mange-affected wombats have bitten, clawed and knocked people over. Wildlife carers treat mange in the field using burrow flaps that dose animals as they pass; one semi-isolated population was temporarily cleared of mange for about 20 months this way.

### Genuine fun facts
- Wombat droppings are cube-shaped, and the physics of how they form won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2019.
- The cubes form within the last 17% of the intestine.
- A wombat can run at over 40 km/h in short bursts.
- Wombats switch the burrow they sleep in every 1–9 days.
- The common wombat is the only marsupial whose teeth grow throughout life.
- Many small animals use wombat burrows, and these burrows stayed activity hotspots after the Black Summer fires.
- One naturalist was bitten 2 cm deep through a rubber boot, trousers and woollen socks.

### Concise site-ready summary
The common wombat is a 28 kg burrowing marsupial with ever-growing teeth, powerful digging claws and a cartilage-backed rump it uses to plug its tunnel against predators. It can sprint over 40 km/h and bite hard, but away from its burrow it is a slow, unarmored herbivore.

### Rich narrative profile
The common wombat is a low, barrel-shaped digger about a metre long and near 28 kg, built around its burrow. Short, heavy legs with long claws excavate tunnels up to 20 m long, and a single pair of chisel-like incisors backed by unusually large jaw muscles crops tough grasses. Every tooth keeps growing throughout life, a feature no other marsupial shares.

Its best-known defense faces backwards. Most of the wombat's rear is cartilage covered by toughened hide, and it has almost no tail, so when a dingo, fox or devil follows it underground the attacker meets a hard wall that is difficult to bite. Some sources describe wombats crushing an intruder's skull against the tunnel roof; others call that a legend, so it is not counted here. In the open the wombat can sprint over 40 km/h for a short distance and will bite, claw or charge when cornered.

On the absolute roster scale the wombat is a small defender rather than a fighter. Its Attack stays low because its weapons are defensive and it weighs under 30 kg, its Defense is boosted by the rump shield, and its Special score reflects how much the burrow-and-plug strategy changes fights against pursuing predators. Away from cover, against a large carnivore, it has few answers.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Koala (12 kg; Attack 28.0, Defense 23.0, Special 62.0):** the wombat is more than twice as heavy with a stronger bite and a rump shield, so Attack (33.0) and Defense (48.0) are higher; Special is similar because each has one distinctive specialization.
- **Versus Badger (13 kg; Attack 38.0, Defense 39.0, Agility 52.0):** the badger is a predator with a more aggressive bite, so it keeps higher Attack despite being half the wombat's mass; the wombat's rump shield gives it higher Defense.
- **Versus Quoll (3.5 kg; Attack 32.0, Agility 77.0):** the quoll is a far more agile predator; Attack is set about equal because the wombat's much greater mass offsets the quoll's hunting bite.
- **Versus Tasmanian devil-sized predators:** devils prey on wombats, yet a healthy adult wombat in its burrow has the advantage; outside the burrow the devil's bite is the more dangerous weapon.
- **Versus Armadillo (5.5 kg; Defense 47.0) and Porcupine (10.5 kg; Defense 67.0):** the wombat's partial rump armor sits near the armadillo's full shell in Defense because of its much greater mass, and well below the porcupine's quill defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the old live placeholders (Attack 14.7, Defense 11.1) are not used. They underrated the rump shield and bite.
