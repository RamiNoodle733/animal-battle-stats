# Budgerigar (*Melopsittacus undulatus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Budgerigar (budgie, shell parakeet, warbling grass parakeet)
- **Scientific name:** *Melopsittacus undulatus* (Shaw, 1805)
- **Exact animal represented:** the budgerigar as a species: the wild green-and-yellow nomadic parrot of inland Australia, which is also the stock that the world's most widely kept pet bird was bred from. Pet-trade budgies are mostly similar in size and build to wild birds; "English" show budgerigars are about twice as large and are treated here as a breed variant, not the canonical animal.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult wild-type male, **30 g (0.03 kg)**, **18 cm** long, matching the Animal Diversity Web average mass of 30 g and BirdLife Australia's average of 18 cm and 28 g.
- **Sex choice:** the sexes are the same size. Males have a blue cere and females a brown or pale blue one (Australian Museum, BirdLife Australia). A male is used only for consistency with the roster convention; ADW notes that females are generally the more aggressive sex in wild flocks.
- **Population notes:** wild birds are uniform in colour and size across Australia. Captive breeding since the 19th century has produced more colour varieties than almost any other animal (ADW), and exhibition birds are heavier and shorter-lived (Wikipedia: about 7 to 9 years).

### Speed
- Schiffner and Srinivasan (2016, *Biology Letters*) filmed budgerigars in a tapered tunnel: they flew at a constant high speed of **9.44 m/s** in the wide section and dropped abruptly to **5.44 m/s** in the narrow section. The high value matches the energy-efficient cruising speed (about 10 m/s) from earlier wind-tunnel respirometry.
- No measured maximum escape or dive speed was found.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 9.4 m/s**, flagged as a measured preferred cruising speed, not a maximum. Real escape bursts are probably faster.
- **Confidence:** High for the measured value; low for any maximum.

### Lifespan
- Wikipedia: captive average **7.8 years**, with 15–21 years reported. AnAge: maximum longevity **21 years** (captivity).
- A zoo flock studied for disease averaged only **3.6 years** (Wikipedia).
- Wild lifespan is not well measured.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 7.8**, the captive average, because no wild average exists.
- **Confidence:** Moderate for captivity; wild survival is unknown and probably shorter.

### Bite and bill
- Harrison et al. (2025, *Journal of Anatomy*) calculated a bite force of **12.4 N** for a budgerigar (N = 1, 29 g body mass), the smallest value among the parrots they studied; the method was validated against force-transducer measurements in other parrots.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** A force in newtons is not a pressure, and no contact area is available, so no PSI is invented.

### Defensive structure
- No armour. Feathers and a 30 g body offer essentially no protection. Defense is flock vigilance, alarm calls ("zit" alarm call, Australian Museum) and fast, precise flight.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The only weapon is a small hooked bill with a calculated bite of about 12 N, used to dehusk grass seeds and, in disputes, to nip flockmates or a human finger. The feet are zygodactyl climbing feet, not talons. Against any vertebrate larger than itself the budgerigar has no way to cause serious injury; its offense is limited to pecks and bites in squabbles over nest hollows and perches.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Flight is the budgerigar's core capability. It switches abruptly between two preferred speeds depending on how cluttered the path ahead is, and it anticipates the change before reaching the narrow section (Schiffner and Srinivasan 2016). Henningsson (2021) filmed a budgerigar flying through gaps down to a quarter of its wingspan, folding its wings into two different postures and keeping safety margins as small as 6 mm on each side. Flocks fly in a characteristic undulating pattern and wheel at high speed around roost trees at dusk (Australian Museum; ADW). On the ground it walks and forages for fallen seed.

### Endurance
Wild budgerigars are nomadic, following rain and seeding grasses across the arid interior and leaving areas as conditions change (Wikipedia; Australian Museum). They fly out to feeding areas after sunrise, shelter motionless in shade through the hottest hours, and return to roost after sunset (ADW). Wind-tunnel work shows they flap-glide at low speeds and flap-bound at high speeds, patterns consistent with saving energy (Tobalske and Dial 1994). No telemetry of daily distances was found, so endurance is scored as strong but not elite.

### Senses
Like most parrots, the plumage fluoresces under ultraviolet light, and UV signals play a role in mate choice (Wikipedia). The budgerigar is one of the best-studied birds for hearing: its auditory perception and vocal precision recover after inner-ear hair cells are damaged, because the hair cells regrow (Dooling et al. 1997; Hashino et al. 1992).

### Intelligence and tactics
Budgerigars are vocal learners that mimic speech and other sounds, with males usually the better mimics (Wikipedia). Females shifted their mate preference toward males they had watched solve extractive foraging puzzles (Chen et al. 2019, *Science*). Yawning and stretching are contagious between birds that can see each other (Gallup et al. 2015, 2017). This is real social cognition, but it is well below the problem solving of cockatoos, keas and corvids.

### Sociality and hunting
Budgerigars are seed-eaters and never hunt. They live in flocks of a few birds up to 100 or more, and after good rains form flocks of tens of thousands (Australian Museum). Pairs breed in loose colonies with nest hollows only a few metres apart (ADW).

### Fighting and aggression
ADW reports relatively few fights within flocks and no clear hierarchy, with females generally more aggressive than males. Disputes are brief pecking and displacement, not damaging fights.

### Environmental strengths
- Open arid grassland and mulga scrub where fast, low flight and flock vigilance work best.
- Heat and drought: shelters through midday heat and keeps evaporative water loss constant across humidity levels (Eto et al. 2017).
- Cluttered woodland: switches to a slower maneuvering speed and threads narrow gaps.

### Weaknesses
- Negligible mass (30 g) and no meaningful weapon.
- No protection; a single strike from a predator is decisive.
- Needs regular access to drinking water in the wild (ADW: about 5.5% of body weight per day).
- Flock-based defense disappears in a one-on-one matchup.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** insects and other tiny animals that cannot follow it into the air.
- **Mixed:** other small birds, where flight skill and vigilance decide who disengages.
- **Bad:** any raptor, snake, cat or medium predator; anything that can seize it.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Two-Gear Flight** — budgerigars hold one of two fixed flight speeds, about 9.4 m/s in open space and about 5.4 m/s in clutter, and switch before they reach a narrowing passage. Combined with folding its wings to pass gaps a quarter of its wingspan, this lets it escape into cover where larger fliers cannot follow.
2. **Warbling Mimicry** — a lifelong vocal learner that copies flockmates, other sounds and human speech. In a matchup this is a communication and alarm system, not a damage source.

### Unique traits
1. **Outback Nomad** — flocks follow rain across the arid interior, swelling to tens of thousands after good seasons, and breed opportunistically whenever grass seed is abundant.
2. **Ultraviolet Plumage** — its feathers fluoresce under UV light and UV signals are used in mate choice, a channel most mammalian predators cannot see.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Wild budgerigars live across the drier parts of mainland Australia, in open grassland, mulga and mallee scrub, open woodland and farmland, seldom far from water. They are absent from the far south-west, the far north of the Northern Territory, Tasmania and most of the east coast (Australian Museum). Drought can push flocks into wooded or coastal areas. Pet birds are kept worldwide; ADW records an introduced population established in southwest Florida.

### Diet and ecology
Budgerigars feed almost entirely on the seeds of native grasses and herbs such as spinifex, tussock grasses and saltbush, mostly picked from the ground, with drinking and feeding concentrated in the morning (Australian Museum; ADW). They drink about 5.5% of their body weight each day. Predators of such small flock birds include raptors, but no species-specific predation study was read in this pass.

### Social structure
Strongly social flock birds. Flockmates preen and feed one another by regurgitating seed. Flocks range from a few birds to 100 or more and can reach tens of thousands after rain. There is little evidence of a dominance hierarchy (ADW).

### Reproduction and life history
Pairs are monogamous and nest in tree hollows, logs and fence posts, often a few metres apart. Breeding is triggered by rain and seeding grasses rather than a fixed season. Clutches average about 5 eggs (ADW; the Australian Museum gives 6–8), hatching after about 18 days; chicks leave the nest at about 35 days, and birds can breed at about 6 months (ADW; Australian Museum).

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List (BirdLife International assessment, as reported by Wikipedia and BirdLife Australia). Wild numbers fluctuate with rainfall, and farm water points have increased populations in some areas.

### Special features
- Two preferred flight speeds, switched in anticipation of clutter.
- Wing-folding postures for passing gaps narrower than the wingspan.
- UV-fluorescent plumage used in mate choice.
- Inner-ear hair cells that regrow after damage, restoring hearing.
- Regulated evaporative water loss suited to the arid interior.

### Human interaction
The budgerigar is the most widely kept pet bird in the world (ADW), valued for its small size, low cost and ability to mimic speech. It is also a major laboratory model for hearing, vocal learning and flight. Large wild flocks can damage cereal crops.

### Genuine fun facts
- Wild flocks can number in the tens of thousands after a season of abundant rain.
- A budgerigar has flown through gaps only a quarter of its wingspan wide, with as little as 6 mm to spare on each side.
- Budgerigars fly at two fixed "gears", about 9.4 m/s and 5.4 m/s, and switch abruptly between them.
- Female budgerigars became more interested in males after watching them solve food puzzles.
- Yawning is contagious between budgerigars that can see each other.
- If their inner-ear hair cells are damaged, the cells regrow and the bird's hearing and song precision recover.
- Their plumage glows under ultraviolet light.

### Concise site-ready summary
A 30-gram nomad of the Australian outback, the budgerigar survives on flight skill, flock vigilance and water-thrifty physiology rather than any weapon. It can thread gaps a quarter of its wingspan and switch flight speeds on the fly, but its tiny bill and body make it harmless to anything larger than an insect.

### Rich narrative profile
The budgerigar is a small parrot built for a harsh, unpredictable country. Wild flocks follow rain across inland Australia, feeding on fallen grass seed in the cool of the morning, sheltering motionless in shade through the midday heat, and wheeling around roost trees at dusk. When rain brings a flush of seed, pairs breed at once in tree hollows, and flocks can swell to tens of thousands of birds.

Its strengths are all about getting away. Laboratory work has shown that it flies in two distinct gears, a fast energy-efficient cruise and a slower maneuvering speed, and that it changes gear before it reaches a tight spot. It can fold its wings to slip through openings far narrower than its wingspan, and flock-mates watch for danger and give sharp alarm calls. Its ultraviolet-fluorescent plumage and finely tuned hearing make it one of the most studied birds in sensory science.

In a fight, none of this adds up to much. At 30 grams, with a bill that produces roughly 12 newtons, the budgerigar cannot seriously injure any vertebrate larger than itself, and it has no protection beyond its feathers. Its best result in any matchup is a clean escape into the air or into cover, and against a raptor, snake or cat that gets close, it has no answer.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 28 g (BirdLife) to 30–40 g (Wikipedia); 30 g is the ADW average and sits in the middle.
- **Speed:** 9.4 m/s is a measured preferred cruising speed in a tunnel, not a maximum; no escape-burst maximum was found.
- **Lifespan:** only captive values exist (7.8 y average, 21 y maximum); wild survival is unknown.
- **Bite:** the 12.4 N value comes from a single dissected bird.
- **Predators:** no species-specific predation study was read; predator statements are kept general.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hummingbird (3.8 g; A5 D8 Ag98 St82):** the budgerigar is about eight times heavier and has a real (if small) biting bill, so Attack is slightly higher (6.0). The hummingbird keeps the roster's top Agility (hovering, reverse flight) and higher Stamina (migration-scale flight).
- **Versus Cockatoo (0.8 kg; A21 D13 Ag86 I94) and Macaw (1.2 kg; A24 Ag86 I86):** the large parrots are 25–40 times heavier with bites of 167–444 N, so they stay far above in Attack and Defense. The budgerigar edges them in Agility (88.0) for its gap flight but sits far below in Intelligence (70.0 vs 86–94).
- **Versus Cockatiel (0.09 kg, this batch):** three times lighter; slightly lower Attack and Defense, slightly higher Agility, similar Intelligence.
- **Versus Black Widow (1 g; A24):** the black widow's much higher Attack comes from venom; the budgerigar has none and stays at 6.0.
- **Anti-inflation check:** speech mimicry is scored under Intelligence and Abilities, not Attack or Special. Special (56.0) stays below the hummingbird (79.0) and cockatoo (76.0).
