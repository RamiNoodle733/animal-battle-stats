# Orca (*Orcinus orca*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Common name:** Orca / killer whale
- **Taxon:** *Orcinus orca* (Linnaeus, 1758), family Delphinidae.
- **Living/extinct:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime fully mature adult male, **5,000.0 kg** and **650.0 cm** long. This is deliberately representative rather than record-sized. Aquarium of the Pacific gives males 5.8-6.7 m and 3,628-5,442 kg; ADW reports an 8 m / 7,200 kg male average from a different synthesis, illustrating real population/ecotype variation.
- **Dimorphism/population variation:** males are materially larger and more heavily built than females, with larger pectoral flippers/flukes and a mature dorsal fin up to about 1.8 m. Ecotypes vary strongly in body size, diet, acoustics, social structure and hunting behavior. The canonical animal is therefore a generalized large mature male, not a claim that every population has the same morphology or prey specialization.

## 2. Physical measurements
- **Mass:** representative canonical 5,000 kg. Institutional male range 3,628-5,442 kg; much larger exceptional males exist. NOAA lists the species up to 11 tons, while USFWS notes males >6 tonnes and exceptional reports nearer 8 tonnes. Record values are not used as the baseline.
- **Length:** canonical 650 cm; institutional adult-male range 580-670 cm. Maximum recorded males approach 9.8 m.
- **Height:** no meaningful terrestrial standing-height field. Mature male dorsal fin can reach ~180 cm, but that is an appendage dimension, not body height. Proposed `height_cm = 0.0`.
- **Speed:** USFWS reports speeds exceeding 56 km/h, equivalent to **15.6 m/s**. This is an agency-reported maximum rather than an instrumented physiological trial, so confidence is moderate.
- **Lifespan:** resident-population evidence gives average male life expectancy near 29-31 years and maximum male longevity around 40-60 years. Canonical `lifespan_years = 50.0` represents a defensible mature-male upper-normal lifespan rather than the female 80-90+ year extreme.
- **Bite force:** `bite_force_psi = 0.0`. No defensible measured species-specific bite pressure was found. The live site's 19,000 psi is treated as an unsupported legacy value and is rejected.
- **Teeth:** roughly 40-56 robust conical teeth. Aquarium of the Pacific gives about 7.6 cm per tooth; Smithsonian educational casts measure individual teeth at 10 x 3 x 2 cm. These sources describe different specimens/context, so 7.6-10 cm is retained as a useful range rather than forcing one exact weapon length.
- **Dorsal fin:** mature males up to ~1.8 m; not an offensive weapon.
- **Blubber:** approximately 7.6-10 cm in a general killer-whale anatomical synthesis, providing insulation, energy storage and some soft-tissue buffering, but it is not armor.

## 4. Combat biology
### Weapons and offensive mechanics
The primary weapon is a huge, muscular head-and-jaw system carrying interlocking conical teeth designed to seize and tear rather than chew. At multi-tonne body mass, bites can be paired with ramming, shaking, dragging, drowning, tail/body impacts and cooperative restraint. Confirmed prey spans fish, pinnipeds, cetaceans, sharks and other marine vertebrates. A 2025 genetic study confirmed killer-whale DNA in the major bite area of a 4.7 m white shark carcass whose liver and other organs were missing, supporting genuine predation on very large dangerous prey without requiring a fictional PSI number.

### Defense and durability
The main defenses are enormous body mass, streamlined muscular bulk, thick skin/blubber and mobility. There is no hard armor. Blubber helps thermoregulation and energy storage and can buffer superficial trauma, but penetrating wounds remain meaningful. Adults are effectively apex predators with no routine nonhuman predator documented at comparable frequency, although disease, toxins, starvation, entanglement and trauma remain real vulnerabilities.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Orcas combine high straight-line speed with unusually precise aquatic control for their size. Pectoral flippers, flukes and flexible body control support rapid turns, acceleration, surfacing attacks and coordinated positioning. Their agility is elite among multi-tonne animals, but should not be confused with the instantaneous turning radius of tiny swimmers.

### Endurance
Satellite-tagged offshore killer whales recorded median qualifying dives around 3.9-4.1 min, maxima of 10.1-12.3 min, and depths to 479.5 m. A 2026 tropical study recorded travel totaling 4,002 km over 8-41 tag days and a maximum dive depth of 528 m. These are strong indicators of sustained marine locomotion, though they do not mean maximum-speed swimming can be held indefinitely.

### Senses
Vision functions above and below water. Biosonar is a major combat/foraging asset: resident killer whales can detect prey at 100 m or more, discriminate fine targets, and increase click rate during pursuit. Acoustic-tag research links rapid buzzes to close-range salmon capture. Mammal-hunting ecotypes may reduce vocal activity to avoid alerting acoustically sensitive prey, so echolocation use is ecotype/context dependent.

### Intelligence and tactics
Killer whales possess exceptionally flexible learned hunting systems, group-specific vocal dialects and socially transmitted traditions. Antarctic pack-ice killer whales were observed creating coordinated waves to wash seals from floes, with 12 of 16 attacked Weddell seals taken and repeated waves during attacks. Other populations use intentional stranding, prey sharing, fish herding or specialized shark predation. These population traditions should not be collapsed into the claim that every individual knows every technique.

### Fighting, predation and aggression
Intraspecific conflict exists, but the strongest direct combat evidence comes from predation. Orcas can attack prey larger than themselves and use role differentiation, repeated passes, body impacts, jaw restraint and group coordination. A solitary canonical male retains enormous individual power, but group hunting is scored chiefly under tactics/intelligence rather than multiplying its raw physical power.

### Environmental strengths and limitations
- **Strong:** open/coastal ocean, cold water, three-dimensional maneuver space, deep water, environments where sonar and group positioning are useful.
- **Weak:** terrestrial or extremely shallow settings, entangling obstacles, prolonged acoustic masking, and any matchup that removes access to water.
- **Good matchup archetypes:** most aquatic vertebrates below its mass, air-breathing marine prey vulnerable to restraint/drowning, prey that can be isolated or exhausted.
- **Bad matchup archetypes:** vastly larger aquatic megafauna in a strict solo fight, heavily protected opponents that cannot be gripped effectively, or any terrestrial matchup where the orca strands.

## 6. Proposed six headline ratings
- **Attack: 94.0** - Multi-tonne power, large gripping/tearing dentition, ramming and documented killing of formidable marine prey. Kept below a 100 ceiling because the animal lacks the sheer weapon scale of the largest extinct/megafaunal anchors and because unsupported bite PSI is excluded.
- **Defense: 78.0** - Huge mass, blubber and robust body provide strong survivability, but the orca has no hard armor and remains vulnerable to penetrating trauma.
- **Agility: 84.0** - Excellent acceleration, turning and three-dimensional control for its size. Speed is supporting evidence, not a substitute for agility.
- **Stamina: 88.0** - Sustained ranging, repeated diving and long hunts are strongly documented.
- **Intelligence: 99.0** - Near-ceiling behavioral flexibility, social learning, dialects, prey-specific tactics and coordination. Not set to 100 because the roster ceiling should remain reserved for an unambiguous maximum.
- **Special: 94.0** - High-resolution biosonar and culturally learned cooperative hunting materially alter matchups. General size and strength are not counted again here.

## 7. Abilities and traits
### Special abilities
1. **Biosonar Target Lock** - Broadband echolocation can detect prey at 100+ m in studied resident whales, discriminate targets and transition to rapid close-range buzzes during capture.
2. **Coordinated Wave Hunt** - Grounded in Antarctic pack-ice killer whales that synchronously generate waves to dislodge seals from ice, demonstrating deliberate environmental manipulation and team timing.

### Unique traits
1. **Cultural Predator Toolkit** - Distinct populations socially transmit prey preferences, vocal dialects and specialized hunting traditions rather than relying on one fixed species-wide strategy.
2. **Multi-Tonne Delphinid Frame** - The largest dolphin combines cetacean streamlining with a mature male body around several tonnes and a dorsal fin that can approach 1.8 m.

## 8. Expanded profile
### Habitat and range
Cosmopolitan across the world's oceans, from polar waters to tropical seas, occupying coastal and offshore habitats. Local ecotypes differ substantially in range use and ecology.

### Diet and ecology
A top marine predator with population-specific diets including salmon and other fish, seals and sea lions, cetaceans, sharks, rays, cephalopods, birds and reptiles. Diet specialization matters: fish-eating residents and mammal-eating Bigg's killer whales differ in acoustic behavior and social ecology.

### Social structure and life history
Many populations center on stable matrilineal social units. Vocal dialects are socially learned. Males become sexually mature around the early teen years but continue morphological maturation afterward. Male longevity is shorter than female longevity; Canadian assessments estimate roughly 29-31 years average life expectancy for males surviving infancy in well-studied resident populations and about 40-60 years maximum longevity.

### Conservation
Species-level assessments are complicated by distinct populations/ecotypes. NOAA lists Southern Resident killer whales as endangered under the U.S. ESA and identifies prey limitation, contaminants, vessel/noise disturbance, entanglement and oil spills among threats.

### Adaptations
Fusiform body, powerful flukes, large pectoral flippers, countershading, thick blubber, large conical dentition, sophisticated hearing and biosonar, and unusually flexible learned foraging behavior.

### Human interaction
Wild orcas are intensely studied and culturally prominent. Vessel noise can reduce foraging performance in endangered Southern Residents. Historical capture, pollution, prey depletion and disturbance have affected populations.

### Fun facts
- The orca is the largest living member of the dolphin family.
- Mature male dorsal fins can reach about 1.8 m.
- Resident killer-whale echolocation can detect prey at 100 m or more in studied contexts.
- Antarctic pack-ice whales have been documented repeatedly making waves to wash seals from ice floes.
- Killer-whale groups possess socially learned vocal dialects.
- Genetic evidence has confirmed killer-whale predation on a 4.7 m white shark in Australia.

### Concise site summary
A multi-tonne apex dolphin combining huge jaws and swimming power with precision biosonar, extreme behavioral flexibility and some of the most sophisticated socially learned hunting tactics known in nonhuman predators.

### Narrative profile
An orca's danger is not reducible to size. A prime male carries several tonnes of streamlined muscle and a mouth of large conical teeth, yet its defining advantage is control: control of position, acoustic information, prey movement and, in social hunts, the behavior of teammates. Fish-eating populations can acoustically search for and discriminate prey, while other populations suppress sound around marine mammals. Pack-ice hunters manufacture waves; other groups strand intentionally or specialize on sharks and cetaceans. This diversity is why the profile separates universal anatomy from population-specific culture. A solitary male should not receive the force of an entire pod, but he still brings elite aquatic mobility, massive physical power, sophisticated perception and a learned tactical repertoire.

## 11. Confidence and conflicts
- **High:** taxonomy, sexual dimorphism, male size order, tooth morphology, biosonar, cooperative hunting, social learning.
- **Moderate:** canonical mass/length because ecotypes and institutional syntheses differ; chosen values intentionally avoid records.
- **Moderate:** 15.6 m/s maximum because an agency reports >56 km/h but a primary instrumented maximum was not recovered.
- **Population-specific:** longevity, diet, dive behavior, dialect and hunting tactics. Exact values from residents, offshore whales, Antarctic type B or tropical whales are not silently generalized.
- **Unsupported:** bite PSI. Retained at 0.0.

## 12. Cross-animal normalization notes
- Attack 94.0 is far above Narwhal 61.0, Moose 69.0 and Orangutan 57.0 because a 5-tonne orca combines far greater absolute mass with effective large dentition and documented predation on formidable marine vertebrates. It remains below a dataset ceiling because 100 must represent the strongest roster-scale weapon systems, not fame or apex status.
- Defense 78.0 is strong but deliberately lower than Attack. Multi-tonne mass and blubber improve survivability, but they are not armor.
- Agility 84.0 is comparable to elite maneuverers despite huge mass because aquatic control is exceptional, but it does not exceed the most extreme small-bodied precision specialists merely because the orca is fast.
- Intelligence 99.0 is above Orangutan 96.0 only as a narrow editorial judgment based on highly flexible socially learned hunting, acoustic culture and coordination. This is not a claim of general cognitive superiority across every domain and should be revisited at the 150-animal checkpoint.
- Special 94.0 is driven by biosonar plus learned environmental manipulation, not by repeating raw power or intelligence.
- No proportional-strength leakage, speed/agility substitution or fabricated PSI is used.
