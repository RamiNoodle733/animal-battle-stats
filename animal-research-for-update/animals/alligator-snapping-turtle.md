# Alligator Snapping Turtle (*Macrochelys temminckii*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Alligator Snapping Turtle
- **Scientific name:** *Macrochelys temminckii* (Troost, 1835), family Chelydridae
- **Exact animal represented:** the alligator snapping turtle of the Mississippi River basin and other Gulf of Mexico drainages west of the Suwannee system. The Suwannee alligator snapping turtle (*Macrochelys suwanniensis*) of Florida and Georgia is now treated as a separate species and is used here only as a size reference. This is not the much smaller common snapping turtle (*Chelydra serpentina*), which the roster already covers as Snapping Turtle.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **45.0 kg** with a **58.0 cm straight carapace length**, representing a large prime male rather than a record animal.
- **Sex choice:** males are clearly the larger sex. Indiana DNR gives adult males up to about 76 cm (30 in) and more than 90 kg (200 lb), with females reaching about 60 cm (24 in). In the Suwannee species, Santa Fe River males averaged 530.7 mm carapace length and 34.0 kg versus 424.0 mm and 17.2 kg for females.
- **Population variation:** field population averages are much lower than headline sizes because samples include many juveniles and females; published population means range from about 13.5 to 21 kg. Record-weight reports of about 96 kg (wild) and 113.9 kg are exceptional. Canonical 45 kg sits above the Suwannee male mean, reflecting the larger size of *M. temminckii*, and far below records.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Indiana DNR: adult males can exceed **90 kg (200 lb)**.
- Johnston et al. (2015), Suwannee alligator snapping turtle, Santa Fe River: adult males mean **34.0 kg**; adult females **17.2 kg**.
- Other population samples: means of about 13.5, 19.7 and 21.1 kg (mixed sexes and ages).
- Record reports: **113.9 kg** and a largest wild specimen of about **96 kg**. Dimensions.com (tertiary) lists 80–114 kg, which describes the largest animals, not typical adults.
- **Canonical:** **45.0 kg**. This is a large prime male: heavier than the mean male of the smaller Suwannee species and well under half of record weights.
- **Confidence:** Moderate. No large male-only dataset for *M. temminckii* was recovered.

### Length
- Record carapace length about **80 cm**; Indiana DNR adult males up to about **76 cm**; females to about 60 cm.
- Suwannee males averaged **53.1–55.2 cm** straight midline carapace length (two summaries of the same study).
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 58.0 cm straight carapace length.** Turtle size is measured by carapace length; no published head-to-tail total length was found, so this field uses the carapace convention instead of reconstructing a total length. The thick tail and large head make the whole animal considerably longer.
- **Confidence:** Moderate.

### Height
No standard height measurement exists. **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Speed
No measured land or water maximum was found. **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (unresolved). The species is a sit-and-wait ambush predator.

### Lifespan
- A search-result summary reported an 80-year captive record for a snapping turtle, but it was not clear whether it referred to this species. No wild average lifespan was found.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 0.0** (unresolved). Long life is widely reported, but no representative figure was verified in this run.

### Bite and dentition
- Wikipedia reports a measured **158 ± 18 kgf (about 1,550 ± 180 N)**; the primary study behind that figure was not recovered.
- A 2023 *Southeastern Naturalist* study measured bite force in **62 alligator snapping turtles** and 33 common snapping turtles. Bite force rose with size in both species, and alligator snapping turtles produced higher maximum bite forces throughout growth, helped by their much larger adult size. Secondary summaries cite maxima near **1,872 N** from this work; that figure was not confirmed from the paper itself.
- Popular claims of "1,000 PSI" are unverified conversions and are rejected.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** Measured values are forces, not pressures.
- The jaws end in a sharply hooked beak; the head is very large relative to the body.

### Defensive structure
The carapace is thick and carries three pronounced rows of raised keels, which give the species its "alligator" name. As in other snapping turtles the plastron is small, and the large head, limbs and tail cannot be fully withdrawn. This is strong dorsal armor with exposed edges.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The main weapon is a massive head with a hooked beak and powerful jaw muscles. The turtle lies still on the bottom with its mouth open and wriggles a pink, worm-like appendage on the floor of its mouth. When a fish investigates the lure, the jaws snap shut. The same bite is used on other turtles, molluscs and occasional mammals. Measured bite forces exceed those of the common snapping turtle at every size, and a 45 kg male bites far harder than the 12 kg common snapper on the roster. The weakness is delivery: the bite reaches only what comes within range of the head.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
This is a slow, heavy, largely sedentary animal. It moves along river bottoms and through submerged cover, and only nesting females regularly leave the water. Oklahoma animals had an average linear home range of about 778 m, though individuals can make long movements. Whole-body agility is very low; the fast part is the head strike.

### Endurance
Ectothermic physiology and an ambush lifestyle favor long, motionless waits rather than sustained effort. It can stay submerged for long periods but is not built for prolonged high-output fighting.

### Senses
Neonate luring experiments tested visual, chemical and mechanical cues in eliciting luring and attack, showing that the turtle responds to several prey cues. No specialized long-range sense is known.

### Intelligence and tactics
Luring is an innate, highly effective tactic rather than evidence of flexible problem solving. The study of neonates described four phases: waiting, luring, attack and prey handling. Tactics beyond ambush are limited.

### Sociality and hunting
Solitary. Adults prefer areas with submerged cover under a high overhead canopy, where they wait for prey.

### Fighting and aggression
When handled or cornered on land it gapes and bites hard, and the bite can seriously injure fingers. In water it more often stays still or retreats. There is no evidence of active pursuit of large animals.

### Environmental strengths
- Murky rivers, oxbows and swamps with submerged cover.
- Close-range fights where an opponent's head or limb comes within reach.
- Bottom ambush of fish and other turtles.

### Weaknesses
- Very slow whole-body movement on land and in water.
- Small plastron and exposed head, limbs and tail.
- Offense depends entirely on the opponent coming within reach.
- Limited sustained high-output effort.
- Almost no tactical options beyond ambush and defensive biting.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** fish, other turtles, small aquatic animals and anything that puts a head or limb near its jaws.
- **Mixed:** medium carnivores that try to bite the shell and risk a counter-bite.
- **Bad:** large crocodilians and big predators that can crush or flip it, fast opponents that simply avoid the head, and any fight away from water.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Tongue-Worm Lure** — a pink, worm-like appendage on the floor of the mouth is wriggled while the turtle lies still with its jaws open. Fish swim into the mouth to investigate and the jaws snap shut. In battle this lets it strike first at anything that approaches its head.
2. **Beak-Trap Bite** — a massive head and hooked beak deliver a bite measured stronger than the common snapping turtle's at every body size. It punishes any opponent that puts a limb, head or snout within reach.

### Unique traits
1. **Ridged River Fortress** — a thick carapace with three rows of raised keels gives it the look of a spiked boulder and strong protection from above.
2. **North America's Heaviest Freshwater Turtle** — adult males can exceed 90 kg, with record weights above 110 kg, far larger than any other freshwater turtle on the continent.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Alligator snapping turtles live in large rivers, oxbow lakes, bayous, swamps and reservoirs in the Mississippi River basin and neighbouring Gulf of Mexico drainages of the south-central and southeastern United States, north to Indiana. Radio-tracked adults preferred areas with submerged cover combined with a high percentage of overhead canopy.

### Diet and ecology
The diet is broad. Studies from Arkansas and Louisiana and species accounts list fish, molluscs and other turtles, especially musk turtles, as frequent prey, along with plant food such as acorns. Medium-sized mammals, including opossums, raccoons and armadillos, are taken occasionally.

### Social structure
Solitary. Adults do not form groups, and contact between individuals is mainly during mating.

### Reproduction and life history
Only nesting females regularly leave the water. Nests are dug in sandy soil usually within about 20 m of water, and females lay about 17–52 eggs in a single clutch each year. With only one clutch a year and a population decline measured over three generations, losses of adults take a long time to recover.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List (2025 assessment), based on a population reduction of at least 50% over three generations. Threats named in the assessment summary include pollution, residential and commercial development, climate change, and invasive species and diseases; historical harvest for meat also reduced many populations. In the United States, the Fish and Wildlife Service proposed listing it as threatened under the Endangered Species Act in 2021; the Suwannee species was listed as threatened in 2024. The final rule for *M. temminckii* was still pending in the sources found in this run.

### Special features
- Worm-like lure on the floor of the mouth.
- Very large head with a sharply hooked beak.
- Thick carapace with three rows of raised keels.
- Small plastron and a head that cannot be withdrawn into the shell.
- Long, thick tail and heavy clawed limbs.
- Algae often grows on the shell, adding camouflage on the river bottom.

### Human interaction
Past harvest for meat contributed to its decline. Federal and state agencies now manage it as a species of conservation concern, and the Fish and Wildlife Service has publicized releases of the species back into the wild. A bite from a large adult can cause serious injury to hands.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its tongue lure is the only prey-catching lure inside the mouth of any living reptile.
- Newly hatched turtles already use the lure; experiments described a four-phase hunt of waiting, luring, attack and handling.
- A 2023 study measured the bites of **62** alligator snapping turtles and found they out-bite common snapping turtles at every size.
- Record weights above **110 kg** make it one of the heaviest freshwater turtles in the world.
- Nesting females are usually the only adults that ever come onto land.
- Algae growing on its ridged shell helps it look like a mossy rock or log.

### Concise site-ready summary
The alligator snapping turtle is North America's heaviest freshwater turtle and a patient ambush specialist. A 45 kg adult male lies still on the river bottom, wriggles a worm-shaped lure on its tongue and snaps its huge hooked beak shut on anything that comes close. Its ridged shell is strong armor, but it is very slow and can only hurt what comes within reach of its head.

### Rich narrative profile
On the bottom of a muddy southern river, a large alligator snapping turtle can pass for a log or a rock. Algae coats the three ridged rows of its shell, and it may not move for long stretches. Its mouth hangs open, and on the floor of that mouth a pink, worm-shaped appendage twitches. When a fish comes to inspect the "worm", the jaws close faster than the fish can turn.

That ambush is the whole fighting style. The head is huge, the beak is sharply hooked, and bite tests on 62 animals showed that alligator snapping turtles out-bite common snapping turtles at every size. A big male weighs several times as much as the common snapper on the roster, so the bite is correspondingly more dangerous. The shell adds real protection from above, but the plastron is small, the head cannot be pulled in, and the animal is very slow. An opponent that avoids the front of the head and attacks from the side, or simply walks away, faces little danger.

In ABS terms the alligator snapping turtle is an armored close-range trap. It earns solid Attack and good Defense, a low Agility score and modest Stamina and Intelligence. Its Special is high because the tongue lure is a genuine one-of-a-kind hunting mechanism. The species is now Endangered, and much of its recent history has been shaped by harvest for meat.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** tertiary sources describe adults as 80–114 kg, which matches record animals, not typical males. Field means are far lower. Canonical 45 kg is a judgment between the Suwannee male mean and record values.
- **Length:** only carapace length is available; the field uses that convention and is not comparable to a head-to-tail total length.
- **Bite:** 1,550 N (Wikipedia) and about 1,872 N (secondary summaries of the 2023 study) could not be traced to exact primary values; PSI stays 0.0.
- **Lifespan:** no verified typical or maximum lifespan for this species was found in this run.
- **Taxonomy:** older sources treat the Suwannee animals as part of this species.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Snapping Turtle (12 kg; 47.0 / 55.0 / 42.0 / 51.0 / 42.0 / 53.0):** nearly four times the canonical mass and measured stronger bites at every size put the alligator snapper above it in Attack, Defense and Weaponry. It sits below the common snapper in Agility and Stamina because it is a heavier, more sedentary ambusher, and below it in Intelligence because its hunting is dominated by an innate lure routine. Special is higher because the lure is unique.
- **Versus Galapagos Tortoise (102 kg; Defense 76.0):** the tortoise keeps higher Defense because its shell is more complete and it can withdraw; the snapper's small plastron and exposed head cap its armor.
- **Versus Alligator (200 kg; Attack 84.0):** the alligator's far greater mass and crocodilian bite keep it much higher in Attack; an alligator can crush turtle shells.
- **Versus Komodo Dragon (85 kg; Attack 62.0):** the Komodo keeps higher Attack for its mobility and larger mass; the turtle is much better protected.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Attack 55.0 reflects a strong bite with very short reach. Special 68.0 is driven by the lure, not by the bite or shell, which are already counted in Attack and Defense.
