# Black Caiman (*Melanosuchus niger*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Black Caiman
- **Scientific name:** *Melanosuchus niger* (Spix, 1825), family Alligatoridae, subfamily Caimaninae
- **Exact animal represented:** the black caiman of the Amazon basin and neighbouring South American freshwater systems. It is the only living member of its genus and should not be mixed with the smaller spectacled caiman (*Caiman crocodilus*), which shares many of the same lakes.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male, **300.0 kg** and **420.0 cm total length**, representing a large fighting-age male rather than a record giant.
- **Sex choice:** males are the larger sex. Species summaries give adult males over 300 kg at about 4.1 m, with average adults commonly described at 4–5 m. Secondary summaries of skull studies state that adult males carry more massive skulls than like-age females and probably take larger prey. Males are therefore the combat specimen.
- **Population variation:** body size depends heavily on age and on hunting history. Past commercial hide hunting caused steep declines, and harvest studies at Mamirauá found hunters mostly take subadult and small adult males, so present-day populations can be skewed toward smaller animals. Claims of 6 m animals exist, but the largest confirmed animals sit around 5 m and are not canonical.

- Teeth are conical gripping teeth. Large prey is held, drowned and broken up by head shaking and rolling.

### Defensive structure
Species overviews describe the osteoderms (bony plates in the skin) as denser than those of the spectacled caiman. The back and neck are well armored, the skull is heavy, and the belly, eyes, throat and limb joints are the soft points.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The jaws are the main weapon. A black caiman hunts mainly at night, lying low in the water near the bank, then lunges with a tail-driven burst, seizes with conical teeth and pulls the victim into deeper water. Fish make up much of the diet, but adults take water birds and mammals at the water's edge, including capybara. Secondary accounts report that adults over about 2 m attack capybara-sized mammals when the chance arises. The measured 4,310 N bite of a mid-sized 103 kg animal, scaled up in a 300 kg male, places this species in the elite crocodilian bite class.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Strong tail propulsion and whole-body control make it effective in flooded forest, lakes and slow rivers. On land it can high-walk and make short bursts but is not a pursuit animal. Maneuverability is moderate: dangerous at the shoreline, limited in open terrain.

### Endurance
Like other crocodilians it runs on a low-cost ectothermic budget. It waits for long periods and can go a long time between large meals, but maximal effort is brief. Sustained high-output fighting is a weakness.

### Senses
Large eyes suit its nocturnal hunting. Crocodilians, including caimans, carry dome-shaped integumentary sensory organs that respond to touch, pressure and vibration; Leitch and Catania studied these organs in spectacled caimans and Nile crocodiles. The black caiman therefore has strong short-range detection of ripples in dark or murky water. No black-caiman-specific sensory thresholds were found.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior centres on ambush placement at night and seasonal movement between flooded forest and dry-season lakes. No documented cooperative hunting was found for this species, unlike the reports of fish barriers in Nile crocodiles. Tactics are effective but narrow.

### Sociality and hunting
Adults are mostly solitary hunters but gather in large numbers in lakes as floodplain water levels fall. Females guard nest mounds and stay near hatchling pods, which can contain young from more than one nest.

### Fighting and aggression
Adults defend space and females aggressively guard nests. The species will attack people: a case report from Rondônia State, Brazil, described an 11-year-old girl killed by a black caiman, and a review of human-wildlife conflict in the tropics lists crocodilian attacks among the most serious conflicts. Attacks are less frequently documented than for the Nile or saltwater crocodile.

### Environmental strengths
- Dark-water lakes, slow rivers and flooded forest at night.
- Shorelines where mammals come to drink or cross.
- Deep water next to the bank, where seized prey can be drowned.
- Warm, stable tropical temperatures that keep ectothermic performance high.

### Weaknesses
- Softer belly, eyes, throat and limb joints.
- Short limbs and poor dry-land pursuit.
- Offense depends on landing the first bite.
- Limited sustained high-output effort.
- Jaguars are documented predators of caimans in the Amazon floodplain, showing that a powerful cat can overcome caimans, at least smaller ones, at the water's edge.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** medium and large land animals that must approach water; poor swimmers; fish and water birds; soft-skinned opponents vulnerable to a seize-and-drown attack.
- **Mixed:** large cats that attack from the bank or from above; heavy horned or tusked herbivores in shallow water.
- **Bad:** much larger crocodilians, hippo-class megafauna, fast opponents that stay away from water, and fights on open dry ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Blackwater Night Ambush** — hunts mainly after dark, lying low in dark Amazon water with its black body almost invisible, then lunges at fish, birds and mammals at the bank. In a battle this gives it first-strike advantage at night and at the water's edge.
2. **Capybara Takedown** — seizes large mammals such as capybara, drags them into deep water and holds or rolls until they drown. This turns any shoreline into a trap for land animals of up to several times human size.

### Unique traits
1. **Amazon's Largest Predator** — the biggest predator in the Amazon basin and the largest member of the alligator family, with adults commonly 4–5 m long and males over 300 kg.
2. **Night-Black Armor** — dark scales and osteoderms described as denser than those of the spectacled caiman give it both concealment in dark water and strong dorsal protection.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Black caimans live in slow-moving rivers, lakes, flooded forest (várzea) and seasonally flooded savannas across the Amazon basin and neighbouring South American freshwater systems, with records from Brazil, Ecuador, Bolivia and Suriname among other countries. During the dry season they gather in lakes and deeper channels as floodplain water drains away.

### Diet and ecology
Hatchlings and juveniles eat insects, crustaceans and other invertebrates, which are largely replaced by vertebrate prey as they grow. Adults eat mainly fish, plus water birds and mammals that come near the water, such as capybara. Carrion is taken. Species summaries repeat reports of black caimans eating anacondas and even jaguars, but these are anecdotal. The relationship with jaguars runs both ways: jaguars prey on caimans in the Mamirauá reserve and are among the main predators of black caiman eggs.

### Social structure
Adults are mostly solitary hunters but tolerate one another where food and water concentrate in the dry season. Males defend space during breeding. Females guard nests, and hatchlings stay together in pods that may mix young from several nests, often with an adult female nearby.

### Reproduction and life history
Females build mound nests of leaf litter, twigs and decaying vegetation in the dry season, usually a few metres to tens of metres from water. Clutches average around 40 eggs. Females defend the nest aggressively, yet eggs are still taken by jaguars, tegu lizards, capuchin monkeys and people. Maturity is reported at around 8 years or about 2 m length.

### Conservation
Current species summaries list the black caiman as **Least Concern** (IUCN 3.1). Older assessments placed it in higher-risk categories, including Endangered, after commercial hide hunting caused steep declines. The IUCN Crocodile Specialist Group now describes the species as widespread and locally abundant, with substantial recovery in several parts of its range. Caiman meat is still harvested in parts of the Amazon, and egg collection by people can affect nest success.

### Special features
- Black, lightly patterned body that is hard to see in dark water at night.
- Dense dorsal osteoderms and a heavy skull.
- Large eyes suited to night hunting.
- Pressure-sensitive skin organs typical of crocodilians.
- Powerful tail for swimming and lunging.
- Conical gripping teeth that are replaced through life.

### Human interaction
The species was hunted heavily for its hide and is still taken for meat. Study of commercial hunting in the Mamirauá Sustainable Development Reserve estimated an annual harvest of about 5,230 black caiman and found that hunters mostly take subadult and small adult males. Fatal attacks on people are documented but rare. In 2004 Amazonas State agencies ran an experimental, authorized caiman harvest in the Mamirauá reserve as a trial of regulated use.

### Genuine fun facts
- It is the largest member of the alligator family, larger than the American alligator.
- A 3 m, 103 kg black caiman bit with a measured **4,310 N** in crocodilian bite tests.
- Hunters at Mamirauá were estimated to take about **5,230 black caiman** a year, mostly subadult and small adult males.
- Hatchlings from different nests can join into one pod guarded by an adult female.
- Jaguars both hunt caimans and raid black caiman nests on the Amazon floodplain.
- Its nest is a mound of leaves and twigs, often around half a metre to 80 cm tall.

### Concise site-ready summary
The black caiman is the Amazon's largest predator and the biggest member of the alligator family. A 300 kg adult male combines an elite crocodilian bite, dense bony armor and a dark body built for night ambush at the water's edge, where it drags capybara-sized prey under to drown. It is formidable at the shoreline but tires quickly and is far less dangerous on open land.

### Rich narrative profile
After dark, a big black caiman is close to invisible. Its black scales merge with the tea-coloured water of Amazon lakes and flooded forest, and only the eyes and nostrils break the surface. Pressure-sensitive organs in its skin pick up ripples as fish move or a capybara wades in to drink. When the moment comes, the tail drives a short, violent lunge, the conical teeth close, and the fight moves into deep water where the caiman holds and rolls until the prey drowns.

This is a heavyweight crocodilian built on the same plan as the Nile crocodile and the American alligator. A mid-sized 103 kg animal was measured biting at 4,310 N, and a full-grown male is about three times heavier. Dense osteoderms cover its back, and its skull is massive. Yet the species also shows the limits of that plan. Like all crocodilians it is a burst animal, strong for seconds rather than minutes, and it has soft spots on the belly, eyes and joints. Jaguars on the Amazon floodplain kill caimans and dig up their eggs, a reminder that a powerful, agile cat can win at the water's edge.

In ABS terms the black caiman sits between the alligator and the Nile crocodile. It earns elite Attack and very strong Defense from its bite, mass and armor, while Agility and Stamina stay moderate. Its Intelligence is competent but narrow, and its Special reflects the night ambush and pressure-sensing skin shared with other crocodilians rather than a unique weapon. Near water at night it is one of the most dangerous animals in South America; on open ground it is far easier to avoid.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** published figures range from 95 to over 400 kg and mix sexes. Canonical 300 kg follows the male-specific figure.
- **Maximum size:** 6 m claims are repeated but unconfirmed; the CSG account treats about 5 m as solid.
- **Bite:** the 4,310 N value belongs to a 103 kg animal; no large-male measurement was found. `0.0` PSI means unresolved, not weak.
- **Lifespan:** the only figure found is a captive record.
- **Conservation status:** current summaries give Least Concern; older IUCN listings were higher-risk categories. The live IUCN page was not opened.
- **Diet anecdotes:** claims of black caimans eating jaguars and anacondas are anecdotal and not used for ratings.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Alligator (200 kg; 84.0 / 77.0 / 49.0 / 40.0 / 51.0 / 72.0):** the black caiman is heavier and larger with the same weapon system, so Attack, Defense, Raw Power and Weaponry sit a few points higher. Agility, Stamina and Intelligence stay close because both share burst physiology and solitary ambush habits.
- **Versus Nile Crocodile (400 kg; 89.0 / 80.0 / 53.0 / 44.0 / 60.0 / 78.0):** the Nile crocodile keeps higher Attack and Defense for its greater canonical mass, and higher Intelligence and Special for documented cooperative fish barriers and shared carcass handling, which were not found for the black caiman.
- **Versus Saltwater Crocodile (500 kg; Attack 92.0):** the saltie remains clearly above in every force category.
- **Versus Jaguar (100 kg; Attack 68.0, Agility 82.0):** jaguars do kill caimans, mostly smaller ones, by attacking from the bank. The caiman's Attack and Defense are far higher, while the jaguar keeps a large Agility and Intelligence advantage; this matches a matchup that depends on who controls the waterline.
- **Versus Green Anaconda (50 kg; Attack 70.0):** the caiman's mass, armor and bite place it well above the anaconda in Attack and Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** Attack 87.0 stays below the Nile crocodile, saltwater crocodile and hippo. Stamina 41.0 reflects burst physiology. Ferocity is high for documented attacks and bold predation, but below the Nile crocodile because attacks on people are less frequent.
