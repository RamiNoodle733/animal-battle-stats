# Giant Panda (*Ailuropoda melanoleuca*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Giant panda
- **Scientific name:** *Ailuropoda melanoleuca* (David, 1869)
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild giant panda of the mountain bamboo forests of central China, a member of the bear family (Ursidae). It is not the red panda (*Ailurus fulgens*), which is already on the roster and belongs to a separate family. The paler Qinling population is treated as part of the same species.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male, **115.0 kg**, about **160.0 cm** head-body length. This is a representative wild male, not a heavy captive animal.
- **Sex choice:** males are typically 10–20% larger than females and are the sex that competes for mates, so the adult male is the combat baseline.
- **Population variation:** adults are usually quoted at 100–115 kg; some males reach about 160 kg. Captive males can be heavier than wild ones (the National Zoo's male Tian Tian weighed about 125 kg), so captive weights are not used as the baseline.

### Bite force
- Christiansen and Wroe (2007) modelled bite forces from skull mechanics across carnivorans. The giant panda's estimated force at the canine tips was about **1,299 N**, a little below the lion (about 1,315 N) and well below the polar bear (about 1,647 N) in the same dataset. Its bite force quotient (about 151) is higher than the lion's (about 124) or the tiger's (about 130), meaning the bite is very strong for the body size.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** The modelled value is force, not pressure, and is not converted.

### Natural weapons
- **Jaws and teeth:** very broad skull with large chewing muscles and wide, flat cheek teeth for crushing bamboo stems. Canines are present and can inflict deep wounds.
- **Claws:** curved non-retractile claws used for climbing and gripping.
- **Pseudo-thumb:** an enlarged wrist bone (radial sesamoid) under a pad of skin that works like an opposable thumb to hold bamboo. It is a grip feature, not a weapon.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Dense, coarse fur and a heavy, stocky build give moderate protection; body mass is the main defense for an adult.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapons and offensive mechanics
The panda's real weapon is its bite. The skull, jaw muscles and teeth are built to crush tough bamboo, and skull modelling places its canine bite force close to a lion's in absolute terms. Claws and forelimbs are strong enough for climbing and pulling bamboo but are not used to hunt. A panda does not stalk, chase or kill large animals, so its offense is defensive: a hard bite and swipes delivered when it is cornered or disturbed.

### Feeding behavior
Bamboo makes up more than 99% of the diet. Pandas eat very large amounts every day, commonly reported at about 12–38 kg depending on the plant part and season, and spend much of the day feeding. They occasionally eat small animals or carrion.

### Intraspecific fighting
Adults live alone and communicate mainly through scent marks and calls. During the short breeding season several males may gather around a female and fight, with bites and swipes.

### Defense and durability
A 115 kg adult with thick fur and a stocky frame has few natural threats; predators such as leopards and dholes are a danger mainly to cubs. Without armor, and with a body not built for long exertion, a panda under sustained attack would tire quickly.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Pandas walk with a slow, rolling gait and climb trees well, especially when young. They rarely run and avoid sharp bursts of activity. Climbing gives an escape route, but on the ground they are slow and cumbersome.

### Endurance
Nie et al. (2015) measured daily energy use in wild pandas at only about 45% of the value expected for a mammal of their size (38% across all pandas measured), with low thyroid hormone levels, smaller internal organs and low physical activity. The panda saves energy rather than spending it, which gives it very low sustained fighting stamina.

### Senses
Smell is important for finding bamboo and reading other pandas' scent marks. Precise sensory thresholds were not sourced in this run, so the score is conservative.

### Intelligence and tactics
Pandas navigate large mountain home ranges, remember bamboo patches and communicate through scent. Combat tactics are simple: avoid, climb, and bite if cornered. No strong evidence of flexible problem-solving in fights was found.

### Aggression and ferocity
Usually placid and avoidant, but not harmless. Three attacks on people by the same captive panda at Beijing Zoo between 2006 and 2009 caused deep bite wounds to legs and arms that needed surgery.

### Environmental strengths
- Dense bamboo forest on steep, cool, wet mountain slopes.
- Trees for climbing out of reach.

### Environmental limitations
- Open ground, heat and any contest that needs speed or long effort.

### Weaknesses
- Very low stamina from an energy-saving physiology.
- Slow, with no sprint or pursuit ability.
- No real offensive technique beyond biting and swiping.
- Low willingness to fight unless cornered.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small and medium animals that come within bite range, and anything that cannot climb once the panda is in a tree.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized bears and medium predators, where the panda's bite is dangerous but its stamina and aggression are lower.
- **Bad:** big cats, large bears, large herbivores with horns or tusks, and fast opponents that can wear it down.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Bamboo-Crusher Bite** — an extremely broad skull, big chewing muscles and flat crushing teeth give a canine bite force modelled at about 1,299 N, close to a lion's. A cornered panda can inflict deep, surgery-level wounds.
2. **Low-Power Mode** — wild pandas use only about 45% of the daily energy expected for their size, with low thyroid hormone levels and small internal organs. They survive on a poor-quality diet, but have almost no reserve for a long fight.

### Unique traits
1. **Radial-Sesamoid Thumb** — an enlarged wrist bone under a skin pad works as a sixth digit, letting the panda grip bamboo stems precisely.
2. **Conservation Comeback** — China's fourth national survey counted 1,864 wild pandas, and in 2016 the IUCN moved the species from Endangered to Vulnerable.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Giant pandas live only in cool, wet mountain forests with dense bamboo understory in six mountain ranges of central China (Sichuan, Shaanxi and Gansu provinces), usually at middle to high elevations.

### Diet and ecology
Bamboo, eaten as shoots, stems and leaves, makes up more than 99% of the diet, with occasional small animals or carrion. Because bamboo is poor food and the panda's gut is that of a carnivore, pandas eat large quantities for most of the day and save energy the rest of the time.

### Social structure
Solitary. Adults keep separate home ranges and communicate by scent marking and calls. Males and females meet briefly during the spring breeding season.

### Reproduction and life history
Females are fertile for only a few days each year. Newborn cubs are tiny and helpless and depend on their mother for well over a year. Twins are born fairly often, but a wild mother usually raises only one.

### Conservation
The giant panda is assessed as **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List, moved down from Endangered in 2016 after China's fourth national survey (2015) counted 1,864 wild pandas. Scientists have argued over whether the change was premature, because habitat fragmentation, bamboo die-offs and climate change remain threats. Panda reserves protect many other species living in the same forests.

### Special features
- Pseudo-thumb formed by an enlarged radial sesamoid bone.
- Broad skull and massive chewing muscles for crushing bamboo.
- Very low daily energy use and low thyroid hormone levels.
- Black-and-white coat.
- Good tree-climbing ability.

### Human interaction
The panda is one of the best-known conservation symbols in the world and is central to China's zoo loan programmes. Attacks on people are rare and almost all involve people entering enclosures; the Beijing Zoo cases between 2006 and 2009 show that an adult panda can still cause serious bite injuries.

### Concise site-ready summary
The giant panda is a 100–115 kg bamboo-eating bear from the mountain forests of central China. Its broad skull gives it a bite modelled close to a lion's, and it can climb well. But it saves energy instead of spending it, rarely runs, and avoids fights unless cornered, so it has little stamina or offensive skill for a sustained battle.

### Rich narrative profile
The giant panda is a bear that lives almost entirely on bamboo. That single fact shapes everything about it as a fighter. To crush tough stems it has one of the broadest skulls and heaviest sets of chewing muscles among the carnivorans, and when researchers modelled its bite, the force at the canines came out close to a lion's. The pseudo-thumb, an enlarged wrist bone, lets it hold bamboo precisely while it eats for much of the day.

The other side of that diet is energy. Bamboo is poor food, and the panda copes by spending as little energy as possible. Measurements published in *Science* found wild pandas using only about 45% of the energy expected for a mammal their size, with low thyroid hormone levels, smaller organs and very little movement. In a fight, that means the panda has almost no reserve: it cannot chase, cannot keep up a long exchange, and generally chooses to walk away or climb a tree.

None of this makes it harmless. An adult male of around 115 kg with thick fur and a crushing bite can badly hurt anything that corners it, as visitors who entered a panda enclosure at Beijing Zoo found out. On the roster it sits near the other medium bears in size and bite, but well below them in stamina, speed and willingness to fight. It is a defensive heavyweight whose best tactic is to avoid battle, and whose worst matchups are fast, persistent predators that can wear it down.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** adult ranges come from a tertiary compilation; captive males can be heavier. 115.0 kg is kept at the top of the usual adult range for a male.
- **Height and speed:** no sourced values; both stay 0.0 rather than being invented.
- **Lifespan:** wild figures are approximate; 18.0 years is a middle value.
- **Bite:** modelled force only. The panda's strong bite for its size is counted in Weaponry and Attack, and only partly in Special.
- **Conservation:** the 2016 downlisting is disputed by some scientists; the current status is still Vulnerable.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Black Bear (120 kg; A56.0 D49.0 Ag70.0 St66.0 I68.0 Sp61.0):** similar mass. The panda gets a slightly higher Attack for its modelled bite, similar Defense, but much lower Agility, Stamina and Intelligence because it rarely runs, spends little energy and shows less behavioral flexibility.
- **Versus Sloth Bear (120 kg; A65.0 D57.0 Ag55.0 Ferocity 91):** the sloth bear's long claws and extreme readiness to fight keep it well above the panda in Attack and Defense despite equal mass.
- **Versus Spectacled Bear (115 kg; A62.0 D55.0 Ag64.0 Sp58.0):** identical mass and a similar plant-crushing jaw, but the spectacled bear is an active climber and forager; the panda stays below in Attack, Agility and Stamina.
- **Versus Red Panda (5 kg; A24.0 D24.0 Ag77.0 Sp67.0):** the giant panda is over twenty times heavier and far above it in Attack and Defense; the small, nimble red panda keeps the Agility lead. Their shared false-thumb feature is named differently (Radial-Sesamoid Thumb here, a trait; False-Thumb Grip is the red panda's ability) and is not counted in the giant panda's Special score beyond a small share.
- **Versus Kodiak Bear and Grizzly Bear:** vastly lower in every physical category; no inversion.
- **Flag:** Intelligence 55.0 is an editorial placement with little direct cognitive evidence; a future audit should check it against other solitary herbivores.
