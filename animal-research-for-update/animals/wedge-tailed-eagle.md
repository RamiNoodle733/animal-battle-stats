# Wedge-tailed Eagle (*Aquila audax*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Wedge-tailed Eagle
- **Scientific name:** *Aquila audax* (Latham, 1801)
- **Exact animal represented:** the living Australian species *Aquila audax*, represented by the widespread mainland subspecies (*A. a. audax*) of the arid and semi-arid interior. The larger Tasmanian subspecies (*A. a. fleayi*) is noted separately.
- **Status:** Living; Least Concern globally. The Tasmanian subspecies is listed federally as Endangered.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **4.2 kg**, **100.0 cm** total length, about **2.2 m** wingspan. Females are the larger sex.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Australian Museum and BirdLife Australia give females **4.2–5.3 kg** and males **3.2–4.0 kg**. ADW gives females 3.2–5.3 kg (average 4.1 kg) and males 2.5–4.0 kg (average 3.2 kg). Other samples: 29 females averaged 3.8 kg on the Nullarbor Plain, 19 females 4.18 kg, and Tasmanian females 4.1 kg.
- **Population notes:** Tasmanian eagles are larger, with longer tails and larger talons. Adults darken for at least the first ten years of life. Density varies with prey: nests are usually 2.5–4 km apart, but can be under 1 km apart when rabbits are abundant.

- Hallux claw: 10 females averaged **44.7 mm** (40.6–48.1 mm); mainland females about 46 mm; Tasmanian females **49.9 mm** (45.5–55.6 mm). Slightly smaller than the golden eagle's, even in proportion, but described as extremely sharp.
- Legs are feathered to the toes; tarsus about 99–140 mm.
- Bill (culmen) about 46–65 mm in females.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bill-force or grip-force measurement exists.

### Vision
- A 1985 *Vision Research* study measured behavioural visual acuity of **132–143 cycles per degree**, matching the calculated anatomical limit of its deep fovea (about 140 c/deg). This is one of the highest acuity values measured in any animal.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. Protection comes from size among Australian birds, flight, wariness around people, and dense plumage.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The feet and talons are the killing tools. The hallux claw (about 4.5–4.6 cm in mainland females) is somewhat smaller than a golden eagle's, but it is very sharp, and the whole foot is feathered to the toes. The bill tears carcasses and kills. Rabbits and hares are the most important live prey, and the species also takes macropods (kangaroos and wallabies), lizards such as bearded dragons, birds over about 100 g and mammals usually over 500 g. It also eats a lot of carrion, including roadkill. It kills lambs occasionally, but lambs are a small part of the diet.

Its most striking offensive behavior is cooperative. Australian Museum and BirdLife Australia both state that a group of eagles working together can kill animals as large as adult kangaroos. In one Central Australian study, about a third of 89 observed hunts were cooperative. One record describes 15 eagles hunting kangaroos, with two chasing at a time and fresh pairs taking over from the circling group.

### Offensive mechanics
It hunts from a perch or from a high soar, often descending in a long slanting stoop. In tandem hunts one eagle distracts or drives the prey while the partner, often unseen, waits to strike. It can hunt in open country and in fairly thick woodland with an open understory. Failed hunts outnumber successful ones.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Long wings and a long tail make it an efficient soarer; BirdLife Australia notes regular flights to almost 2 km altitude, and glider pilots have met the species above 3,000 m. The long tail gives good control, and it attacks hang gliders, paragliders and drones in flight. Agility is scored high but below short-winged forest specialists.

### Endurance
It spends much of the day perched but soars long distances with little effort. Juveniles have moved more than **850 km** in seven to eight months (Australian Museum). Home ranges surround defended nest territories. Stamina is scored strong.

### Senses
Vision is exceptional and measured, not assumed: behavioural acuity of 132–143 cycles per degree, close to the physical limit of its eye. This is the best-documented sensory figure in this batch.

### Intelligence and tactics
Cooperative and tandem hunting, with one bird distracting and another striking, is well documented, and group hunts on kangaroos rotate chasers. The species readily changes diet with prey availability: in the Strzelecki Desert, eagles on the side of the dingo fence with few dingoes ate more kangaroos and bearded dragons and a wider range of species. These behaviors support above-average raptor tactics.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Breeding pairs are territorial and defend the nest area from other wedge-tailed eagles all year. Pairs often stay together for several years. Its scientific name *audax* means "bold", yet it is generally wary and even timid around people on the ground. It is the only bird with a reputation for frequently attacking hang gliders and paragliders.

### Environmental strengths
- Open arid and semi-arid country, where thermals and wide views favor soaring and spotting.
- Open woodland with sparse understory.
- Landscapes with plentiful rabbits, macropods and carrion.

### Weaknesses
- About 4.2 kg in absolute mass.
- Smaller talons than golden or Verreaux's eagles.
- No armor; light skeleton.
- Gorged birds on the ground can struggle to take off.
- Heavy reliance on carrion exposes it to lead poisoning and roadkill collisions.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** rabbits, hares, small macropods, lizards, medium birds, animals caught in the open.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized raptors, foxes and cats, adult kangaroos (winnable mainly by groups).
- **Bad:** large carnivores, great apes, large ungulates, armored animals and any heavy grappler once the eagle is on the ground.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Tag-Team Kangaroo Hunt** — pairs and groups hunt together: one eagle distracts or drives the prey while another strikes, and in large group hunts fresh pairs replace tired chasers. Together they can kill adult kangaroos that no single eagle could handle.
2. **Record-Resolution Vision** — behavioural tests measured acuity of 132–143 cycles per degree, matching the limit of its deep fovea. It can spot prey and threats from far beyond the range at which they can see the eagle.

### Unique traits
1. **Widest Verified Eagle Wingspan** — a Tasmanian female measured 284 cm, the largest verified wingspan for any eagle, and the long wedge-shaped tail is unique among large Australian raptors.
2. **Glider Challenger** — it is the only bird with a reputation for frequently attacking hang gliders and paragliders, and it also attacks drones.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The wedge-tailed eagle is found throughout mainland Australia and Tasmania and on many offshore islands. It uses more habitat types than perhaps any other Aquila eagle, from deserts and arid shrubland to farmland, open woodland and mountain forest with an open understory. It is common across the arid interior wherever prey and nest trees or cliffs are available.

### Diet and ecology
It is a generalist predator and a major scavenger. Rabbits and hares are the most important live prey; macropods, lizards, birds and other mammals are also taken, and carrion, including roadkill kangaroos, makes up a large part of the diet. In the Strzelecki Desert, where dingoes are rare, eagles ate more kangaroos and bearded dragons and a wider range of species than on the other side of the dingo fence. By eating rabbits, it helps limit an invasive pest.

### Social structure
Breeding adults live as long-term territorial pairs. Around each defended nest territory is a larger home range that overlaps with neighbors and non-breeding birds. Pairs, and sometimes larger groups, hunt together.

### Reproduction and life history
Pairs build huge stick nests that are reused for years; nests 1.8 m across, 3 m deep and weighing about 400 kg are known. Eggs are incubated for about 42–45 days, and in lean years the first-hatched chick may kill its nestmate. Plumage darkens over at least ten years. Juveniles disperse widely, one moving more than 850 km in about eight months.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** globally. Persecution was heavy in the past because of supposed lamb killing, but numbers recovered strongly, helped by rabbits and roadkill. The Tasmanian subspecies is listed federally as **Endangered**. Current threats include lead poisoning from ammunition in carcasses: lead was detected in 84% of 62 mainland eagle bones, at severe levels in 4.8%. Rodenticide exposure (residues in 74% of 50 Tasmanian carcasses) and collisions with vehicles while feeding on roadkill are also threats.

### Special features
- Measured visual acuity of 132–143 cycles per degree.
- Very long wings and a long, wedge-shaped tail.
- Legs feathered to the toes.
- Cooperative and tandem hunting.
- Very large, long-used stick nests.

### Human interaction
It was once persecuted for killing lambs, which make up only a small share of its prey. Today it is widely admired, and it is a notorious attacker of hang gliders, paragliders and survey drones. Eagles feeding on roadkill are often hit by vehicles.

### Genuine fun facts
- A Tasmanian female had a verified wingspan of 284 cm, the largest measured for any eagle.
- Its visual acuity was measured at 132–143 cycles per degree, close to the limit set by the optics of its eye.
- Groups of wedge-tailed eagles can kill adult kangaroos.
- Old nests can weigh around 400 kg.
- Its scientific name *audax* means "bold".
- It is famous for attacking hang gliders and paragliders, and eagles once knocked nine of a mining company's drones out of the sky.
- Adults keep getting darker for at least their first ten years.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Wedge-tailed Eagle is Australia's largest bird of prey. The canonical female weighs about 4.2 kg and spans about 2.2 m. It has some of the sharpest measured vision of any animal and hunts rabbits, hares and wallabies, while pairs and groups can bring down adult kangaroos. Alone, its light build and modest talons keep it below the golden eagle in raw killing power.

### Rich narrative profile
The wedge-tailed eagle is built for wide country. Its long wings and long, wedge-shaped tail let it ride thermals to 2 km or more over the Australian interior. From there, eyes measured at 132–143 cycles per degree, close to the physical limit for a vertebrate eye, pick out rabbits, hares, lizards and carcasses far below.

Much of its daily food is ordinary: rabbits, roadkill and whatever is plentiful. What makes it stand out is cooperation. Pairs hunt in tandem, one bird drawing attention while the other strikes. Groups have been seen running down kangaroos in relays, with fresh eagles dropping in to replace tired chasers. In the Strzelecki Desert, eagles on the side of the dingo fence where kangaroos are abundant ate far more of them.

Alone in an absolute matchup, it is a 4.2 kg bird with talons a little smaller than a golden eagle's and no armor. It wins with vision, surprise and, when it has help, numbers. Against a heavy grappler that survives the first strike, one eagle has little left to use.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** some guides give female averages near 4.7 kg; field samples give 3.8–4.3 kg. The canonical 4.2 kg follows the Australian Museum's lower female bound and most sample means.
- **Length:** ADW's 1.0–1.2 m exceeds Wikipedia's 81–106 cm range. The canonical 100 cm is a Queensland female average.
- **Lifespan:** the 5.9-year wild average probably includes young birds and short banding records; adults can clearly live longer.
- **Group kills:** adult kangaroo kills are documented for groups, not single eagles. Attack is scored for one eagle; cooperation goes into Tactics and Special.
- **Speed:** no instrumented measurement; field left at 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Golden Eagle (5.0 kg; A40.0 Ag88.0 St79.0 I66.0 Sp70.0):** a close relative in the same genus with similar hunting style. The wedge-tailed eagle is lighter with a smaller hallux (Attack 38.0) but has measured record vision and documented group hunting (Intelligence 68.0, Special 74.0).
- **Versus Bald Eagle (5.3 kg; A36.0 Ag79.0 St75.0 Sp63.0):** both scavenge heavily, but the wedge-tailed eagle hunts mammals more actively and more cooperatively, so it sits above on Attack, Agility and Special.
- **Versus Martial Eagle (4.8 kg; A41.5):** the martial eagle has larger talons and regularly kills dangerous prey, so it stays above on Attack and Weaponry.
- **Versus Peregrine Falcon (1.0 kg; Se95.0):** the wedge-tailed eagle's Senses of 93.0 is backed by a direct acuity measurement; the peregrine keeps a higher Special because of its instrumented stoop.
- **Anti-inflation check:** kangaroo kills by groups are not counted as solo Attack or Raw Power.
