# Rabbit (*Oryctolagus cuniculus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Rabbit (European rabbit, domestic rabbit, coney)
- **Scientific name:** *Oryctolagus cuniculus* (Linnaeus, 1758)
- **Exact animal represented:** the European rabbit species, including its wild populations and all domestic rabbit breeds, which were bred from it. Not hares (*Lepus*) or American cottontails (*Sylvilagus*), which are different animals.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (wild species plus domestic breeds).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult buck of about **2.0 kg**, a wild-type or medium pet rabbit, rather than a 1 kg dwarf breed or a 5–8 kg giant breed.
- **Sex choice:** the sexes are similar in size. Bucks are chosen because fierce fights erupt among them during the breeding season and dominant bucks hold access to does; does also fight hard over nest sites, sometimes to serious injury or death.
- **Population variation:** wild rabbits in the native Iberian range are generally smaller than introduced populations elsewhere, and size tracks food quality. Domestic breeds span roughly 0.9 kg (Netherland Dwarf class) to more than 5 kg (Flemish Giant class), so the canonical 2.0 kg animal represents the species' natural body plan.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. The skeleton is unusually light and fragile, only about **8%** of body weight compared with about 13% in a domestic cat, which helps speed but lowers durability.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The rabbit's weapons are its incisors, claws and hind-leg kicks. Rival bucks fight fiercely in the breeding season, biting and raking with the hind feet, and dominant does fight over the best nest sites, sometimes fatally. A captured rabbit kicks hard with its hind legs. None of this makes it dangerous to a predator: the rabbit is a prey animal whose weapons matter only against other rabbits and very small animals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Rabbits are built for rapid escape. Most of their muscle is in the lumbar region and thighs, the hind legs are long and powerful, and the gait is a hopping (saltatorial) one; a single-gene mutation in one domestic strain destroys this hopping gait, showing how specialized it is. A rabbit flees in short, sharp bursts, changing direction, and rarely strays far from cover: when feeding in fields it typically stays within about 25 m of its burrow.

### Endurance
Rabbits are sprinters, not distance runners. Unlike hares, which outrun predators in the open, rabbits rely on reaching a burrow or thicket quickly.

### Senses
Eyes set high on the sides of the head give a panoramic field of vision of nearly 360°, with much of it devoted to scanning overhead for raptors. Large ears detect approaching predators. Scent is important socially, through chin-gland marking ("chinning") and groin-gland secretions used to recognize group members.

### Intelligence and tactics
Rabbits live in organized social groups with separate dominance hierarchies for bucks and does. They warn each other by thumping the ground with their hind feet and by flashing the white underside of the tail while fleeing, a signal that may also tell a predator the rabbit has seen it. Warrens have emergency exits dug from the inside that drop steeply 1–1.2 m.

### Sociality
Gregarious. A warren holds one to five adult bucks and one to eight adult does, usually around a dominant buck. Groups defend territory against outsiders.

### Fighting and aggression
Most disputes are settled by the hierarchy, so costly fights are relatively rare, but when they happen they are serious. Pet rabbits can bite when hurt or frightened.

### Predator defense
Run and hide. Foxes, wolves, lynxes, wild and domestic cats, mustelids, badgers (which dig out young), eagles, buzzards and owls all take rabbits; in Iberia the Iberian lynx and Spanish imperial eagle depend heavily on them. Relatively few adults are caught in the open because they reach cover so quickly.

### Environmental strengths
- Short grassland with nearby burrows, scrub, hedgerows or woodland edge.
- Open ground where early detection and a short dash to cover decide the encounter.
- Its own warren, where predators struggle to follow.

### Weaknesses
- Fragile, light skeleton and very low absolute durability.
- Weapons that barely matter outside rabbit-on-rabbit fights.
- Poor endurance if cut off from cover.
- Extremely vulnerable to diseases such as myxomatosis and rabbit haemorrhagic disease.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** very small animals; slow predators that cannot close the distance before it reaches cover.
- **Mixed:** similar-sized rodents and small mammals in a direct scuffle.
- **Bad:** foxes, cats, mustelids, raptors, dogs, lynxes and any larger predator that catches it in the open.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Burst-to-Burrow Escape** — long, powerful hind legs and a hopping gait give explosive acceleration and sharp turns over the short distance to cover; predators catch relatively few adults in the open. In a matchup the rabbit wins by not being there.
2. **Hind-Leg Kick** — a captured or cornered rabbit kicks out with hind limbs strong enough that veterinarians warn of serious injury, and bucks rake rivals with the hind feet. It can hurt small attackers but not deter a large one.

### Unique traits
1. **Warren Engineer** — rabbits dig communal warrens with emergency exits dropping 1–1.2 m, and in Iberia their grazing, latrines and burrows shape the whole ecosystem, earning the region the label "the rabbit's ecosystem".
2. **World Traveler** — the only domesticated rabbit species, with breeds from under 1 kg to over 5 kg, it has been introduced to at least 800 islands and every continent except Antarctica; Australia's rabbit plague grew from a single 1859 release.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Native to the Iberian Peninsula and southwestern France, the European rabbit prefers short grassland next to secure refuge such as burrows, boulders, hedgerows, scrub and woodland edges; in large conifer plantations it keeps to the margins and firebreaks. It has been introduced across Europe, Australia, New Zealand, Chile, many islands and parts of Africa, and domestic rabbits live worldwide.

### Diet and ecology
Herbivorous: grasses (especially fescues), leaves and shoots, plus crops such as cereals and sugar beet, bark and berries. Grazing creates a gradient of short vegetation around warrens. In Iberia the rabbit is a keystone species, altering plant communities, fertilizing soil with latrines, providing burrows for other animals and feeding predators including the Iberian lynx and Spanish imperial eagle.

### Social structure
Social groups of one to five bucks and one to eight does share a warren, with parallel dominance hierarchies. Dominant bucks sire most young, and dominant does get the best nest sites. Home ranges are small, about 4 ha.

### Reproduction and life history
Gestation is about 30 days; a doe may produce four or five litters a year of three to seven blind, nearly naked kittens, raised in a separate nest burrow ("stop") lined with belly fur and sealed between once-nightly nursings. Does can breed at three months. Wild mortality is very high, especially in the first year.

### Conservation
The European rabbit is listed as **Near Threatened** in its native range (IUCN assessment cited by Wikipedia, 2025), after steep Iberian declines driven by myxomatosis, rabbit haemorrhagic disease and habitat change. Paradoxically it is a serious invasive pest where introduced, especially in Australia.

### Special features
- Nearly 360° field of vision.
- Long, powerful hind limbs and a hopping gait.
- Light skeleton, about 8% of body mass.
- Two pairs of upper incisors, including small peg teeth.
- Foot-thumping and white tail-flag warning signals.

### Human interaction
Rabbits have been hunted since the Paleolithic and kept in enclosures since Roman times; the Romans hunted them with ferrets. Domestic rabbits are raised for meat, fur and wool and are popular pets. Where introduced, feral rabbits have caused enormous agricultural and ecological damage and have proved hard to control by physical and biological means, as in Australia.

### Genuine fun facts
- A rabbit's skeleton makes up only about 8% of its body weight, compared with about 13% in a cat.
- Rabbits have a second, tiny pair of upper incisors hidden behind the front pair.
- Warrens include emergency exits dug from the inside that drop straight down about a metre.
- Genetic work shows Australia's rabbit invasion began with one 1859 release by Thomas Austin.
- One domestic strain, the sauteur d'Alfort, cannot hop because of a single gene mutation and often walks on its front legs.
- Rabbits flash the white underside of the tail as they flee, warning other rabbits and possibly telling the predator it has been seen.

### Concise site-ready summary
The rabbit is a 2 kg burrowing grazer built to spot danger early and sprint to cover, with nearly 360° vision, explosive hind legs and a social warren life. It kicks and bites hard enough to fight other rabbits, but its light, fragile skeleton and small size make it one of the roster's easy prey animals.

### Rich narrative profile
The European rabbit is a clear answer to a simple question: how does a small grazer survive in a world full of predators? Its answer is vigilance and speed. Eyes set high on the sides of the head give almost all-round vision, the ears catch the first sound of danger, and a thump of the hind feet warns the rest of the warren. When a threat comes close, the rabbit bursts away on long, powerful hind legs, jinking toward a burrow it rarely strays far from.

That escape-first body has a cost. The skeleton is unusually light and fragile, and a struggling rabbit can even break its own back with its kicks. Its weapons, incisors, claws and hind-foot rakes, are used in real fights between bucks and between does over nest sites, but they are no match for a fox, cat, lynx or eagle. On the absolute ABS scale the rabbit therefore scores low in Attack and very low in Defense, while its agility and senses are strong.

Beyond the battlefield, the rabbit is one of the most influential mammals on Earth. In Iberia it is a keystone species that feeds lynxes and eagles and engineers the landscape; elsewhere it has become one of history's most destructive invaders; and as the only domesticated rabbit it has been bred into dozens of breeds from tiny dwarfs to giants.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Length:** Wikipedia 36–38 cm versus ADW 38–50 cm; canonical 40.0 cm head-body.
- **Speed:** widely quoted top speeds were not traced to measurements; no value assigned.
- **Lifespan:** wild rabbits usually die in their first year while pets can reach about 9; canonical 8.0 reflects a pet and is low-confidence.
- **Conservation:** IUCN listed the species as Endangered in its native range in 2019; Wikipedia now cites a 2025 Near Threatened listing. The newer listing is used.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hedgehog (1.0 kg; A11 D43 Ag41 Sp69):** the rabbit is twice as heavy and kicks harder, so Attack sits slightly higher (14.0), but it has no spines, so Defense is far lower (9.0) and Agility far higher (80.0).
- **Versus Fennec Fox (1.5 kg; A15 D10 Ag89 St67 I64 Sp79):** the fox is a predator with a killing bite, so it stays just above in Attack despite being lighter, and well above in Stamina, Intelligence and Special for its desert specializations.
- **Versus Quokka (3.5 kg; A18 D18 Ag73 St56 I49 Sp48):** the heavier hopping marsupial sits above in Attack and Defense; the rabbit is more explosive (Agility 80.0 vs 73.0) and equal in Intelligence terms.
- **Versus Guinea Pig (1.0 kg; A10.5 D10 Ag40):** twice the mass, much stronger hind legs and far better escape movement.
- **Anti-inflation check:** escape speed is scored under Agility and Speed, not Defense; rabbit-on-rabbit fighting is scored under Ferocity (30.0), not Attack.
