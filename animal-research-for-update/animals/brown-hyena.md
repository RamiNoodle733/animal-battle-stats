# Brown Hyena (*Parahyaena brunnea*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Brown hyena (also brown hyaena; called strandwolf in South Africa)
- **Scientific name:** *Parahyaena brunnea* (Thunberg, 1820); formerly *Hyaena brunnea*
- **Exact animal represented:** the living brown hyena of southern Africa's arid west, from the Namib coast and Kalahari to northern South Africa. It is a different species from the spotted hyena already on the roster.
- **Living / extinct:** Living.
- **Status:** Living; Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male of about **43.0 kg**, head-body about **115 cm**, shoulder height about **79 cm**. This is at the top of the usual adult range without using the exceptional 60–72 kg animals on record.
- **Sex choice:** sexual dimorphism is small. Published averages put males at about 40–44 kg and females at about 38–40 kg, and one source quoted by ADW lists 47 kg for males and 42 kg for females. Males are slightly larger, so a mature male is the combat specimen.
- **Population variation:** coastal Namib animals feed heavily on seal carcasses and live in different conditions from Kalahari animals. A few exceptionally heavy individuals (67.6 kg for an Eastern Cape female; 72.6 kg and 59.9 kg for two Lowveld animals of unrecorded sex) are recorded but are not representative.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- IUCN/SSC Hyaena Action Plan (Mills 1982, in Mills & Hofer 1998): adults usually about **40 kg (28–47 kg)**, little sex difference.
- ADW: average **40.7 kg**, range 34.2–72.6 kg; also quotes averages of 47 kg for males and 42 kg for females.
- Wikipedia summary: average adult male 40.2–43.7 kg, female 37.7–40.2 kg.
- **Canonical:** **43.0 kg** mature male.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Height
- IUCN action plan: **0.79 m (0.72–0.88 m)** at the shoulder. Wikipedia gives 70–80 cm.
- **Canonical:** **79.0 cm** shoulder height.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Length
- The IUCN action plan gives **1.4 m (1.26–1.61 m) head to tail**. ADW and Wikipedia repeat 144 cm (130–160 cm) as "body length", which appears to be the same total-length figure. The tail is **25–35 cm**.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 115.0 cm** head-body, taken as the 1.4–1.44 m total length minus a typical 30 cm tail.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate, because sources disagree on whether the published figure includes the tail.

### Speed
- ADW states that brown hyenas forage at a slow walk but can run at **40–50 km/h** when necessary. No instrumented measurement was found.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 11.1 m/s** (40 km/h, the low end of the ADW range), low confidence.

### Lifespan
- ADW lists about 12–13 years as a high value and notes that for animals reaching old age, around 10 years, tooth wear becomes limiting.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 10.0 years**, low confidence; no long-term wild survival study was located.

### Bite and dentition
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No measured bite pressure exists. Comparative bite-force quotient tables (Christiansen & Wroe 2007, summarised on Wikipedia) give the brown hyena a quotient of about 123, close to the spotted hyena's 124, but a size-corrected quotient cannot be converted to PSI.
- **Weapons:** a large skull with robust, massive teeth. Its skull is larger than the striped hyena's and, in a 2026 craniometric study, the brown and spotted hyenas had the largest skulls of the family. Young animals can crack the leg bones of springbok in about five minutes, but this ability declines as the teeth wear. Solitary scavenging hyaenids such as this species have relatively large canines and shorter carnassials than the hunting spotted hyena.

### Defensive structure
- No armor. A long, shaggy dark coat with erectile hairs up to about 30 cm on the neck and back, powerful forequarters and a thick neck. Territorial fights are ritualised neck-biting bouts, which the neck skin and coat withstand.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The brown hyena's main weapon is a heavy bone-cracking bite from a large, robust skull. It is overwhelmingly a scavenger: in the southern Kalahari, vertebrates it killed made up only 4.2% of food items, and of 128 observed hunts only six (4.7%) succeeded, mostly on springhare, springbok lambs and bat-eared foxes. On the Namib coast it feeds mainly on Cape fur seal pups, nearly all found dead. Where it does use its bite on other animals, it is mainly in contests over food.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Long forelegs, strong forequarters and weaker hindquarters give it a sloping back and a steady, tireless walk. ADW reports it can run at 40–50 km/h when needed, but it is not built for quick turns or long sprints. Agility is modest.

### Endurance
Endurance is its strongest physical trait. In the southern Kalahari brown hyenas were active for about 80% of the hours of darkness and covered an average of **31.1 km per night**, with a maximum recorded of **54.4 km** (Mills 1990, in the IUCN action plan).

### Senses
Its sense of smell is well developed: carrion is found mainly by smell, and even old, dry carcasses can be detected from **2 km downwind**. Pasted scent marks are also read by other hyenas to learn who has passed and when.

### Intelligence and tactics
It forages alone but shares a territory, a communal den and food with its clan. Clan members carry food back to the den for cubs, and females may suckle each other's young. It caches surplus food and is an aggressive food thief: it takes kills from black-backed jackals, cheetahs and leopards, and camera-trap work in Botswana found brown hyenas turning up before and after leopards more often than expected by chance, consistent with scavenging from them.

### Sociality and hunting
Clans range from a single female with cubs to several related females with offspring of different ages. Adult males may stay, become nomadic or join a new clan. Clan territories in the southern Kalahari covered 235–480 km², and 220 km² on the Namib coast. The brown hyena has no long-distance call.

### Fighting and aggression
Territorial fights are ritualised neck-biting bouts between two animals of the same sex, with loud yelling by the loser. Against other species it can be bold: single brown hyenas charge leopards with open jaws and have treed adult male leopards, even when no food was at stake, and in the Kalahari they are often the dominant carnivore where lions and spotted hyenas are scarce. Spotted hyenas and lions occasionally kill them.

### Environmental strengths
- Deserts and semi-deserts, including the fog-bound Namib coast.
- Night, when it forages over huge distances.
- Carcass sites, where it can drive off leopards, cheetahs and jackals.

### Weaknesses
- About 43 kg; outclassed by lions and spotted hyena groups.
- A poor, rarely successful hunter; little experience subduing healthy prey.
- No armor; its bite is its only serious weapon.
- Heavy teeth wear down with age, reducing bite effectiveness in old animals.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** jackals, cheetahs, foxes and other medium or small carnivores contesting food; single leopards in carcass disputes.
- **Mixed:** similar-mass canids and wolves, where the brown hyena's bite and toughness offset lower agility.
- **Bad:** lions, spotted hyena clans, large herbivores and armored animals.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Carcass Radar** — carrion is located mainly by smell, and even old, dry carcasses can be detected from 2 km downwind. This lets it find food and rivals' kills across huge desert territories.
2. **Leopard Charge** — a lone brown hyena will rush a leopard with its jaws wide open and can drive an adult male leopard up a tree, then take its kill. Its heavy bite and thick-necked build back up the bluff.

### Unique traits
1. **Strandwolf of the Namib** — on the Namib Desert coast it patrols beaches and seal colonies, living largely on Cape fur seal pups and other washed-up marine animals, and even uses abandoned diamond mining towns.
2. **Two-Tone Scent Paste** — each time it pastes a grass stalk it leaves a thin black smear and a thick white blob whose smell lasts over 30 days; marks are so dense that a hyena is rarely more than 500 m from one.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Southern Africa's arid west: Namibia, Botswana, western and southern Zimbabwe, southern Mozambique and South Africa. It lives in desert with under 100 mm of rain, especially along the coast, and in semi-desert, open scrub and open woodland savanna up to about 650 mm of rain. It is independent of drinking water but needs cover to lie up in by day, and it can survive close to farms and towns.

### Diet and ecology
A scavenger of a wide range of vertebrate remains, supplemented by wild fruits such as tsama melons, insects, birds' eggs and the occasional small animal it kills. In farming areas of South Africa it scavenges cattle carcasses and medium and small wild animals. It caches food and steals kills from jackals, cheetahs and leopards.

### Social structure
Lives in clans that share and defend a territory but usually forage alone. Clans have separate male and female dominance hierarchies. Many males are nomads that roam between clan territories, and both nomadic and immigrant males may mate with clan females.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding is non-seasonal. Gestation is about 97 days and litters average 2.3 cubs (range 1–5), each weighing about 1 kg. Dens are narrow holes, often in remote sand dunes away from lions and spotted hyenas. The denning period lasts about 15 months, cubs are moved between several dens, and all clan members bring food to the den.

### Conservation
Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List, with a global population estimated at 4,000–10,000. Main threats are persecution by livestock farmers, poisoning, road deaths and disease from domestic dogs; 43% of Namib Desert brown hyenas sampled had antibodies to canine distemper virus. Density estimates in fenced South African reserves varied almost tenfold depending on whether fences were assumed to be permeable, showing how uncertain population numbers are.

### Special features
- Large skull with robust, bone-cracking teeth.
- Long, shaggy coat with erectile neck and back hair up to about 30 cm.
- Pale mantle around the neck and shoulders.
- Two-part anal-gland scent paste.
- Very long nightly foraging range.

### Human interaction
Farmers often blame brown hyenas for livestock losses, but most damage comes from a few individuals, and removing a single culprit has stopped stock killing even with other hyenas present. Finding a brown hyena at a carcass does not prove it made the kill. It lives near people in some agricultural areas and near towns.

### Genuine fun facts
- It can smell an old, dried-out carcass from 2 km downwind.
- One Kalahari brown hyena walked 54.4 km in a single night.
- Only about 1 in 20 of its hunts succeed, so it relies on scavenging.
- A single brown hyena can chase an adult male leopard up a tree.
- On the Namib coast it lives on seal pups and has been seen in abandoned diamond mining towns.
- Its white scent paste keeps its smell for more than a month.
- Females in a clan may suckle each other's cubs.

### Concise site-ready summary
The brown hyena is a shaggy 43 kg desert scavenger of southern Africa that can smell carrion from 2 km away and walk more than 50 km in a night. Its heavy bone-cracking bite and bold temper let it drive leopards off their kills, but it rarely hunts successfully and cannot match lions or spotted hyena clans.

### Rich narrative profile
The brown hyena looks like a large, long-haired dog with a sloping back: dark brown to black, with a cream mantle around the neck and shoulders and a coat that bristles up to 30 cm when it is excited. A mature male weighs around 43 kg. It is the hyena of the Kalahari and the Namib, where rain can be less than 100 mm a year and food is thinly spread.

Its answer to scarcity is distance and a nose. Brown hyenas spend most of the night on the move, averaging about 31 km, and can find even dried carcasses by scent from 2 km away. On the Namib coast they patrol beaches for dead seal pups and washed-up marine life. They hunt only occasionally and rarely succeed, taking springhares, lambs and bat-eared foxes, so most of what they eat comes from carcasses and from kills they steal.

Stealing is where its fighting ability shows. A lone brown hyena will charge a leopard with its jaws open and can force an adult male up a tree, and it routinely displaces cheetahs and jackals. Its skull is among the largest in the hyena family and its bite-force quotient is close to the spotted hyena's. On the absolute roster scale it sits slightly above the striped hyena, just below the gray wolf in Attack and well below the spotted hyena, with very high Stamina from its long desert walks.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Length:** the 1.4–1.44 m figure is described as head-to-tail by the IUCN action plan but as body length by ADW and Wikipedia. The canonical 115 cm head-body subtracts a typical tail and is low-moderate confidence.
- **Mass:** a few animals of 60–72 kg are on record, but typical adults weigh about 40 kg; the canonical 43 kg is a large but normal male.
- **Seal diet:** the IUCN action plan says only 2.9% of the Cape fur seal pups eaten on the Namib coast were killed by the hyenas; one secondary summary garbles this into a diet percentage.
- **Speed:** the 40–50 km/h figure has no stated method; the low end is used with low confidence.
- **Bite:** the bite-force quotient shows relative strength only; no PSI value is adopted.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Hyena (spotted, 75 kg female; A61 D53 Ag61 St83 I78 Sp72):** much lighter and a poor hunter without clan hunting, so it stays below the spotted hyena in every headline rating despite a similar bite-force quotient.
- **Versus Striped Hyena (38 kg; A50 D42 Ag50 St72):** heavier, with a larger skull and more robust teeth and much longer measured nightly travel, so it sits a few points higher in Attack and Defense and clearly higher in Stamina.
- **Versus Gray Wolf (50 kg; A55 D41 St89):** Attack two points lower because the wolf is heavier and an efficient killer of large prey; Defense four points higher for the brown hyena's thick coat, neck and robust build. The wolf keeps higher Agility, Stamina and Intelligence.
- **Versus Jackal (8.1 kg; A34 D27 Ag74):** the brown hyena routinely steals from jackals and is about five times heavier; it is far above the jackal in Attack and Defense and far below it in Agility.
- **Versus Leopard (A60):** a lone brown hyena can tree a leopard, but that is bluff and willingness at a carcass, not superior killing power; the leopard keeps the higher Attack.
- **Anti-inflation check:** long walks and a strong nose raise Stamina and Senses, not Attack.
