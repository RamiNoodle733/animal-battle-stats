# Wild Turkey (*Meleagris gallopavo*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Wild Turkey (adult male: tom or gobbler; adult female: hen)
- **Scientific name:** *Meleagris gallopavo* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the wild North American turkey *Meleagris gallopavo*, represented by the eastern wild turkey (*M. g. silvestris*) of hardwood forests. The domestic turkey was bred from wild stock and is not the canonical animal here.
- **Status:** Living; Least Concern.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult male (tom), **7.6 kg**, **110.0 cm** total length. Toms are much heavier than hens and carry leg spurs used in fights.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** very strong. Adult males normally weigh 5–11 kg and measure 100–125 cm; adult females weigh 2.5–5.4 kg and measure 76–95 cm. Two large studies give average weights of 7.6 kg for males and 4.26 kg for females. Males have spurs, a bare red-and-blue head with a snood and wattles, and a breast "beard"; hens usually have neither spurs nor beards.
- **Population notes:** the National Wild Turkey Federation record male weighed 17.06 kg, and toms over 13.8 kg are uncommon but not rare. Pennsylvania gobblers weigh up to about 11 kg and average about 7.3 kg (16 lb). Juvenile males (jakes) have short beards and uneven tail fans.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The tom's weapons are its legs and body. Each leg carries a sharp spur used to spar with other males. When fighting off predators, turkeys kick with their legs and spurs, bite with the bill and ram with their relatively large bodies. Wikipedia's account says this can deter predators up to the size of mid-sized mammals. Hens have been seen chasing off at least two species of hawks in flight when their poults were threatened. Turkeys are not predators of large animals; they eat mostly plants, including acorns, seeds, berries and roots, plus insects and occasionally small salamanders, lizards and snakes.

### Offensive mechanics
Most turkey fighting is between males over dominance. Males display by strutting, gobbling, drumming and "spitting" to signal status, and fights use kicks and spurs. Toms sometimes attack parked cars and other reflective surfaces, apparently treating their reflection as a rival. Against predators, adults usually run first and fight only if cornered.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Despite their weight, wild turkeys are agile, fast fliers, unlike domestic turkeys. They burst off the ground on loud wings and usually fly low for no more than about 400 m, and they also run fast, with an estimated top speed of about 8 m/s. They can fly beneath the canopy to perches and swim when needed. Agility is scored for explosive escape and quick running, not sustained maneuvering.

### Endurance
Turkeys walk and forage over large home ranges, and GPS studies across the southeastern US track males moving between winter ranges and breeding-season display areas. Flights are short bursts. Stamina is scored moderate.

### Senses
Wild turkeys have very good daytime vision and good hearing; PGC describes keen eyesight and hearing. Their night vision is very poor, which is why they roost in trees at dusk.

### Intelligence and tactics
Turkeys are wary and hide cleverly (PGC). Flocks share vigilance, and foraging turkeys have been described alongside deer and squirrels that add other senses. Male coalitions court together: subordinate helper males, often brothers, display with a dominant male and do not breed themselves, and dominant males courting in pairs fathered more eggs than lone males (*Nature*, 2005). Tactics are moderate for a bird.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Males compete fiercely for dominance in spring, with gobbling, strutting and spur fights. Most hens prefer dominant males. Toms are not usually aggressive toward people, but they can attack when startled, cornered or approached too closely.

### Environmental strengths
- Mature hardwood forest with oak and hickory, mixed with clearings and fields.
- Open woodland where it can run and take off fast.
- Group settings where many eyes watch for predators.

### Weaknesses
- About 7.6 kg, with no armor.
- Short bursts of flight only; heavy wing loading.
- Very poor night vision; vulnerable on the ground in darkness.
- Spurs and kicks are close-range weapons with little killing power against large animals.
- Prefers flight to fight, so it rarely presses an attack against a dangerous opponent.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** smaller birds, small raptors, snakes and lizards, and small mammals it can kick and chase off.
- **Mixed:** foxes, raccoons and similar mid-sized predators; other large gamebirds and geese.
- **Bad:** coyotes, bobcats and larger carnivores, large raptors against hens and poults, and anything that attacks at night.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Spur Kick Brawl** — toms fight with sharp leg spurs, powerful kicks, bill strikes and body ramming. The same toolkit can deter mid-sized predators when a turkey is cornered.
2. **Rocket Take-Off** — a turkey bursts off the ground on loud wings and can reach recorded flight speeds of about 88.5 km/h over short distances, or sprint away at about 8 m/s.

### Unique traits
1. **Heavyweight Flier** — at 7.6 kg on average and sometimes over 13.8 kg, the wild turkey is among the heaviest flying birds in the world, yet it still flies fast and roosts in trees.
2. **Brother Display Teams** — subordinate males, often brothers, court alongside a dominant male without breeding themselves, and paired dominant males father more young than males courting alone.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Wild turkeys are native to North America, from southern Canada through much of the US to northern and central Mexico. They prefer mature hardwood forest with oak, hickory, beech, cherry and ash, mixed with clearings, fields and pasture, but they also live in montane conifer and oak woods and near farmland and suburbs. Reintroductions have restored them to most of their former range and beyond, including parts of Canada.

### Diet and ecology
They are omnivores that feed mostly on plants: acorns and other nuts, seeds, grains, berries, buds, leaves, grass blades, roots and bulbs. They also eat insects and occasionally small salamanders, lizards and snakes. Their broad diet lets populations grow large in small areas. They are prey for many predators; poults are most vulnerable in their first two weeks.

### Social structure
Turkeys live in flocks outside the breeding season, and roosting flocks of 6–40 birds may share trees. Flocks have dominance hierarchies. In spring, males display in groups and form coalitions, and most hens prefer to mate with dominant males. GPS data from 151 males and 261 females in the southeastern US showed males revisiting small display areas (about 50 ha on average) within their breeding-season ranges.

### Reproduction and life history
Males are polygamous. After mating, hens nest on the ground and lay clutches of 10–14 eggs, one per day. Poults leave the nest soon after hatching and hide under the hen when threatened. Mortality is high: ADW gives an average life expectancy of only 1.3–1.6 years, although some wild turkeys have lived at least 13 years.

### Conservation
The species is listed as **Least Concern** and its population trend has been increasing (National Geographic). Habitat loss and market hunting drove numbers in the US as low as an estimated 30,000 by the late 1930s, and turkeys disappeared from 18 states. Protection and trap-and-transfer reintroductions restored them; current estimates put the wild population at about 7 million. Some eastern populations have recently declined, with invasive wild pigs and disease among the factors being studied.

### Special features
- Sharp leg spurs on males.
- Bare, colorful head with snood and wattles that change color with excitement.
- Breast "beard" of hair-like feathers that grows through life.
- Fast burst flight despite its weight.
- Very good daytime vision.

### Human interaction
Wild turkeys are a major game bird whose recovery is a notable conservation success. Domestic turkeys were bred from wild stock by indigenous peoples of southern Mexico and Central America about 2,000 years ago. Wild toms occasionally attack people, cars or their own reflections, usually when approached too closely or during the breeding season.

### Genuine fun facts
- The record wild tom weighed about 17 kg.
- Wild turkeys have been recorded flying at 88.5 km/h.
- A tom's beard keeps growing throughout its life and can reach about 30 cm.
- Toms sometimes attack their own reflection in car paint or windows.
- Subordinate males, often brothers, help a dominant male court without breeding themselves.
- US wild turkey numbers fell to an estimated 30,000 in the 1930s and have recovered to around 7 million.
- Chased gobblers have been measured taking strides of about 1.2 m.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Wild Turkey is North America's heavyweight flying gamebird. The canonical tom weighs about 7.6 kg and fights with sharp leg spurs, kicks and body slams. It can burst into flight at up to about 88 km/h or sprint away on foot. It is wary and sharp-eyed by day, but it is not a predator, and against real hunters it relies on escape more than combat.

### Rich narrative profile
The wild turkey is not the slow farm bird most people picture. A healthy tom weighs around 7.6 kg, runs at about 8 m/s, and can explode into the air on loud wings, reaching recorded speeds of about 88 km/h before gliding into cover or up to a roost. Keen daytime eyes and a flock full of watchers make it hard to surprise in daylight.

Its fighting is mostly among its own kind. In spring, toms strut, gobble and drum to show status and settle disputes with kicks and sharp leg spurs. Some males work in teams: subordinate brothers display beside a dominant male and help him win mates without breeding themselves. When cornered by a predator, a turkey will kick, spur, peck and ram, which is enough to drive off some mid-sized mammals, and hens defending poults have chased off hawks.

In an absolute matchup, however, it is a plant-eating bird that prefers to run or fly. Its spurs are short-range weapons without real killing power against large animals, it has no armor, and its night vision is very poor. It does well against small animals it can bully and against opponents it can outrun, but true predators usually decide the fight.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** records of 13.8–17 kg are real but exceptional; the canonical 7.6 kg is the male average.
- **Speed:** the 88.5 km/h flight figure (ADW) and PGC's 40–55 mph and 18 mph running figures are estimates without published methods. Speed is scored at moderate confidence and not used as a stand-in for Agility.
- **Lifespan:** ADW's 1.3–1.6 years includes heavy poult mortality; National Geographic's 3–4 years is used as the typical adult-inclusive value.
- **Spurs:** spur length is widely used by hunters to age toms, but no authoritative measurement was retrieved in this run.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Peacock (5.0 kg; A29.0 D19.0 Ag70.0 St58.0 I64.0 Sp62.0):** another spurred galliform, but the turkey is about 50% heavier, so it sits above on Attack, Defense, Size and Raw Power. The peacock's display plumage and higher-rated cognition keep it above on Special and Intelligence.
- **Versus Goose (3.5 kg; A25.0 Ag76.0 St82.0 I67.0):** the goose is famously aggressive but less than half the turkey's mass, so the turkey is higher on Attack and Defense. The goose's long migratory flights keep it far above on Stamina.
- **Versus Cassowary (65 kg; A57.0):** the cassowary's dagger-like claw and far greater mass put it in a different class; the turkey's spurs are much smaller weapons.
- **Versus Coyote (15 kg; A38.0):** a medium carnivore of turkey country; it stays well above on Attack, and the turkey relies on escape.
- **Anti-inflation check:** record 17 kg toms and burst flight speed do not raise Attack; speed is kept separate from Agility.
