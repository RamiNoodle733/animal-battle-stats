# Wels Catfish (*Silurus glanis*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Wels catfish (European catfish, sheatfish)
- **Scientific name:** *Silurus glanis* Linnaeus, 1758
- **Exact animal represented:** the wels catfish of central and eastern Europe and western Asia, including the large introduced populations in western Europe (Spain, France, Italy and elsewhere). Not the Amur catfish or other *Silurus* species.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy large adult, **22 kg** and **160 cm** long, sex unspecified, representing the upper end of the common adult size rather than the rare 2 m-plus giants.
- **Sex choice:** no reliable size difference between the sexes was found in this run, so no sex is assumed.
- **Population notes:** most adults are about 1.3–1.6 m long and fish over 2 m are rare. A 1.5 m fish typically weighs 15–20 kg and a 2.2 m fish about 65 kg (Wikipedia, tertiary). FishBase accepts maxima of **2.73 m and 130 kg** with evidence, and notes that the long-quoted 5 m and 306 kg figures came from misidentification and unit errors. Introduced populations in warm western European rivers produce many large fish.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The wels catfish's weapon is its huge mouth. It attacks by sudden suction, engulfing prey and gripping it with pads of hundreds of small, inward-sloping teeth, then crushing it at the back of the mouth. The teeth grip rather than cut, so the wels swallows prey whole instead of slicing pieces off. It is an opportunist that eats fish, crayfish, amphibians, birds and small mammals.

Two studies show how effective this can be. In the Tarn River in southern France, Cucherousset et al. (2012) filmed wels catfish deliberately beaching themselves on gravel banks to grab pigeons at the water's edge, a behavior they compared to killer whales stranding to catch seals. About 28% of the filmed attempts succeeded, and only moving pigeons were attacked. In the River Garonne, Boulêtreau et al. (2018) found that wels caught **35% (14 of 39)** of the adult Atlantic salmon passing through one fishway during the 2016 migration, with a few specialized individuals timing their presence to the salmon run.

The wels will also bite people. In June 2025 a wels about 2 m long and about 90 kg injured at least five swimmers at the Brombachsee lake in Bavaria before it was killed; another wels bit a swimmer there in July 2025. Fishing experts suggested the fish were near spawning grounds.

### Defense and durability
The skin is scaleless and soft, and there is no armor. Defense comes from moderate mass, a hardy physiology and a habit of resting in deep water and cover during the day.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The wels is a lunging ambush predator. It is powerful over short distances and can throw itself onto a bank and wriggle back, but it is not a fast open-water pursuer. No speed measurement was found.

### Endurance
Telemetry studies show that wels can move more than 10 km, especially in summer, and are active through the night. They are active even in cold water (under 5 °C) in late winter. This supports moderate sustained activity rather than elite endurance.

### Senses
The senses are the wels catfish's strongest feature. It has long barbels and taste buds on the barbels and skin, a lateral line, and **electroreception**: its ampullary electroreceptors are tonic, sensitive to currents of about 10⁻¹⁰ A/mm² with an upper frequency limit of about 25 Hz, and are used to detect other fish, including prey and predators. Its hearing has also been studied experimentally. In the pigeon study, the catfish raised their barbels while hunting and only attacked moving birds, suggesting vibration cues.

### Intelligence and tactics
The pigeon-beaching and fishway-ambush behaviors are strong evidence of learned, flexible hunting: individual fish exploited new prey in new ways and timed attacks to prey availability. This is unusual for a fish.

### Hunting behavior
Mostly nocturnal and crepuscular. Wels rise from deep water to the surface layer at dusk, hunt actively near the surface through the night and descend again at dawn.

### Intraspecific fighting and predator defense
Large wels have few predators. No detailed fighting between wels was documented in this research; aggression toward swimmers has been linked to spawning areas.

### Aggression
Generally wary of people, but large fish near spawning areas have repeatedly bitten swimmers.

### Environmental strengths
- Murky, dark or night-time water, where its electrical, chemical and touch senses outperform vision.
- Riverbanks and shallows where it can ambush prey at the edge.
- Warm, slow rivers and reservoirs.

### Weaknesses
- No armor; scaleless skin.
- Teeth grip but cannot cut large prey.
- Slow in open pursuit.
- Stranding on land is short and risky.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small or medium animals at the water's edge; fish and birds that can be swallowed whole; opponents blind in murky water.
- **Mixed:** similarly sized predatory fish.
- **Bad:** crocodilians, large sharks, armored turtles and anything too large to swallow.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Riverbank Lunge** — wels in France have been filmed beaching themselves to grab pigeons at the water's edge and sliding back into the river, about 28% of attempts succeeding. In a matchup it can strike opponents that think they are safe in the shallows or on the bank.
2. **Electric Barbel Radar** — ampullary electroreceptors detect the weak electric fields of other fish, while long barbels and skin covered in taste buds track chemical and touch cues. It can find and follow an opponent in total darkness or thick mud.

### Unique traits
1. **Freshwater Killer Whale** — the pigeon-beaching behavior earned wels the nickname "freshwater killer whales" in the 2012 study that documented it.
2. **Salmon Ambusher** — a few wels in the River Garonne learned to wait in a fishway and caught about 35% of the adult salmon passing through in 2016.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The wels catfish lives in large rivers, lakes and reservoirs of central and eastern Europe and western Asia, preferring warm, slow or still water with deep holes and cover. It has been introduced widely in western Europe, including the Ebro and Guadalquivir in Spain (the Ebro from 1974) and the Rhône, Garonne and Tarn in France, where it is now a major predator.

### Diet and ecology
Juveniles eat invertebrates, small fish and detritus; adults eat fish, crayfish, amphibians, birds, small mammals and other available prey. Wels exploit seasonal food pulses such as migrating shad and salmon. In invaded rivers they are an apex predator and a conservation concern for native fish.

### Social structure
Solitary for most of the year. Adults spend the day in deep water or cover and become active at dusk.

### Reproduction and life history
Wels spawn in spring and early summer in shallow, vegetated areas, where adults can become defensive. They grow quickly in warm water, which helps explain the size of fish in introduced southern European populations.

### Conservation
The wels catfish is listed as **Least Concern** on the IUCN Red List, although some native populations have declined through overfishing and habitat change. Outside its native range it is an invasive species of concern, threatening salmon, shad and other native fish.

### Special features
- Very wide mouth with pads of hundreds of small gripping teeth.
- Long barbels and taste buds over the barbels and skin.
- Electroreceptive ampullary organs.
- Scaleless, slimy skin.
- Ability to beach briefly to seize prey on land.

### Human interaction
The wels is a major sport fish and is farmed for food. Its spread by anglers into western European rivers has created ecological problems. Bites on swimmers are rare but real, as the 2025 Brombachsee incidents in Germany showed.

### Genuine fun facts
- Wels catfish in France beach themselves to catch pigeons, succeeding in about 28% of filmed attempts.
- Scientists nicknamed them "freshwater killer whales".
- At one River Garonne fishway, wels caught 35% of the adult salmon passing through.
- Their skin is covered in taste buds, so they can taste with their whole body.
- They can sense the weak electric fields of other fish.
- FishBase accepts a maximum of 2.73 m and 130 kg; older claims of 5 m were errors.
- In 2025 a 2 m wels bit at least five swimmers in a Bavarian lake.

### Concise site-ready summary
The wels catfish is Europe's giant river ambusher: a 1.6-metre, 22 kg night hunter that finds prey with electroreception and taste-covered skin, then engulfs it with a huge suction gulp. It even beaches itself to snatch pigeons, but its gripping teeth and scaleless skin leave it outmatched by armored or much larger opponents.

### Rich narrative profile
The wels catfish hunts by feel, taste and electricity. It spends the day in deep holes and rises at dusk, sweeping the water with long barbels. Its skin is covered in taste buds, its lateral line senses movement, and electroreceptive organs pick up the faint electric fields of other fish. In the dark, murky water where it lives, that sensory package lets it find prey that cannot see it coming.

Its attack is a gulp. The mouth is enormous, and when it opens suddenly, water and prey rush in to meet pads of hundreds of small, backward-pointing teeth. Wels have learned to use that attack in surprising ways. On the Tarn River in France, researchers filmed catfish sliding up onto gravel banks to grab pigeons before wriggling back into the water. On the Garonne, a few wels learned to wait in a fishway and caught about a third of the adult salmon passing through.

In a battle, the wels is dangerous at the water's edge and in the dark, and almost anything small enough to swallow is at risk. Its limits are just as clear: it has no scales or armor, its teeth grip rather than cut, and it is slow in open water. Against a crocodile, a large shark or a heavily armored turtle, the wels would lose.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 22 kg is interpolated from tertiary typical values; there are many much larger fish in introduced populations.
- **Maximum size:** old 5 m and 306 kg claims are rejected in favour of FishBase's verified 2.73 m and 130 kg.
- **Lifespan:** the 80-year figure is unverified; 25.0 is a low-confidence typical value.
- **Speed and bite:** no measurements; both fields are 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Bull Shark (111 kg; A72 D52 Ag73 St69 I58 Sp82):** the bull shark is five times heavier with a forceful serrated bite and is far above in Attack, Defense and Agility. Both are river-going predators with unusual abilities; the wels's electroreception and land-edge strikes put Special at 74.0, below the bull shark's 82.0 freshwater-tolerance score. Intelligence is similar (57.0 vs 58.0) because the wels's learned beaching and fishway ambushes match the bull shark's flexible predation.
- **Versus Piranha (1.2 kg; A37 D20 Ag82 St55 I59 Sp67):** the piranha has a much better cutting bite but is eighteen times lighter. The wels sits a little higher in Attack (42.0 vs 37.0) and Defense (30.0 vs 20.0), much lower in Agility, and higher in Special for its electroreception.
- **Versus Goliath Tigerfish (25 kg; A52 D30 Ag72):** similar mass; the tigerfish's dagger teeth and fast strikes give it higher Attack and Agility, while the wels leads in senses, Special and Intelligence.
- **Versus Electric Eel (15 kg; Sp98):** both detect electric fields, but only the electric eel produces a powerful discharge, so the eel keeps the Special ceiling.
- **Versus Alligator (200 kg; A84 D77):** a clear bad matchup for the wels, reflected in far lower Attack and Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** headlines about "monster" catfish were not allowed to set the canonical body size; the 2 m, 90 kg Brombachsee fish is above the representative adult.
