# Warthog (*Phacochoerus africanus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Warthog (common warthog)
- **Scientific name:** *Phacochoerus africanus* (Gmelin, 1788)
- **Exact animal represented:** the common warthog of sub-Saharan African savannas and open woodlands, not the smaller, drier-country desert warthog (*P. aethiopicus*) of the Horn of Africa. The site's live photo is a Nolan warthog, *P. a. africanus*.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult boar of about **85 kg**, **75 cm** at the shoulder and **130 cm** head-body length, with upper tusks of about **25 cm**.
- **Sex choice:** boars are heavier (60–150 kg vs 45–75 kg for sows), carry much larger facial warts and tusks, and do the head-to-head fighting.
- **Population variation:** body mass varies by region and season; Kruger boars are usually 60–100 kg, while the species range reaches 150 kg. Tusk records of over 60 cm are exceptional trophies, not typical animals.

- Sparse body hair and a long mane; no armor.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The warthog has two tusk pairs. The long, curved upper tusks are blunt and used for pushing and striking with the head in fights between boars. The lower tusks are shorter but honed to a sharp edge against the uppers, and are used to slash predators. San Diego Zoo describes warthogs as "not afraid to fight", slashing with the lower tusks while squealing loudly. A warthog cornered in or near its burrow bursts out head first with its tusks forward. It never attacks animals as prey: it is a grazer and rooter, and all its fighting is defensive or between rivals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Warthogs have longer legs than other pigs and run with their tails held upright. Institutional top-speed claims range from 40 to 55 km/h, and San Diego Zoo notes that they are good at dodging. When leaving a burrow they dash out at top speed in case a predator is waiting. They enter burrows backwards so they can face the entrance. They feed kneeling on calloused wrist pads, which slows their reaction when grazing.

### Endurance
Warthogs are sprinters rather than long-distance runners. Home ranges are small, about **0.62–3.3 km²** (ADW), and animals are active by day and shelter in burrows at night. Burrows protect them from heat at midday and cold at night, and they wallow at waterholes. There is no evidence of long pursuit or long-distance travel.

### Senses
Warthogs have poor eyesight but keen hearing and smell (ADW). They use their large snouts to find bulbs, roots and tubers. In a playback experiment in a South African savanna, warthogs and antelopes were more likely to run from recorded leopard, hyena and wild dog calls in thick shrub than in clearings, showing they judge risk by cover.

### Intelligence and tactics
The burrow is the warthog's main tactic: it uses aardvark holes and other burrows for sleep, birth and escape, backs in so its tusks face out, and bursts out when threatened. It avoids night predators by being active in daylight. Warthogs allow banded mongooses and vervet monkeys to groom ticks off them, and ADW notes that they associate with oxpeckers, which help them avoid predators. These are sound survival habits, but not complex problem solving.

### Sociality and hunting
Sows live in sounders with their young and other females; adult boars live apart and join sounders only when sows are in heat. Warthogs graze short grass and dig for bulbs, rhizomes and roots with their snouts and tusks in the dry season.

### Fighting and aggression
Boars fight in the mating season by pushing and striking with their heads and blunt upper tusks. The dangerous lower tusks are rarely used on each other, and the facial warts cushion blows, so injuries are rare; the loser squeaks and retreats. Against predators the warthog's first choice is to run, but a cornered warthog fights hard.

### Predator defense
Lions, leopards, cheetahs, spotted hyenas, African wild dogs, crocodiles and humans kill adults; eagles and jackals take young. Lions prefer prey of 190–550 kg but take warthogs in proportion to their availability, and in Kruger lion diets carcass-only methods under-count warthog and impala meals by more than half. Leopards prefer prey of 10–40 kg that pose minimal risk of injury, so a full-grown 85 kg boar is above their preferred size, though leopards do take warthogs.

### Environmental strengths
- Open savanna and grassland near burrows, where a short sprint to cover works.
- Its own burrow, which it defends tusks-first.
- Hot, dry conditions, which it handles by sheltering and wallowing.

### Weaknesses
- Poor eyesight.
- Short-range sprinter with little stamina for long chases.
- No armor; sparse hair on the body.
- Vulnerable when kneeling to graze.
- Mid-sized prey for all of Africa's large carnivores.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small and medium carnivores, animals that must dig it out of a burrow, rivals of similar size.
- **Mixed:** single leopards, cheetahs and hyenas; similar-sized antelopes with horns.
- **Bad:** lions, hyena clans, wild dog packs, crocodiles at waterholes, and any much larger animal.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Burrow Ambush Exit** — reverses into aardvark burrows with its head toward the opening, then bursts out at full speed with tusks forward if a predator waits outside.
2. **Lower-Tusk Slash** — the short lower tusks are honed to a cutting edge against the uppers and inflict severe wounds on predators while the long uppers push and strike.

### Unique traits
1. **Cushion Warts** — thick cartilage "warts" on the face, the suborbital pair up to 15 cm long in boars, pad the blows of head-to-head fights so injuries are rare.
2. **Kneeling Grazer** — calloused, padded wrists let it graze short grass on its knees and even shuffle forward in that position.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Sub-Saharan Africa, from West Africa through East Africa to southern Africa, in savanna, open woodland and grassland, often near waterholes and marshy ground. It is widespread in parks such as Kruger in South Africa, where its range tracks open woodland savanna. A genome study of 35 common warthogs from throughout the range found that the species interbred with the desert warthog in eastern Africa.

### Diet and ecology
Mainly a grazer of short grasses, switching in the dry season to digging up bulbs, tubers, rhizomes and roots with its snout and tusks. Warthogs wallow in mud at waterholes and rely on burrows dug by aardvarks and others. The soft ticks in their burrows and the warthogs themselves maintain African swine fever virus in a sylvatic cycle in eastern and southern Africa.

### Social structure
Sounders of sows and their young, sometimes several females together; adult boars live apart and join sounders only to mate. Sows are much more social than boars. Home ranges are about 0.6–3.3 km².

### Reproduction and life history
Sows give birth in burrows and stay underground for several weeks nursing the piglets. Young may stay with the mother for up to two years. Boars wait near burrows for sows to emerge in the mating season, and males rarely mate before about four years old. Wild warthogs live about 7–11 years on average.

### Conservation
Least Concern on the IUCN Red List. Warthogs are hunted for meat and trophies and killed for raiding crops. In Gonarezhou National Park, Zimbabwe, warthog and other herbivores under 140 kg declined after 2013, probably because of predation.

### Special features
- Upper tusks up to about 25.5 cm, curving 90 degrees or more.
- Lower tusks honed to a sharp edge.
- Three pairs of cartilage facial warts, the largest up to 15 cm.
- Calloused wrist pads for kneeling.
- Runs with its tail held straight up.

### Human interaction
Warthogs are among the best-known animals of African safari parks. They are hunted for meat, which is lean (under 2% fat) and about 32% protein, and for tusk trophies; tusks are carved for the tourist trade. They are also killed as crop raiders, and they matter to veterinarians as carriers of African swine fever virus.

### Genuine fun facts
- Warthogs back into their burrows so their tusks face any intruder.
- Their "warts" are not warts at all but cartilage pads that cushion blows.
- Warthogs often kneel to graze, on calloused wrist pads.
- They run with their tails straight up like a flag.
- Banded mongooses and vervet monkeys groom ticks off warthogs.
- Warthogs rarely dig their own homes, preferring old aardvark burrows.
- The dangerous lower tusks are rarely used in fights between boars.

### Concise site-ready summary
The common warthog is an 85 kg African pig with long curved tusks, razor-edged lower tusks and padded facial warts. It is a fast sprinter that dives into burrows backwards and bursts out tusks-first, dangerous when cornered but still regular prey for lions, leopards and hyenas.

### Rich narrative profile
A warthog boar looks built for trouble: a broad, flat head with two long upper tusks curving out of the snout, a pair of shorter lower tusks sharpened like knives, big cartilage warts on its face, and a bristly mane down its back. At about 85 kg and 75 cm at the shoulder, it is longer-legged than most pigs and can outrun many threats over a short distance, tail held high.

Its survival strategy is built around the burrow. Warthogs sleep, give birth and hide in abandoned aardvark holes, always reversing in so their tusks face the entrance, and they leave at a run in case a predator is waiting. In the open they graze on their knees, dig for roots in the dry season and keep an ear and nose out for danger, since their eyesight is poor. Boars fight each other with head blows and the blunt upper tusks, cushioned by their warts, saving the razor-edged lower tusks for predators.

On the ABS scale the warthog is a mid-weight scrapper. It is better armed than most antelope of similar size, and a cornered warthog fights back with its sharp lower tusks, but it remains a staple prey species for lions, hyenas and wild dogs. Its Attack and Defense sit well below the giant forest hog and the big predators, while its Agility and burrow tactics give it a real chance to escape.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Speed:** 40, 48 and 55 km/h claims without methods; 13.3 m/s is a midpoint with low confidence.
- **Tusk length:** typical upper tusks are about 25 cm; ADW's 635 mm upper figure is a trophy maximum.
- **Lifespan:** 7–11 years average (Wikipedia, ADW) vs 15–18 years (AWF), which looks like a maximum.
- **Mass:** 60–100 kg (Kruger) vs 60–150 kg (Wikipedia); 85 kg sits inside both.
- **Sex of photo animal:** not verified; the canonical specimen is a boar regardless.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Giant Forest Hog (210 kg; A56 D50 Ag50 St60 I48 Sp54):** the forest hog is two and a half times heavier, rams skull-first and is more aggressive, so it stays above the warthog in Attack, Defense, Size and Raw Power. The warthog keeps longer tusks for its size, higher Agility (60.0 vs 50.0) as a savanna sprinter, and a slightly higher Special for its burrow defense.
- **Versus Hyena (75 kg; A61 D53 Ag61 St83 I78 Sp72):** the spotted hyena is a similar-mass predator that kills warthogs; it stays far above in Attack, Stamina and Intelligence. The two are close in Agility.
- **Versus Leopard (60 kg; A60 D43 Ag84 St62 I68 Sp63):** leopards kill warthogs but prefer 10–40 kg prey that is low-risk, and an 85 kg boar is above that. The leopard stays far higher in Attack and Agility; the heavier, padded warthog is level in Defense (44.0 vs 43.0).
- **Versus Impala (65 kg; A43) and Ibex (100 kg; A51):** the warthog's slashing tusks and willingness to fight put its Attack (47.0) between these similar-sized horned herbivores.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the legacy Toughness 93.5, Ferocity 86 and Defense 71.9 are rejected; an 85 kg unarmored pig that is routine prey for lions and hyenas is not near the top of the roster in toughness.
