# Philippine Eagle (*Pithecophaga jefferyi*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Philippine Eagle (also called the monkey-eating eagle or great Philippine eagle)
- **Scientific name:** *Pithecophaga jefferyi* Ogilvie-Grant, 1896
- **Exact animal represented:** the living species *Pithecophaga jefferyi*, family Accipitridae, endemic to forests on Luzon, Samar, Leyte and Mindanao in the Philippines.
- **Status:** Living; Critically Endangered.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **6.0 kg**, **100.0 cm** total length and roughly **2 m** wingspan. The female is the heavier and longer sex and therefore the stronger fighting form.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** published summaries describe the smaller sex as roughly 10% smaller than the female. A museum-specimen survey summarized on Wikipedia gives average lengths of about 95 cm for one sex and 105 cm for the other (females larger), and average masses of about 4.5 kg versus 6.0 kg (females heavier).
- **Population notes:** Mindanao holds the majority of remaining breeding pairs. Diet differs by island: flying lemurs dominate on Mindanao, while on Luzon (where flying lemurs are absent) macaques and cloud rats are reported as main prey.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The feet and talons do the killing, and the deep, arched bill tears apart prey once it is secured. Prey records show the practical reach of this weapon system: Philippine flying lemurs (colugos), Asian palm civets, macaques, flying squirrels, fruit bats, rats, hornbills and owls, snakes and monitor lizards. A five-pair Mindanao study (1999–2007) found flying lemurs made up **54.2%** of identifiable prey, with at least 14 prey taxa recorded and arboreal mammals the most important prey group. Some summaries put flying lemurs as high as 90% of the diet in parts of Mindanao.

These prey show a raptor able to kill medium arboreal mammals in the canopy, but on the absolute roster a 6 kg eagle still has modest total force. Its prey-killing power is recorded mostly in Weaponry and Attack, not Raw Power.

### Offensive mechanics
Two hunting methods are described. In still-hunting, the eagle sits almost motionless on a branch near the canopy and watches for prey. In perch-hunting, it glides from perch to perch, working down through the tree layers and climbing back up to start again. Pairs are reported to hunt monkey troops together: one bird perches nearby as a distraction while the other attacks from behind.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
This eagle is a forest raptor that moves between perches and through canopy gaps. Its long wings give a very large wing surface for its mass, so it is less compact than the harpy eagle's short-winged build, but it is still a skilled forest flier. Agility is scored as very high but a little below the harpy eagle's canopy specialization.

### Endurance
Satellite telemetry of six adults on Mindanao found a median 95% home range of **68 km²** (range 39–161 km²) and a median core range of **13 km²**. Adults spent **75–80%** of their space use outside the core area, probably while searching for food. This points to sustained daily movement across a large territory, not long pursuit flights.

### Senses
Like other large forest eagles, it hunts by sight from concealed perches in cluttered canopy. No species-specific sensory measurement was located, so Senses is scored in line with other forest eagles, not from invented acuity figures.

### Intelligence and tactics
Evidence of tactical flexibility includes the two hunting methods above, reported cooperative pair hunting of monkeys, prey switching between islands, and juveniles seen practicing grips on knotholes and attacking objects. This supports good raptor-level tactics, not corvid or primate problem solving.

### Intraspecific behavior and aggression
Pairs are monogamous, hold large territories and share parental care. The Mindanao study recorded eagles taking domestic animals, which leads to conflict with farmers. Ferocity is based on predatory commitment and territorial behavior; no evidence of routine eagle-versus-eagle combat was found.

### Environmental strengths
- Mature dipterocarp rainforest with tall emergent perches.
- Canopy prey that cannot leave the three-dimensional forest volume.
- Large forested home ranges that allow patient, low-cost searching.

### Weaknesses
- Low absolute body mass compared with medium and large mammals.
- No armor; hollow-boned avian body vulnerable to crushing or grappling.
- Very dependent on intact forest structure.
- Loses most of its advantage if grounded or forced into close contact with a larger terrestrial animal.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** small-to-medium arboreal mammals, snakes and lizards, lightly built birds, opponents that can be ambushed from above in forest.
- **Mixed:** similar-mass raptors and agile medium carnivores where first contact decides the outcome.
- **Bad:** armored animals, large terrestrial grapplers, big cats, bears, large primates in a committed ground fight, and anything too heavy for the feet to control.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Tag-Team Troop Ambush** — pairs are reported to hunt monkey troops together, one eagle drawing attention from a nearby perch while the partner strikes from behind. In a matchup this adds a distraction-and-flank pattern that single raptors lack.
2. **Canopy Still-Hunt** — the eagle sits motionless near the canopy, then works down through the tree layers by gliding from perch to perch before a sudden talon strike. This keeps the first-contact advantage with the eagle.

### Unique traits
1. **Colugo Specialist** — on Mindanao the Philippine flying lemur makes up more than half of identified prey in nest studies, a narrow prey specialization unusual among large eagles.
2. **Great Arched Bill** — a very deep, strongly curved bill, among the most massive of any eagle, used to tear apart prey the feet have secured.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
The Philippine Eagle lives only in the Philippines, on Luzon, Samar, Leyte and Mindanao. It lives mainly in remnant primary dipterocarp forest but also uses second-growth and gallery forest. A 2023 habitat model identified about 2.86 million hectares of suitable forest across its range, with Mindanao holding the largest share of breeding pairs.

### Diet and ecology
The eagle is the top avian predator of Philippine forests. On Mindanao the Philippine flying lemur is the main prey, supplemented by palm civets, macaques, flying and tree squirrels, fruit bats, rats, snakes, monitor lizards and birds including hornbills and owls. On Luzon, where flying lemurs are absent, macaques and giant cloud rats are reported as principal prey. As a top predator with large space needs it is treated as an umbrella species for Philippine forest conservation.

### Social structure
Adults live as monogamous territorial pairs, reportedly mating for life unless a partner dies. Telemetry shows a median home range around 68 km² per adult. Hunting is usually solitary, with reported cooperative hunting by pairs on monkey troops.

### Reproduction and life history
A full breeding cycle lasts about two years, and both parents share care of the young for roughly 20 months. A Mindanao study of five pairs (1999–2007) found average breeding success of about 60% and productivity of about 0.3 young per pair per year. During courtship, one bird presents its talons and the other rolls over in mid-air to present its own. This slow reproduction makes every lost adult significant for the population.

### Conservation
The species is **Critically Endangered**. A 2023 population estimate based on habitat modelling and telemetry-measured territory size gives about **392 breeding pairs** (about 784 mature individuals), higher than earlier estimates of 340 pairs (2018) and 88–221 pairs (1989). The main threats are deforestation and loss of nesting sites, accidental capture in traps set for wild pigs, and shooting, including retaliatory killing after attacks on domestic animals. The Philippine Eagle Foundation runs captive breeding, rescue, rehabilitation and release work.

### Special features
- Very deep, arched bill and powerful talons.
- Long, shaggy crest of head and nape feathers.
- Among the longest eagles, with a very large wing surface for its mass.
- Still-hunting and perch-hunting methods suited to dense forest.
- Pair-based territorial life with a two-year breeding cycle.

### Human interaction
It is the national bird of the Philippines and a flagship species for forest conservation. Conflict arises when eagles take poultry or other domestic animals, and birds are still lost to shooting and to snares set for other animals. Conservation breeding, rescue and release by the Philippine Eagle Foundation are among the main responses.

### Genuine fun facts
- Although known as the "monkey-eating eagle", its main prey in Mindanao nest studies is the Philippine flying lemur, at 54.2% of identified prey items.
- It is the national bird of the Philippines.
- The Peregrine Fund calls it the largest eagle alive by length and wing surface area.
- Satellite-tracked adults on Mindanao had a median home range of about 68 km².
- Parents share care of a single brood for about 20 months, so pairs breed only about every two years.
- Pairs have been reported hunting monkeys as a team, one bird distracting while the other strikes.
- A captive eagle at the Philippine Eagle Center in Davao is reported to have lived 46 years.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Philippine Eagle is a critically endangered forest giant of the Philippines: about 6 kg in the canonical female, roughly a metre long, with a 2 m wingspan and a massive arched bill. It still-hunts flying lemurs, civets and monkeys from canopy perches, and pairs sometimes attack monkey troops together. Its forest ambush is formidable, but low absolute mass and no armor leave it far below large mammals in power and defense.

### Rich narrative profile
The Philippine Eagle is built for one of the hardest hunting problems in the tropics: finding and seizing gliding and climbing mammals in tall, dense dipterocarp forest. It sits almost motionless near the canopy for long periods, then works down through the layers of the forest from perch to perch. When a flying lemur, civet or macaque is found, the feet make the kill and the massive, deep bill finishes the job.

That toolkit is excellent on its own ground. Reported pair hunting on monkey troops, one eagle drawing attention while the other strikes from behind, adds coordination that most raptors lack. Wide forest home ranges, measured by satellite telemetry at a median of around 68 km², show a predator that covers a lot of ground patiently, not in long chases.

On the absolute roster, though, a 6 kg eagle stays a 6 kg eagle. It cannot match the grappling strength, mass or durability of medium and large mammals, and once grounded or pinned it has little protection. Its best matchups are ones where it chooses the moment and angle of contact in forest; open ground, armor, large body size and heavy grappling reverse the result.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** 4.04–8.0 kg (Peregrine Fund) versus 4.7–8 kg and lower averages (other samples). Canonical 6.0 kg follows the reported female average.
- **Height:** "stands a metre tall" is descriptive, so `height_cm` is left at 0.0.
- **Lifespan:** 30–60 year wild estimates are weakly sourced; the 46-year figure is a captive maximum. Canonical 30.0 is flagged low-moderate.
- **Diet share:** the 54.2% flying-lemur figure is a measured nest-study value; the popular "90%" figure is a regional upper claim and is not used as the species norm.
- **Cooperative hunting:** reported in secondary summaries; no quantified frequency was located.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Harpy Eagle (8.0 kg; A44.0 D19.0 Ag91.0 St60.0 I65.0 Sp79.0):** the harpy has heavier feet, the most extreme talons and the stronger canopy sensory package, so it stays above in Attack, Agility and Special. The Philippine Eagle gets slightly higher Stamina (telemetry-backed wide ranging) and Intelligence (reported cooperative pair hunting).
- **Versus Golden Eagle (5.0 kg; A40.0 Ag88.0 St79.0):** similar-weight raptor. The Philippine Eagle gets slightly higher Attack for its larger body and bill and its medium-mammal prey, but lower Stamina because the golden eagle is a long-distance soaring and pursuit hunter.
- **Versus Bald Eagle (5.3 kg; A36.0 D22.0):** the Philippine Eagle is a more committed mammal-killer, so its Attack and Weaponry are higher; Defense stays close because neither has real protection.
- **Versus Coyote (15 kg; A38.0 D25.0):** the eagle's Attack is slightly higher because of its weapon quality and air-to-canopy strike geometry, but Raw Power (21 versus 31) and Defense stay lower, reflecting its much smaller absolute mass.
- **Anti-inflation check:** "largest eagle" status is expressed in Size 16.0, not in Raw Power or Defense. Special is not pushed toward the harpy's 79.0 because the two signature features (pair hunting, bill) are less extreme than the harpy's talons plus hearing-aided targeting.
