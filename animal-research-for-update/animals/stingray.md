# Stingray (*Hypanus americanus*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Stingray (southern stingray)
- **Scientific name:** *Hypanus americanus* (Hildebrand and Schroeder, 1928); formerly *Dasyatis americana*, the name used in most older papers and by the Florida Museum
- **Exact animal represented:** the southern stingray, a large whiptail stingray of the western Atlantic, chosen as the site's representative stingray. The photo currently on the site shows an ocellated eagle ray (*Aetobatus ocellatus*), a different family; it is due to be replaced with a southern stingray photo.
- **Status:** Living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy mature adult female, **35.0 kg** and **100 cm disc width**, matching wild mature females tagged at Grand Cayman (mean 102.2 cm disc width).
- **Sex choice:** females grow far larger than males (maximum about 150 cm against 67 cm disc width), and both sexes carry the tail spine, so the larger adult female is the representative animal. Adult males in aquaria weigh only about 7 kg.
- **Population notes:** the upper surface is olive-brown, green or grey and the underside white. Size at maturity varies by region; in the southern Gulf of Mexico females matured at 76.4 cm and males at 51.7 cm disc width.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- Florida Museum: maximum about **97 kg** (214 lb) at 200 cm disc width. FishBase: maximum published weight **135.6 kg**. Both are extremes.
- Aquarium-housed females (Frontiers in Veterinary Science 2024): **20.5 kg at 83.5 cm** disc width and 10.7 kg at 70.3 cm.
- Adult aquarium males (Animals 2021): mean **6.85–7.22 kg**.
- **Canonical:** **35.0 kg**, an estimate scaled from the measured 83.5 cm / 20.5 kg female to a 100 cm disc (mass taken to rise with the cube of disc width). No directly weighed wild female of 100 cm was found.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Height
- Not meaningful for a flattened ray; no body-thickness measurement was found.
- **Canonical `height_cm`: 0.0.**

### Length
- Wikipedia: females grow to **150 cm** disc width, males to **67 cm**; a captive growth study estimated average maximum widths of 150.9 cm for females and 67.4 cm for males (Henningsen and Leaf 2010).
- Florida Museum and FishBase: maximum **200 cm** disc width; FishBase common width 90 cm. The disc is about 1.2 times as wide as it is long, and the tail can be up to twice the body length.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 100.0 cm** as disc width, the standard size measure for rays; tail length is excluded.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Speed
- Swims by passing waves along its pectoral fins; described as a smooth, efficient and highly manoeuvrable swimmer. No measured maximum speed was found.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 0.0** (no reliable measurement).

### Lifespan
- FishBase: maximum reported age **28 years**.
- Henningsen and Leaf (2010): estimated longevity **31.5 years** for females and 7.9 years for males from captive growth curves; the oldest animals in the study were 13 (female) and 12 (male).
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 28.0**, the reported maximum age; typical wild lifespan is unknown.
- **Confidence:** Low-moderate.

### Bite
- Flat, grinding tooth plates for crushing crabs, clams and other hard prey. No bite measurement was found.
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.**

### Weapon dimensions
- One long, serrated spine near the base of the tail, with **52–80 teeth on each side**; its length is roughly equal to the distance between the outer edges of the eye sockets (Florida Museum).
- The spine is covered in venomous mucus. Wikipedia notes stings are not fatal to humans but are extremely painful. Southern stingray mucus contains protein-cutting enzymes and causes swelling and white-cell recruitment in mice (Coelho et al. 2019).

### Defensive structure
- An irregular row of short spines along the upper surface of the disc (FishBase), tough skin, and a cartilage skeleton. The main passive defense is burying in sand.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The weapon is the tail spine, used in defense. When the ray is stepped on or grabbed, it uses its tail to drive the serrated, venom-coated spine into the attacker. FishBase describes the result as a painful laceration, and Wikipedia notes that stings are not fatal to people but are extremely painful. The spine protects the ray mainly from above and behind, the direction from which a foot or a predator's mouth arrives. The mouth, underneath the head, crushes buried crabs, clams, worms and small fish but is not used as a weapon against large animals.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The ray swims by sending waves along its broad pectoral fins, which gives fine control, quick turns and the ability to hover, settle and bury itself. It can also flap its fins hard to stir up sand. It is a bottom specialist rather than a fast open-water swimmer.

### Endurance
Southern stingrays forage almost continuously and can travel along the tide over large feeding areas. Wild females at Grand Cayman were solitary and nocturnally active; rays fed by tourists switched to daytime activity and used much smaller areas (Corcoran et al. 2013). Tagged females in the Bahamas showed small home ranges and high site residency (Schwanck et al. 2020).

### Senses
Ampullae of Lorenzini concentrated around the head detect the weak electric fields of prey buried in sand. A strong sense of smell, a lateral-line system and touch complete the package. In the wild, females reacted to low-frequency tones from 140 dB and males from 160 dB (Mickle et al. 2020), the first field demonstration of sound sensitivity in rays.

### Intelligence and tactics
It uncovers hidden prey by blowing jets of water from its mouth or by flapping its fins against the bottom. It learns quickly: at Stingray City, Grand Cayman, at least 164 rays, over 80% female, learned to gather at a feeding site, reversed their normal day-night activity and crowded into shared areas (72% overlap in core areas, against 3% in wild rays). Where reef sharks are common, southern stingrays shift to safer shallow flats (Bond et al. 2019), showing risk-sensitive habitat choice.

### Sociality and hunting
Usually solitary or in pairs, but groups form for mating, resting and protection, and densities of up to 245 per km² occur in shallow nursery areas. Double-crested cormorants follow foraging rays to catch fish flushed from the sand.

### Fighting and aggression
Docile towards divers and easily hand-fed. During mating a male bites and holds the female's pectoral fin, and females in aquaria sometimes bite each other's fin edges. Aggression is otherwise defensive.

### Environmental strengths
- Sandy and muddy bottoms, seagrass beds and shallow flats where it can bury.
- Reef-edge sand patches near cover.
- Night-time foraging, when its electrical sense matters most.

### Weaknesses
- Large sharks are its main predators; great hammerheads are among the predators it uses its spine against.
- The spine covers only part of the body; the flat disc edges are exposed.
- No speed advantage in open water.
- Relies on hiding rather than escape.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** animals that step on it, grab it from above or try to bite its back.
- **Mixed:** medium fish and crustaceans that avoid the tail.
- **Bad:** great hammerheads and other large sharks, crocodilians, orcas, and any large predator that attacks the disc edge from the front.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Serrated Venom Spine** — a long tail spine with 52–80 teeth on each side and a venomous mucus coat, driven into anything that steps on or grabs the ray and leaving a painful, jagged laceration.
2. **Buried-Prey Detector** — ampullae of Lorenzini around the head sense the electric fields of animals hidden in sand, and the ray blows water jets or flaps its fins to dig them out.

### Unique traits
1. **Sand Blanket** — it buries itself in the bottom with only its eyes and spiracles showing, breathing through the spiracles on top of its head instead of through its mouth.
2. **Stingray City Regular** — at Grand Cayman, more than 160 wild southern stingrays have learned to be hand-fed by tourists, reversing their normal night-time activity.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Tropical and subtropical western Atlantic from New Jersey to southern Brazil, including the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean. It lives on sandy and muddy bottoms, seagrass beds, lagoons and reef-edge sand from the shallows down to about 53 m, and enters brackish water.

### Diet and ecology
An opportunistic, almost continuous bottom forager on crabs and other crustaceans, bony fishes, worms, clams and other molluscs. One Florida ray's stomach was filled almost entirely with lancelets. Its digging stirs the sea floor and exposes prey for other animals.

### Social structure
Mostly solitary or in pairs. Groups form for mating, resting and predator protection, and dense aggregations gather at tourist feeding sites and in nursery areas. Females tend to stay in small home ranges.

### Reproduction and life history
Live-bearing: embryos feed first on yolk and then on uterine "milk" secreted by the mother. After a gestation of about 4–11 months, 2–10 pups (average 4) are born at 20–34 cm disc width. Males mature at about 48–52 cm disc width and 3–4 years, females at about 75–80 cm and 5–6 years. Larger females carry more pups.

### Conservation
Listed as **Near Threatened** by the IUCN (2020 assessment). FishBase rates its vulnerability to fishing as very high (77 of 100), and population growth is slow (minimum doubling time 4.5–14 years). Tourism feeding at sites such as Stingray City changes its behaviour and crowds animals together, which managers are asked to limit.

### Special features
- Venomous, serrated tail spine.
- Ampullae of Lorenzini for electroreception.
- Spiracles on top of the head for breathing while buried.
- Undulating pectoral-fin swimming.
- A row of short spines along the upper surface of the disc.

### Human interaction
Injuries commonly happen when waders step on a buried ray, which strikes with its spine in self-defense. Southern stingrays are the stars of hand-feeding sites in the Cayman Islands, Antigua and the Turks and Caicos, and live in many public aquarium touch pools. Stingray spines have been used by people in several regions to make spear points and knives, and the venom is being studied for biomedical research.

### Genuine fun facts
- Its tail spine carries 52–80 small teeth along each edge.
- The spine is roughly as long as the distance between the ray's eye sockets.
- Females grow more than twice as wide as males.
- Rays at Stingray City, Grand Cayman, switched from night-time to daytime activity because of tourist feeding.
- Cormorants follow foraging stingrays to catch fish flushed out of the sand.
- Female southern stingrays reacted to underwater sound at lower levels than males.
- It can breathe through the spiracles on top of its head while buried in sand.

### Concise site-ready summary
A broad, diamond-shaped western Atlantic ray, the southern stingray hunts buried prey with an electrical sense and protects itself with a serrated, venom-coated tail spine. Docile unless stepped on or attacked, it relies on hiding in sand and on its sting rather than speed or strength.

### Rich narrative profile
The southern stingray spends much of its life lying flat on the sea floor under a thin layer of sand, with only its eyes and the breathing holes behind them showing. Adult females grow a disc around a metre wide, more than twice the width of males, and trail a long, whip-like tail armed near its base with a serrated spine.

At night it goes hunting. Sensors around its head pick up the faint electric fields of crabs, clams and fish hidden in the sand, and the ray blows jets of water or flaps its fins to dig them out, then crushes them with flat tooth plates. It is also a quick learner: on Grand Cayman, more than 160 wild rays have learned that boats mean food and now swarm around snorkellers by day.

In an Animal Battle Stats matchup, the stingray is a defender rather than an attacker. Anything that steps on it or seizes it from above risks a deep, venomous, tearing wound. But the spine covers only part of its body, it cannot outswim large predators, and large sharks, its main predators, still catch and eat stingrays despite their stings.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Mass:** the canonical 35 kg is an estimate scaled from a measured 83.5 cm female; published maxima of 97 kg (Florida Museum) and 135.6 kg (FishBase) describe the largest individuals.
- **Maximum width:** 150 cm (Wikipedia, growth-curve asymptote) versus 200 cm (Florida Museum, FishBase); the representative 100 cm is below both.
- **Lifespan:** 28 years (FishBase reported age) versus a 31.5-year growth-curve estimate for females; the oldest known-age captive animals were only 12–13.
- **Photo:** the live image is an eagle ray, not this species.
- **Speed and bite:** no measurements; both 0.0.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Manta Ray (1,400 kg; A42 D57 Ag72 St89 I76 Sp70):** the manta is forty times heavier and a tireless open-water swimmer, so it stays far above in Defense, Stamina and Agility. The southern stingray's venomous spine gives it a higher Special and an Attack not far below the unarmed giant.
- **Versus Sawfish (300 kg; A76 D58 Ag61 St68 I48 Sp88):** another electroreceptive ray, but its huge toothed rostrum and mass put it far above in Attack and Defense; its Special stays higher because the rostrum is both a sensor and a weapon.
- **Versus Electric Eel (15 kg; A49 D29 Sp98):** electric discharge is a far stronger and rarer weapon than a defensive sting, so the eel stays above in Attack and Special; the heavier stingray, with a spine that deters attacks, is above in Defense.
- **Versus Lionfish (0.5 kg; A25 D38 Sp87) and Stonefish (1.5 kg; A27 D50 Sp95):** the stingray is far larger and its spine can wound big animals, hence higher Attack; stonefish venom is much more dangerous and its camouflage better, so it keeps higher Defense and Special.
- **Anti-inflation check:** the sting is dangerous to people who step on it, but it is a defensive weapon; Attack is held at 35.0.
