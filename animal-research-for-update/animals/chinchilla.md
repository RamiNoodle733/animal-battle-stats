# Chinchilla (*Chinchilla lanigera*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Chinchilla (long-tailed chinchilla, Chilean chinchilla)
- **Scientific name:** *Chinchilla lanigera* (Bennett, 1829); also written *Chinchilla laniger*
- **Exact animal represented:** the long-tailed chinchilla, including the endangered wild population of northern Chile and the domestic pet and fur-farm chinchilla bred from it. Not the short-tailed chinchilla (*C. chinchilla*) or the related viscachas.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (wild species plus a domestic form).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult female of about **0.6 kg** in domestic condition.
- **Sex choice:** females are the larger and dominant sex. ADW notes that females are very aggressive toward one another and toward males during estrus, and San Diego Zoo reports adult females about 30% larger than males in human care, a difference less marked in the wild.
- **Population variation:** wild adults are small (males 369–493 g, mean 412 g; females 379–450 g, mean 422 g). Domestic chinchillas are almost twice the size of wild ones, with females up to about 800 g and males up to about 600 g. The canonical 0.6 kg female sits between the wild mean and the domestic maximum.

## 4. Combat biology

### Weapons and offense
The chinchilla is a grazing and seed-eating rodent with a notably weak incisor bite for its family, measured at about 23.5 N. It fights mainly within its own species: females threaten with growls, teeth chattering and urine, and group-housed chinchillas can inflict bite wounds that cost each other pieces of ear and toe. Against predators it does not fight back meaningfully.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Short forelimbs and long, muscular hind limbs make it a bounding, jumping rodent. ADW describes wild chinchillas climbing and jumping on rocks "with amazing agility", and it can clear gaps of up to about 1.8 m. On broken rock this makes it extremely hard to corner.

### Endurance
Chinchillas are burst movers that dash for cover. Their dense coat and inability to pant or sweat make them prone to overheating, which limits sustained exertion in warm conditions.

### Senses
Large eyes with vertical slit pupils for twilight activity, long whiskers (10–13 cm) and big ears over greatly expanded middle-ear bullae. A behavioral audiogram measured hearing from about **50 Hz to 33 kHz**, a range more like a human's than most rodents'. They use about ten different calls in different contexts.

### Intelligence and tactics
Chinchillas live in colonies (herds) that can exceed 100 animals, which helps predator detection. Their tactics are escape tactics: run fast, dart into rock crevices, spray urine, and shed fur if caught.

### Sociality
Highly social and matriarchal, with females dominant. Serious fighting in the wild is rare, and most pairs are monogamous.

### Fighting and aggression
Mostly threat displays. Females are aggressive toward other females and toward males around estrus, but injuries mainly occur in crowded captive groups.

### Predator defense
Predators include birds of prey, foxes and other canids, wild cats, skunks and snakes. The chinchilla's defenses are speed, crevices, urine spraying and fur slip, which leaves a biting attacker with a mouthful of hair while the chinchilla escapes.

### Environmental strengths
- Steep, rocky, broken ground full of crevices and ledges.
- Cool, dry, high-altitude or arid climate.
- Colony settings where many eyes watch for predators.

### Weaknesses
- Very weak bite and tiny absolute mass.
- Overheats easily; cannot pant or sweat.
- Fragile ear pinnae easily injured.
- Flat, open ground removes its rock refuges.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** almost none offensively; it outlasts slow ground predators by escaping into rocks.
- **Mixed:** small predators that must grab and hold it, where fur slip can break the grip.
- **Bad:** raptors, foxes, cats, mustelids, snakes, and anything larger than itself.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Fur Slip** — when bitten or roughly grabbed, a chinchilla releases a large patch of fur, exposing clean skin and leaving the attacker holding hair while it escapes. It can break a predator's first grip but does nothing against a crushing or killing bite.
2. **Rock-Hopper Leap** — long, muscular hind limbs let it bound across rocks and clear gaps of up to about 1.8 m, darting into crevices where most predators cannot follow.

### Unique traits
1. **Densest Fur on Land** — about 20,000 hairs per square centimetre, with 50–75 hairs sprouting from each follicle compared with two or three in humans; chinchillas keep it clean by rolling in fine volcanic dust instead of water.
2. **Hunted to the Brink** — its fur made it the target of an international trade that emptied most of its range; one full-length coat can need about 150 pelts, and the wild species is now Endangered and listed on CITES Appendix I.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
Wild long-tailed chinchillas survive only in northern Chile, in arid, rocky mountain country where they den in crevices and holes among rocks; known colonies include Aucó near Illapel (Reserva Nacional Las Chinchillas) and La Higuera north of Coquimbo. Historically the genus ranged across the Andes of Chile, Peru, Bolivia and Argentina. Domestic chinchillas live worldwide.

### Diet and ecology
Mainly herbivorous: grasses, herbs, sedges, seeds, lichens and mosses, with occasional insects and bird eggs. They sit upright and hold food in their forepaws. The native climate swings from about 30 °C by day in summer to below freezing on winter nights.

### Social structure
Colonies (herds) of 14 to more than 100 animals, matriarchal, with dominant females and mostly monogamous pairs.

### Reproduction and life history
Gestation is long for a rodent, about 111 days, producing one to six (usually two) fully furred young with open eyes that can run almost at once. Females usually have two litters a year; young mature at about 8 months. Chinchillas can live about 10 years in the wild and up to 20 in human care.

### Conservation
The long-tailed chinchilla is **Endangered** on the IUCN Red List (2025 assessment) and on CITES Appendix I. Centuries of hunting for fur, including dynamiting of burrow systems, destroyed most wild populations, and poaching continues. Domestic chinchillas are not covered by CITES.

### Special features
- Densest fur of any land mammal (about 20,000 hairs per cm²).
- Fur slip escape reaction.
- Long, muscular hind limbs for jumping.
- Greatly expanded middle-ear bullae and large ears.
- Vertical slit pupils and very long whiskers.

### Human interaction
The Chincha people of the Andes wore chinchilla fur and ate the meat, and the Inca kept chinchillas. Commercial hunting in northern Chile began in earnest in 1828 and drove the wild animals to near extinction. Farm-raised chinchillas now supply the fur trade and the pet market; pet chinchillas need cool, dry housing and regular dust baths.

### Genuine fun facts
- A single chinchilla hair follicle can sprout 50–75 hairs; a human follicle grows two or three.
- If a predator bites, a chinchilla can shed a patch of fur and escape, a reaction called fur slip.
- Chinchillas bathe in fine volcanic dust, not water.
- They can jump up to about 1.8 m.
- Newborn chinchillas are fully furred with open eyes and can run almost immediately.
- Chinchilla hearing, measured at 50 Hz to 33 kHz, is closer to a human's than most rodents' hearing is.

### Concise site-ready summary
The chinchilla is a 0.6 kg Andean rock-hopper wrapped in the densest fur of any land mammal, which it can shed in clumps to escape a predator's bite. It is agile, social and wary, but its bite is weak even by rodent standards, so it survives by leaping away rather than fighting.

### Rich narrative profile
The chinchilla is built for escape on broken rock. Long hind legs launch it across gaps of nearly two metres, big ears and eyes watch for predators at dusk, and colonies of dozens of animals share the job of spotting danger. When a fox, cat or bird of prey does close in, the chinchilla has two last tricks: it sprays urine, and if bitten it releases a clump of its extraordinarily dense fur and bolts.

What it lacks is any real weapon. Its incisor bite, measured at about 23.5 N, is weak even compared with other South American rodents, and its jaw is poorly built for hard biting. In ABS terms that means very low Attack and Defense, high Agility, and a Special score lifted by the unusual fur-slip escape rather than by any capacity to hurt an opponent.

That same fur nearly wiped the species out. Hunted for centuries for pelts, the long-tailed chinchilla now survives in the wild only in a few colonies in northern Chile and is listed as Endangered, even as its domestic descendants-by-breeding live in homes and farms around the world.

### Conflicts and uncertainty
- **Elevation:** ADW, Merck and San Diego Zoo give 3,000–5,000 m, a figure largely describing the genus, while Wikipedia's recent wild records for *C. lanigera* are colonies in the coastal mountains of north-central Chile. The report avoids a canonical elevation.
- **Length:** wild maximum 26 cm versus ADW up to 38 cm; canonical 28.0 cm is a low-confidence estimate for a domestic female.
- **Hairs per follicle:** sources give 50, 60 or up to 75.
- **Jump height:** 1.8 m and "a six-foot crevice" are institutional descriptions, not measured trials.
- **Bite:** a measured force of 23.5 N exists, but models suggest the animals may not have bitten at full strength; PSI is not assigned.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Flying Squirrel (0.16 kg; A9 D8 Ag96 St61 I66 Sp87):** the chinchilla is heavier with a similar weak bite, so Attack is barely higher (10.0); the squirrel keeps elite Agility and Special for controlled gliding.
- **Versus Quokka (3.5 kg; A18 D18 Ag73 St56 I49 Sp48):** the quokka is six times heavier and sits well above in Attack and Defense; the chinchilla is more agile on rock (84.0 vs 73.0) and has a more unusual defense (Special 64.0 vs 48.0).
- **Versus Naked Mole Rat (0.057 kg; A14):** the two have similar measured absolute bites (about 21 N vs 23.5 N), but the mole-rat's bite is a specialized weapon used in colony defense while the chinchilla's is weak for its size and rarely used, so the chinchilla sits lower in Attack.
- **Versus Hedgehog (1.0 kg; D43 Sp69):** both rely on a defensive specialization. The hedgehog's spines protect it during an attack, so its Defense is far higher; the chinchilla's fur slip helps it escape afterward, so it earns Special (64.0 vs 69.0) but not Defense.
- **Anti-inflation check:** jump descriptions raise Maneuverability, not Speed; fur density raises Protection only slightly.
