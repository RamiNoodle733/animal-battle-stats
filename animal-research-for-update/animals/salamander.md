# Salamander / Eastern Tiger Salamander (*Ambystoma tigrinum*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Roster name:** Salamander
- **Resolved taxon:** Eastern tiger salamander, *Ambystoma tigrinum* (Green, 1825), family Ambystomatidae, order Caudata.
- **Why this resolution is necessary:** the live legacy profile labels the animal only as “Salamander” and gives the scientific name *Caudata*, an entire order rather than a species. A battle profile cannot defensibly assign one mass, length, ecology or image to 800+ morphologically diverse salamander species. This staging report therefore resolves the generic slot to the large, well-studied terrestrial eastern tiger salamander. It is distinct from the roster's separately represented Axolotl and Hellbender.
- **Living/extinct:** living.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime post-metamorphic **adult male**, **0.125 kg**, **25.0 cm total length**. Males are proportionally longer, with longer hind limbs and a more compressed tail; there is no strong evidence for a major sex-based mass advantage, so the male choice is conservative rather than a claim of dramatic dimorphism.
- **Variation/taxonomy:** older literature often used *A. tigrinum* broadly for tiger salamanders now split between eastern *A. tigrinum* and western/barred *A. mavortium*. Canada and NatureServe explicitly document this taxonomic history. This report uses the modern eastern-species concept.

## 2. Physical measurements and ranges
### Mass and length
Animal Diversity Web reports adult total length **17-33 cm**. National Geographic gives **7-14 in (17.8-35.6 cm)** and **4.4 oz (~125 g)**. ADW's automated “average mass 9.402 g” conflicts strongly with the body dimensions and other adult summaries and is not used as an adult canonical mass. The canonical animal is therefore 125 g and 25 cm, representative rather than maximum-sized.

### Height
No standardized standing-height convention is biologically useful for a low-slung salamander. `height_cm` remains 0.0 rather than inventing a body depth.

### Speed and locomotion
No defensible species-specific maximum running or swimming speed was recovered. USGS telemetry is useful for movement ecology, not sprint speed: tracked adults typically moved 50-350 m after leaving breeding wetlands, averaged **7.9 ± 5.6 m/day**, and were found underground on **84.8%** of relocations. `speed_mps` therefore remains 0.0.

### Lifespan
ADW reports normal adults reaching **16 years**, while aquatic captive adults can reach 25 years. National Geographic gives roughly 12-15 years in the wild. Canonical `lifespan_years` is **16.0**, explicitly an observed longevity benchmark rather than mean wild life expectancy.

### Bite and feeding apparatus
No reliable *A. tigrinum* bite-force pressure in PSI was found. Terrestrial adults capture prey through tongue and/or jaw prehension. High-speed work on adult tiger salamanders shows tongue protrusion peaks around maximum gape, while aquatic feeding after metamorphosis uses a different kinematic pattern. `bite_force_psi` remains **0.0** rather than converting unrelated salamander estimates.

### Defensive skin secretion
A peer-reviewed Copeia study found tiger-salamander granular-gland secretion contains **adhesive and neuroactive components**. At least one component inhibited neurotransmission in rat hippocampal slices, and disulfide-linked proteins were required for adhesive properties. This supports a real chemical defense but does not establish a lethal dose against a particular predator or justify calling the animal venomous.

### Regeneration
Tiger salamanders retain meaningful regenerative ability after metamorphosis. Modern work shows post-metamorphic animals can regenerate limbs, although more slowly and with more skeletal anomalies than larvae. Earlier controlled work found complete limb regeneration in adult land-phase *Ambystoma tigrinum* under suitable conditions in roughly **155-180 days**. Tail-regeneration work likewise documents regeneration across larval, metamorphic and postmetamorphic stages. This is long-term recovery, not an in-fight healing factor.

## 4. Combat biology
### Weapons and offensive mechanics
The tiger salamander is an opportunistic small-prey carnivore. Its broad head, jaws and tongue system capture earthworms, arthropods and other small vertebrate prey. On land, tongue projection and jaw prehension bring prey into the mouth; in water, metamorphosed adults can use oropharyngeal suction. Against another animal of comparable size, the bite can grip soft tissue, but absolute tissue damage is tiny on the full ABS roster scale.

### Defenses and toughness
Its strongest active defense is skin secretion rather than armor. The body is soft, permeable and mechanically vulnerable to claws, teeth, trampling, heat and desiccation. Cryptic yellow-on-dark patterning and fossorial behavior reduce encounter risk. Regeneration improves long-horizon recovery after survivable injury but cannot prevent immediate incapacitation.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
Adults walk with a low, sprawling tetrapod gait and can swim during breeding-season aquatic activity. They can turn in confined burrows and around cover, but do not have the acceleration, jumping or aerial/aquatic control of roster agility specialists. Their best movement tactic is concealment and short repositioning rather than speed.

### Endurance
Tiger salamanders are ectothermic and spend much of the year underground. Seasonal migrations can cover hundreds of meters, but telemetry shows low day-to-day displacement after burrow settlement. This supports adequate endurance for a fossorial amphibian while strongly arguing against treating breeding migration as continuous high-output stamina.

### Senses
Chemical cues and vision are important in salamanders. Adults locate prey at close range and orient to seasonal breeding habitat. There is no evidence here for echolocation, extreme visual acuity or another specialist sense that would warrant a high absolute score.

### Intelligence and tactics
Behavior is flexible enough to switch prey-capture mechanics between land and water and to time breeding migrations to suitable wet conditions. Adults use burrows and protective cover strategically in the ecological sense. Evidence does not support mammal, corvid or cephalopod-level general problem solving.

### Fighting, predation and predator defense
Tiger salamanders are voracious predators of appropriately small prey and can consume worms, insects, frogs and other salamanders. Adults are largely solitary outside breeding. Their combat style is therefore ambush/close capture against much smaller prey, while predator defense emphasizes concealment, chemical secretion and escape into cover rather than prolonged fighting.

### Environmental strengths
- Damp soil, burrows, leaf litter and cool nocturnal conditions.
- Temporary or fish-free breeding wetlands.
- Confined cover where small size and fossorial behavior reduce detection.
- Water permits suction feeding by metamorphosed adults.

### Environmental limitations
- Permeable amphibian skin creates strong dependence on moisture.
- Heat and desiccation rapidly erode performance.
- Open dry terrain removes the primary concealment/refuge advantage.
- Small absolute body mass means even moderate mammalian or avian opponents can inflict catastrophic trauma.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favorable:** tiny soft-bodied invertebrates and other prey substantially below its size, especially in damp cover.

**Mixed:** similarly sized small amphibians where jaw capture, body size and skin secretion matter.

**Unfavorable:** almost every medium or large roster vertebrate, fast aerial predators, armored opponents, dry-environment specialists, and anything able to ignore or avoid the secretion and deliver one decisive bite or strike.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings
- **Attack: 7.0** - capable predator at invertebrate/small-vertebrate scale, but its jaws deliver negligible absolute damage against most of the roster.
- **Defense: 14.0** - chemical deterrence and concealment help, while soft permeable skin and tiny mass leave little direct damage resistance.
- **Agility: 50.0** - good close-control movement for a fossorial amphibian, without conflating agility with an unsupported sprint number.
- **Stamina: 42.0** - can migrate overland and persist efficiently as an ectotherm, but telemetry supports a mostly low-output lifestyle.
- **Intelligence: 32.0** - context-sensitive feeding and migration behavior, but no evidence for high general problem solving.
- **Special: 74.0** - neuroactive adhesive skin secretion and post-metamorphic regeneration are genuinely unusual matchup/recovery mechanisms, even though neither turns this 125 g amphibian into a powerful fighter.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities
1. **Adhesive Neuroactive Secretion** - granular skin glands produce a sticky secretion with experimentally demonstrated neuroactive components, potentially making a predator's mouth contact unpleasant or disruptive. It is a defensive secretion, not injected venom, and lethality is not assumed.
2. **Regenerative Repair** - post-metamorphic tiger salamanders retain the ability to rebuild lost tail and limb tissue over long time scales. This strongly improves recovery from survivable injury but provides essentially no instantaneous healing during a battle.

## 8. Exactly two unique traits
1. **Burrowed Moisture Refuge** - adults spend most of the year underground, sometimes more than 60 cm deep, using humid refuges to avoid surface temperature and moisture extremes.
2. **Dual-Medium Feeding Shift** - metamorphosed adults can use terrestrial tongue/jaw prey capture and switch feeding kinematics when feeding in water, preserving predatory function across land and aquatic breeding contexts.

## 9. Expanded profile
### Habitat and geographic range
Eastern tiger salamanders occupy much of eastern and central North America in habitats with suitable loose soil, underground refuges and access to breeding wetlands. They tolerate grassland, agricultural mosaics and other relatively open habitats better than many woodland salamanders, but successful reproduction depends on appropriate ponds or temporary waters.

### Diet and ecology
Adults are carnivorous generalists taking earthworms, insects and other invertebrates plus suitably small vertebrates, including frogs and salamanders. During aquatic breeding periods, metamorphosed adults can consume substantial aquatic invertebrate prey by suction. They are predators at the soil/litter and pond-edge scale and prey for larger vertebrates.

### Social structure
Adults are primarily solitary outside the breeding season. During breeding migrations they aggregate in wetlands. Males generally arrive earlier and court females using nudging and spermatophore deposition rather than parental care or coordinated group behavior.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding occurs in late winter or spring, often following warm rain. Eggs develop in water and larvae are aquatic before metamorphosing into terrestrial juveniles. Some tiger-salamander lineages/populations historically grouped under the broad species concept can retain aquatic adult morphology, one reason taxonomy and life-history context must be recorded carefully.

### Conservation
The eastern tiger salamander is globally secure/Least Concern in broad assessments, but status varies sharply by region. NatureServe lists the species G5, while Canada documents the Carolinian population as Extirpated and historically assessed Prairie populations separately. Habitat loss, road mortality, wetland alteration and fish introduction can cause local declines.

### Adaptations
- thick-bodied fossorial morphology and strong digging/refuge behavior
- moisture-dependent but comparatively terrestrial adult skin
- cryptic disruptive coloration
- tongue/jaw terrestrial prey capture plus aquatic suction capability
- adhesive neuroactive skin secretion
- substantial regenerative capacity after metamorphosis

### Human interaction
Tiger salamanders are important wetland and grassland indicators, are encountered during mass rainy-night breeding migrations, and are used in comparative developmental, feeding and regeneration research. Road mortality can be severe where migration routes cross traffic corridors.

### Genuine fun facts
- USGS telemetry found tracked adults underground during roughly **85%** of relocations.
- Adults can switch from terrestrial tongue/jaw prey capture to suction-based feeding when in water.
- Tiger-salamander skin secretion is not merely slippery: laboratory work identified both adhesive and neuroactive properties.
- Post-metamorphic adults can regenerate limbs, but the process is much slower than the popular image of instant salamander regeneration.
- Modern taxonomic treatments split many western “tiger salamanders” into *Ambystoma mavortium*, so older *A. tigrinum* studies require geographic/taxonomic care.
- A recent USFWS photograph shows the species' entire compact body and tail particularly clearly against a simple pale background, making it a strong future cutout candidate once adulthood is independently established.

### Concise site summary
The Eastern Tiger Salamander is a stout 125 g burrowing amphibian whose direct combat power is tiny on the ABS roster scale. Its real advantages are unusual biology: adhesive neuroactive skin secretion, flexible feeding on land and in water, and the ability to regenerate major tissues after survivable injury. Those traits give it high Special and Recovery without inflating Attack or Defense.

### Rich narrative profile
The generic “Salamander” slot needs a real organism behind it. This report uses the eastern tiger salamander, one of North America's largest and best-studied terrestrial salamanders, rather than pretending the entire order Caudata shares one body size and combat profile. A representative adult is only around a quarter-meter long and roughly an eighth of a kilogram, so absolute battle scaling immediately places its raw power far below mammals, large reptiles and even many birds.

Its biology is nevertheless specialized. Adults spend most of their lives hidden underground and emerge under favorable moisture conditions, especially for breeding migrations. That refuge strategy is a better defense than trying to absorb attacks. If seized, granular skin secretions add a chemically and mechanically unusual deterrent: experiments found adhesive proteins and neuroactive components. The evidence supports a meaningful chemical defense, but not internet-style claims of a quantified lethal toxin.

Tiger salamanders also show why Recovery and Defense must remain separate. They can regenerate lost appendage tissue after metamorphosis, yet adult regeneration takes months and can be imperfect. It cannot rescue an animal from catastrophic trauma in the moment. ABS therefore gives the species an unusually high Recovery/Special profile while keeping Defense low.

Feeding is similarly flexible rather than powerful. On land, adults use tongue and jaw prehension; in water they can switch to suction-based capture. This is a genuine dual-medium predatory system, but it is optimized for worms, arthropods and small vertebrates. Against a fox, hawk or even a modest mammalian predator, the salamander's best strategy remains not to be caught.

## 12. Confidence and conflicts
- **Taxon resolution:** moderate. The roster itself says only Caudata; choosing *A. tigrinum* is an editorial resolution needed to make a scientifically coherent species profile, not a claim that the old site already meant this species.
- **Mass:** moderate. 125 g is credible and representative from a major zoological source; ADW's 9.402 g automated value is inconsistent with adult dimensions and is treated as nonrepresentative/error-prone.
- **Length:** high for range, moderate-high for 25 cm canonical representative.
- **Speed:** unresolved; 0.0 is a missing-data value, not literal immobility.
- **Bite PSI:** unresolved; 0.0 is a missing-data value, not literal zero bite force.
- **Regeneration:** high confidence that adults retain regeneration; lower confidence in translating lab regeneration rate to wild injury recovery.
- **Chemical defense:** high confidence in adhesive/neuroactive secretion chemistry; low confidence for any opponent-specific dose/effect, so none is invented.

## 13. Cross-animal normalization notes
- At **0.125 kg**, this animal must remain dramatically below Red Fox (7.2 kg), Red Panda (5.0 kg), Red-tailed Hawk (1.2 kg), Ring-tailed Lemur (2.5 kg) and all large predators in Attack/Defense. Its biology cannot erase the absolute mass gap.
- Attack 7.0 stays above essentially harmless tiny animals only because the tiger salamander is an active carnivore with functional jaws; it remains far below Red-Eyed Tree Frog Attack 3.0 only by a modest margin in roster terms, not dozens of points.
- Agility 50.0 is intentionally far below the Red-Eyed Tree Frog's 85.0 and Red-tailed Hawk's 90.0. A low-speed fossorial walker should not inherit high agility from small size alone.
- Special 74.0 can legitimately exceed much larger ordinary animals because adhesive neuroactive secretion and regeneration are unusual mechanisms. This does **not** raise Raw Power, Attack or immediate Defense.
- Recovery 79.0 is high but explicitly long-horizon. Adult limb regeneration measured in months prevents double-counting it as combat toughness.
- No old live-site placeholder, including the legacy 100 PSI bite or 36 km/h speed, was used as an anchor.
