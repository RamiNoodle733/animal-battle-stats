# Sailfish (*Istiophorus platypterus*)

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen
- **Common name:** Sailfish
- **Scientific name:** *Istiophorus platypterus* (Shaw, 1792)
- **Roster interpretation:** the living circumtropical sailfish. Modern sources differ on whether Atlantic sailfish should be split as *I. albicans*; this report follows the widespread single-species treatment for the roster while recording the taxonomic conflict.
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy prime adult female, **70.0 kg**, approximately **270.0 cm total length**. Females attain the largest sizes and southeastern-Florida ageing work found females slightly larger and potentially longer-lived than males.
- **Living/extinct:** living.
- **Variation:** Florida Museum reports Atlantic and Pacific maxima near 3.4 m and 100 kg, with southern-Florida fish commonly 1.73-2.29 m. FishBase reports 3.48 m maximum fork length, 2.70 m common total length and 100.2 kg maximum published mass. These maxima are not used as the representative fighter.

## 2. Physical measurements
### Mass and dimensions
- ADW gives 50-100 kg and ~70 kg average, but its associated 130-162 cm length range is inconsistent with fishery references and appears to reflect a different measurement/context.
- Florida Museum gives maximum total length about 3.4 m and maximum mass about 100 kg; largest fish are usually females.
- FishBase gives common total length **270 cm**, maximum fork length **348 cm**, and maximum published mass **100.2 kg**.
- **Canonical mass:** 70.0 kg, a representative large adult rather than a record specimen.
- **Canonical length:** 270.0 cm total length, using FishBase common length and keeping the 3.4 m class as an upper-end value.
- A standardized body `height_cm` is not appropriate for this fish and is left unresolved rather than treating sail height as body height.

### Speed and locomotion
The famous 110 km/h sailfish claim is not used. Svendsen et al. (2016) revisited the claim with muscle contraction time and stride length and estimated **8.3 ± 1.4 m/s** as a theoretical maximum; observed predator-prey interactions averaged about **7 m/s**. The authors specifically concluded that older ~35 m/s estimates are implausible and that actual loaded swimming should be lower than the unloaded-muscle theoretical estimate. Canonical `speed_mps` is therefore **8.3**, with that context retained.

The body is elongate, laterally compressed and streamlined. The first dorsal fin can be depressed during routine swimming, reducing drag, while the caudal peduncle and forked tail provide propulsion and control.

### Lifespan and growth
- NOAA ageing of 1,071 southeast-Florida sailfish yielded readable ages of 1-7 years; the authors stated maximum age could exceed seven because the largest fish could not be aged. Females were slightly larger and may live longer.
- FishBase reports a maximum age of **13 years**.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 13.0**, explicitly a reported maximum-age value rather than a claim of average adult longevity.

### Bill and other weapons
The upper jaw forms a long, narrow rostral bill. Exact representative bill length varies with fish length and measurement convention, so no unsupported single centimeter figure is promoted here. High-speed field video shows the bill is primarily used by inserting it into prey schools with little evasive response, followed by rapid lateral taps or slashes. Domenici et al. found the lateral bill motion among the highest accelerations recorded in an aquatic vertebrate.

The jaws carry small villiform/file-like teeth. There is no defensible standardized bite-pressure measurement suitable for PSI conversion.

### Defense
Defense comes from speed, maneuvering room, streamlined body form, countershading, large size relative to most prey, and the ability to erect or depress the sail. It lacks armor, shell, thick hide or a mammal-like trauma-resistant frame. Adults can still be preyed upon by large pelagic predators.

## 4. Combat biology
### Weapons and offense
The bill is the primary offensive adaptation. Sailfish approach schooling prey, insert the slender bill with surprisingly little hydrodynamic warning, then tap individual prey or whip the bill laterally through the school. The weapon works by speed, reach and rapid lateral acceleration rather than by behaving like a rigid stabbing sword. The jaws then seize disabled prey.

### Defensive adaptations and toughness
The fusiform body and high-performance swimming make interception difficult in open water. Countershading reduces visual contrast. The sail can be folded to reduce drag. Physical toughness is moderate for a 70 kg pelagic fish, but there is no armor and the long bill and fins are exposed structures.

### Maneuverability
Sailfish combine a narrow body, powerful tail, controllable paired fins and a very large dorsal sail. During hunting, the sail and other fins contribute to stability and prey-herding behavior. Agility is therefore elite in its native medium even though measured/theoretical maximum speed is far below the folklore value.

### Endurance
This is a highly migratory pelagic fish. Tagging summaries document movements of hundreds to thousands of kilometers, with maximum reported total movement approaching 4,960 km and daily movements up to tens of kilometers. That supports strong sustained locomotor capacity, but migration distance is not treated as continuous sprint performance.

### Senses and tactics
Vision is central to open-water pursuit and precise bill placement. Hunting is tactically sophisticated at the behavioral level: sailfish can approach schools without triggering early evasion, position the bill among prey, then select taps or broad slashes. Group hunting can occur, but individual scores do not multiply because several fish cooperate.

### Fighting and predation behavior
Primary prey include schooling fishes and cephalopods. Sailfish may hunt around prey schools in groups, with individual fish taking attack opportunities. The bill is a feeding weapon rather than evidence for routine lethal dueling with similarly sized billfish. Spawning involves pursuit, and females tend to reach larger sizes.

### Environmental strengths
- Open tropical/subtropical pelagic water with room to accelerate and turn.
- Schooling prey where the bill's stealth and lateral sweep are most effective.
- Clear-water visual hunting.

### Environmental limitations and weaknesses
- Performance collapses outside water.
- Confined terrain reduces the value of long approach lanes and lateral bill sweeps.
- No heavy armor or shell.
- Bill damage would disproportionately reduce its specialized offense.
- Cold or unsuitable water constrains a warm-water pelagic specialist.

### Matchup archetypes
**Favorable:** smaller unarmored aquatic opponents, schooling fish, opponents that cannot match open-water maneuverability, soft-bodied targets vulnerable to repeated bill slashes.

**Unfavorable:** very large sharks and marine mammals, heavily armored aquatic animals, opponents able to absorb a slash and close decisively, or any terrestrial matchup where normal locomotion is impossible.

## 6. Proposed six headline ABS ratings
- **Attack: 52.0** - A 70 kg predator with a long, rapidly accelerated bill can inflict meaningful wounds, but its feeding system is optimized for small schooling prey and is far below large sharks, crocodilians and megafauna in absolute damage delivery.
- **Defense: 38.0** - Excellent avoidance and respectable size, but little passive protection once caught.
- **Agility: 89.0** - Elite open-water control, acceleration, turning and precision. This score is not derived from the discredited 110 km/h folklore figure.
- **Stamina: 79.0** - Strong migratory locomotion and sustained pelagic activity, clearly separated from burst speed.
- **Intelligence: 53.0** - Flexible, precise predatory tactics and group context support moderate cognition, without evidence for mammal/corvid-level general problem solving.
- **Special: 82.0** - The hydrodynamically stealthy bill and deployable sail create unusual, matchup-changing hunting mechanics not captured by ordinary size or speed.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and exactly two unique traits
### Special abilities
1. **Stealth Bill Slash** - the narrow rostrum can enter a prey school with little detectable disturbance, then accelerate laterally to tap or slash prey before they can evade effectively.
2. **Deployable Sail Control** - the enormous first dorsal fin can be erected during hunting/control behavior and depressed during streamlined swimming, changing stability and drag profile.

### Unique traits
1. **Cavitation-Limited Sprinter** - modern biomechanical work overturns extreme folklore speed estimates and places realistic performance near an 8.3 m/s theoretical muscle-based maximum, illustrating how hydrodynamic limits constrain even elite swimmers.
2. **Fast-Growing Pelagic Billfish** - sailfish grow rapidly early in life, mature within only a few years, and females tend to attain larger sizes than males.

## 8. Expanded profile
### Habitat and range
Sailfish occupy tropical and subtropical epipelagic waters, often near coasts and islands but also in oceanic habitat. FishBase describes the species as highly migratory and generally above the thermocline.

### Diet and ecology
They eat fishes and cephalopods, including schooling prey such as sardines, mackerels, jacks, halfbeaks and related pelagic fishes. Their hunting method makes them both pursuit predators and specialized school exploiters.

### Social structure
They are often solitary or in small groups, but temporary hunting aggregations can exploit dense prey schools. Spawning can involve pairs or several males pursuing a female. Group hunting is treated as behavioral context, not as a multiplier of an individual's physical stats.

### Reproduction and life history
External spawning occurs in warm waters. Growth is rapid in the first year and then slows. NOAA fin-spine ageing found age classes 3-4 especially common in the sampled southeast-Florida sport fishery; females were slightly larger and may live longer.

### Conservation
FishBase/Fishes of Texas reports the 2021 IUCN assessment as **Vulnerable**. Sailfish are also important gamefish and encounter commercial fisheries, including longline gear.

### Adaptations
Key adaptations include the elongated rostrum, high-aspect forked tail, streamlined compressed body, depressible sail, paired-fin control, countershading and visually precise prey-school attacks.

### Human interaction
Sailfish are major recreational gamefish. Handling and release practices matter because long fight times and air exposure can impose physiological stress. Historical claims that they routinely reach roughly 110 km/h remain common in popular sources but should not be propagated as measured fact.

### Fun facts
- High-speed video showed that sardines near an inserted sailfish bill did not respond earlier than fish farther away, supporting the bill's hydrodynamic stealth.
- The bill can be used for both gentle individual taps and violent lateral slashes.
- The famous ~110 km/h figure is contradicted by modern biomechanics; 8.3 ± 1.4 m/s was the 2016 theoretical muscle-based estimate.
- The first dorsal fin contains dozens of rays and can be folded down to reduce drag.
- NOAA researchers examined more than one thousand sailfish fin spines in a major age-and-growth study.

### Concise site summary
A fast, highly maneuverable pelagic predator, the sailfish uses a long hydrodynamically stealthy bill to enter prey schools and deliver lightning lateral slashes. Its giant dorsal sail aids control and can fold away for streamlined swimming. Modern biomechanics put its plausible maximum speed far below the famous 110 km/h myth, but its acceleration, precision and specialized weapon still make it an elite aquatic skirmisher.

### Narrative profile
The sailfish is less a straight-line speed caricature than a precision weapons platform. Its body is shaped for sustained pelagic movement, but the decisive adaptation sits ahead of the eyes: a long, slender rostrum that can be threaded into a dense school of fish without producing enough disturbance to trigger an immediate escape. Once positioned, the head whips laterally. High-speed field work showed that these slashes combine extraordinary angular motion with surprise, damaging or disorienting prey that can then be seized by the jaws.

Its enormous dorsal fin is similarly functional rather than ornamental. The sail can be deployed when control and prey manipulation matter, then depressed when reducing drag matters. Together with a forked tail and paired fins, this gives the fish excellent three-dimensional control in open water. The result is high Agility and Special, but not exaggerated Attack or Defense: a sailfish is still a lightly protected ~70 kg fish whose weapon evolved principally against much smaller prey.

The profile also corrects one of zoology's most persistent speed claims. Older rod-and-reel calculations helped popularize values around 110 km/h. Modern muscle-contraction and stride-length analysis estimated 8.3 ± 1.4 m/s and argued that extreme older values are biomechanically implausible. ABS therefore rewards verified maneuvering and strike mechanics rather than folklore velocity.

## 11. Confidence, conflicts and cross-animal normalization
- **Speed conflict:** popular 110 km/h claims are intentionally rejected in favor of Svendsen et al.'s 8.3 ± 1.4 m/s biomechanical estimate and ~7 m/s observed predation context.
- **Length conflict:** ADW's 130-162 cm range conflicts with fishery and museum sources reporting much larger adults. Canonical length uses FishBase's 270 cm common TL, not a record maximum.
- **Taxonomy conflict:** some references retain Atlantic *I. albicans* separately, while many modern treatments use one circumglobal *I. platypterus*. The roster's generic Sailfish is treated as *I. platypterus* pending any future taxonomic split policy.
- **Attack normalization:** 52.0 keeps this 70 kg specialist below Sable Antelope (62.0) and far below Reticulated Python (72.0), whose absolute killing mechanics against larger targets are stronger. The sailfish's bill advantage is reflected more strongly in Weaponry/Special than raw Attack.
- **Defense normalization:** 38.0 is above small unarmored animals but below large terrestrial tanks and heavily protected aquatic species; evasiveness is not double-counted as armor.
- **Agility normalization:** 89.0 is elite and comparable to other precision aerial/aquatic movers, but it comes from control and maneuverability, not the rejected folklore speed figure.
- **Stamina normalization:** 79.0 rewards highly migratory pelagic locomotion without equating migration with continuous sprinting.
- **Special normalization:** 82.0 is justified by a rare, experimentally documented stealth/slash weapon system plus deployable sail control; the same bill is not separately counted again as Raw Power.
