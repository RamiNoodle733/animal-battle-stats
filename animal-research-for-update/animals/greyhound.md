# Greyhound (*Canis familiaris*) research profile

## 1. Identity and canonical specimen

- **Common name:** Greyhound (English Greyhound)
- **Scientific name:** *Canis familiaris* Linnaeus, 1758 (domestic dog; Greyhound breed, FCI standard No. 158)
- **Exact animal represented:** the racing and coursing Greyhound as a sprint sighthound, including retired racers kept as pets, not the smaller Italian Greyhound or Whippet.
- **Living / extinct:** Living (domestic breed).
- **Canonical combat specimen:** healthy adult male, **32.0 kg**, **74.0 cm** at the withers.
- **Sex choice:** males are larger. A UK VetCompass study of 5,419 pet greyhounds found males averaged **32.3 kg** (SD 4.1) and females 27.2 kg. The FCI gives ideal heights of 71–76 cm for males and 68–71 cm for females.
- **Population notes:** racing greyhounds registered with coursing and racing authorities differ somewhat from show-bred Kennel Club greyhounds. Most pet greyhounds in the UK are retired racers. Wikipedia gives male weights of 27–40 kg.

## 2. Physical measurements

### Mass
- VetCompass UK (O'Neill et al. 2019): adult males mean **32.3 kg** (SD 4.1); all adults 29.7 kg.
- AKC (tertiary): males **65–70 lb (29.5–31.8 kg)**.
- Wikipedia (tertiary): males usually **27–40 kg**.
- **Canonical:** **32.0 kg**, the measured male mean.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Height
- FCI: males **71–76 cm**. AKC: males 28–30 in (71–76 cm).
- **Canonical:** **74.0 cm**.
- **Confidence:** High.

### Length
- The FCI standard describes the back as "rather long, broad and square" but gives no length-to-height ratio, and no measured body length was located.
- **Canonical `length_cm`: 76.0 cm**, a low-confidence estimate of trunk length slightly exceeding withers height, following the standard's "rather long" back. This is **not** head-body length.
- **Confidence:** Low.

### Speed
- Eager et al. (2021), tracking racing greyhounds with jacket-mounted transmitters on an Australian track: a typical greyhound's speed rises to a maximum **slightly below 19.5 m/s** between 80 m and 150 m into a race, then falls to about **16.1 m/s** by the finish of 525–600 m races and below **15.5 m/s** at 725 m. The same paper notes greyhounds can reach top speed in under 80 m and under 4 s, with average forward acceleration above **4.0 m/s²** about 2 s after the start.
- Wikipedia: peak racing speeds of at least **69 km/h (19.2 m/s)**; typical winning speeds about 58–61 km/h.
- **Canonical `speed_mps`: 19.0 m/s**, a conservative reading of the measured typical race maximum.
- **Confidence:** High; one of the best-measured running speeds of any domestic animal.

### Lifespan
- McMillan et al. (2024), as cited by Wikipedia: UK life expectancy **11.5 years**.
- AKC (tertiary): **10–13 years**.
- **Canonical `lifespan_years`: 11.5**.
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high.

### Bite force
- **Canonical `bite_force_psi`: 0.0.** No bite-force measurement for the breed was located.
- General dog data are forces, not pressures: Lindner et al. (1995) 13–1,394 N in pet dogs; Ellis et al. (2008) 147–3,417 N in anesthetized dogs.

### Muscle, blood and running build
- Williams et al. (2008): racing greyhounds have a relatively large volume of hip extensor muscle, some pelvic limb muscles relatively larger per unit body mass than in less specialized dogs, and longer fascicles in many hip extensors, allowing high power output and rapid acceleration.
- Williams et al. (2009): the largest increases in joint work during acceleration occur at the hip, consistent with greyhounds powering running by torque about the hip.
- Usherwood and Wilson (2005): on entering a tight bend greyhounds do not change their foot-contact timing and so withstand a **65% increase** in limb forces instead of slowing.
- Zaldívar-López et al. (2011): retired racing greyhounds have higher red blood cell mass than other breeds, along with other distinct blood values.
- Wikipedia summarizes: large heart, the highest share of Type IIa fast-twitch fibres of any breed, a double-suspension gallop and a very flexible spine.

### Coat and defensive structure
- Short, fine coat with no undercoat and little body fat (Wikipedia). In VetCompass UK records, wounds (6.2%) and claw injuries (4.2%) were among the most common disorders of pet greyhounds.

## 4. Combat biology

### Primary weapon and offensive mechanics
The Greyhound was bred to run down game by sight and catch it, historically hares in coursing. Its weapon is the bite delivered at the end of a sprint, from a long head on a long neck. It is built to catch and kill small, fast prey, not to wrestle large or armed animals.

### Defense and toughness
Its defense is speed. It has a lean body, a short coat with no undercoat and little fat, so bites and scrapes cut deep quickly, and wounds are among its most common veterinary problems. Its 32 kg mass gives some durability, but it is the most fragile dog in this batch.

### Locomotion and maneuverability
The Greyhound is a sprint specialist. It reaches top speed within about 80 m and 4 s, holds its stride through tight bends while its limbs take 65% more force, and follows smooth, efficient paths around turns. It can change heading quickly at speed, but it is a long-legged runner, not a dodging close-quarters fighter.

### Endurance
Race tracking shows speed falling from about 19.5 m/s to about 16 m/s over 500–600 m, the pattern of a sprinter. Its blood (high red cell mass) and aerobic enzyme activity comparable to human endurance runners mean it recovers and keeps running better than a cheetah, and the FCI credits it with "remarkable stamina", but it is not a wolf-like endurance hunter.

### Senses
A sighthound: it hunts by sight in open country. Normal canid smell and hearing.

### Intelligence and tactics
Coursing requires tracking and cutting off evasive prey at speed. Beyond that, its fighting tactics are limited. Wikipedia notes a strong prey drive and often little recall.

### Intraspecific fighting
Generally not dog-aggressive; racing greyhounds are commonly muzzled while racing. No quantitative data on fighting were located.

### Predator defense
Relies on flight.

### Aggression and ferocity
Usually gentle and placid with people, but with a strong chase-and-catch drive toward small running animals. It is not built or bred to press a fight against a dangerous opponent.

### Environmental strengths
- Open grassland and flat ground where it can reach full speed.
- Short chases after fleeing prey.

### Weaknesses
- Lean, lightly protected body with no undercoat.
- Speed drops off after a few hundred metres.
- Poor at close grappling; no heavy musculature in the forequarters or jaw built for fighting.
- Sensitive to heat and cold.
- Far outmatched by any serious fighter of its size or larger.

### Matchup archetypes
- **Good:** hares, rabbits, foxes and other small animals it can outrun and catch; opponents that try to flee.
- **Mixed:** coyotes and jackals, where catching is easy but finishing is less certain.
- **Bad:** wolves, guardian and bull breeds, big cats, bears, ungulates, armored or venomous opponents; anything that stands and fights.

## 7. Exactly two special abilities and two unique traits

### Special abilities
1. **Double-Suspension Sprint** — a gallop in which all four feet leave the ground twice per stride, powered by large hip-extensor muscles, taking it to about 19.5 m/s within 80–150 m. In a matchup it can close distance or escape faster than almost any other dog.
2. **Bend Runner** — keeps its foot-contact timing through tight bends and absorbs 65% higher limb forces rather than slowing, as Usherwood and Wilson showed. It turns at speed where other runners must brake.

### Unique traits
1. **Red-Cell Rich Athlete** — retired racing greyhounds have higher red blood cell mass than other breeds, part of a distinct blood profile that veterinarians must account for.
2. **Hip-Driven Sprinter Build** — a deep chest, long legs, very flexible spine and the highest share of fast oxidative muscle fibres of any breed, with running power coming mainly from torque about the hips.

## 8. Expanded profile

### Habitat and range
A domestic breed from Britain, now kept worldwide as a racing dog, coursing dog and pet, mostly in homes. It was historically used for hunting by sight in open country.

### Diet
Fed by its owners on commercial dog food, meat and household scraps.

### Ecology
No wild ecological role. Historically used to course hares and other game in open country; later used in organised coursing and track racing.

### Social structure
Usually gentle and quiet with people and often good with other dogs, though many have a strong urge to chase small animals and poor recall.

### Reproduction and life history
Breeding is managed by people, largely through racing and coursing studbooks. Litters of 1–12 pups are reported (Wikipedia). VetCompass UK found that most pet greyhounds are retired racers.

### Conservation
Not applicable as a wild species; it is a **Domesticated** breed.

### Special features
- Long legs, deep chest and a very flexible spine.
- Double-suspension rotary gallop.
- Large hip-extensor muscle mass.
- High red blood cell mass and distinct blood chemistry.
- Short coat with no undercoat and little body fat.

### Human interaction
Greyhound racing has brought welfare scrutiny, including research on track design to reduce injuries. Many retired racers are rehomed as pets, where they are known as calm companions. Some greyhounds sleep with their eyes partly open and can startle if woken suddenly.

### Genuine fun facts
- Racing greyhounds typically reach top speed, just under 19.5 m/s, within 80–150 m of the start.
- Greyhounds do not slow down for tight bends; their legs simply take about 65% more force.
- Greyhounds have more red blood cells than other dog breeds, which can confuse vets unfamiliar with the breed.
- The FCI breed standard claims that "only the cheetah tops the Greyhound for speed".
- Many greyhounds sleep with their eyes partly open.
- A UK study of 5,419 pet greyhounds found males averaged 32.3 kg and females 27.2 kg.

### Concise site-ready summary
The Greyhound is a 32 kg sprint sighthound that reaches about 19.5 m/s within 150 m and holds its speed through tight bends. It wins by catching or escaping, not by fighting: its lean, short-coated body is easily wounded and its speed fades over longer distances, so any opponent that stands its ground has the advantage.

### Rich narrative profile
The Greyhound was bred in Britain to hunt by sight, running down hares in open country, and its racing and coursing studbooks go back to the 18th century. A typical male weighs about 32 kg and stands about 74 cm, with long legs, a deep chest for a large heart and a spine that flexes and extends with every stride of its double-suspension gallop.

It is one of the best-measured sprinters in the animal world. Tracking studies show racing greyhounds reach just under 19.5 m/s within 80–150 m, and biomechanics research shows their power comes mainly from huge hip-extensor muscles. They do not slow down for tight bends; their legs simply absorb about 65% more force. Their blood carries more red cells than other breeds, helping supply oxygen during the race, but speed still falls to about 16 m/s by 500–600 m.

In a fight that speed is the Greyhound's only real weapon. It can catch and kill a hare, and it can escape from almost anything on open ground, but its lean body, thin coat and gentle temperament make it one of the weakest dogs on the roster in close combat. It rates high in Agility and Special, below the cheetah and pronghorn, and low in Attack and Defense.

## 11. Cross-animal normalization notes

- **Versus Cheetah (46 kg; A41 D24 Ag97 St43 I62 Sp76; speed 25.9 m/s):** the cheetah is much faster, far more maneuverable at speed and armed with claws, so it keeps higher Attack, Agility and Special. The Greyhound's measured speed fade is gentler, so it gets higher Stamina.
- **Versus Coyote (15 kg; A38 D25 Ag82 St74 I76):** the Greyhound is twice as heavy and measurably fast, so slightly higher Attack; the coyote is more evasive in close quarters, far better at sustained travel and much more tactically flexible.
- **Versus Dingo (18 kg; A40 D24 Ag82 St81 I75):** similar Attack and Defense despite the Greyhound's extra mass, because the dingo is a committed wild predator; the dingo outscores it in Stamina and Intelligence.
- **Versus Gray Wolf (50 kg; A55 D41 Ag66 St89 I78):** the wolf is far stronger, tougher and more enduring; the Greyhound is faster and rates higher only in Agility and Special.
- **Versus Belgian Malinois (30 kg; A46 D32 Ag76 St75):** similar mass, but the Malinois is built and bred to fight; the Greyhound is faster and rated higher in Agility and Special only.
- **Anti-inflation check:** top speed is scored in the Speed substat and Special; Agility is kept below the cheetah and close to agile wild canids, not driven by speed alone.
